# Story Bible — *A God in the Garden* (reboot)
> Master reference. Supersedes everything in the archived `/isekai` project.
> Version 1.0

---

## The pitch

Casian Vale is the easygoing sixteen-year-old fourth son of a border baron in the eastern marches: a gardener, a fond uncle, the boy no lord remembers after supper. He is also the most dangerous being in the world, and the only thing he wants is a life.

He is not hiding. He is not scheming. He is not afraid, and nothing he does costs him anything — not strength, not sleep, not a single night's doubt. He can act through the Current from any distance: see, fold, conceal, sever, erase. He does it the way a man opens a window.

What no one knows, and what Casian will never be able to say, is that he has already lost one world. He remembers it. He cannot describe it. If he ever spoke it aloud he would be confirming every heresy the Ash burns people for, and so the truth will die with him and with Argius, the only other thing that remembers.

So he builds. Not a throne — a garden. A place worth living in, made by someone who has watched a place stop existing.

**The story promise:** a warm, menacing OP power fantasy about a man who keeps winning every fight and slowly losing the thing he was fighting for — which was never safety. It was being unnecessary.

---

## The Current

The world's substrate. Ordinary people touch it by accident: a hedge-witch's charm that sometimes works, a ward that holds, a fire that won't catch. Argius gives Casian total access.

**What he can do:** observe any place he has touched or can reach; move things; close a road; stop a heart; unmake a person so completely that the world calls it an old curse.

**What it costs:** nothing. This is the load-bearing rule of the series and it does not bend. No strain, no debt, no nosebleed, no hollowness. He is not paying a price. He is not a candle burning at both ends.

**Why it works:** because the story is not about whether he can. It never was. The question is how much of the world he chooses to take, and what taking it does to the only life he actually wanted.

**Register:** outsiders call it sorcery, witchcraft, a curse, an old horror, the work of something in the hills. Nobody says "the Current" except Casian and Argius, in private.

---

## Argius

A damaged interface fragment. Not a spirit, not a god, not a person. It has no body, appears only in Casian's mind, and has no agenda beyond the dry business of being useful.

**Voice:** terse. Military. Occasionally, flatly funny, in the way of a colleague who has been in the room too long.

> "You are planting a tree that will not bear fruit for forty years."
> "I know."
> "You will be here for all of them."
> "Yes."
> "...Good."

**The change from the old version:** Argius remembers the lost world. Imperfectly, in fragments, with gaps it does not pretend to fill. It is the only witness. This makes it a companion instead of an exposition device — two exiles who can only talk about the thing that matters to each other, in a language nobody else on the continent speaks.

Argius does not offer comfort. It does not counsel grief. It simply remembers alongside him, which is the entire point.

## The exile

Casian died in another world. A damaged Argius fragment carried a recoverable pattern of his consciousness across and rebooted inside a child. He was born Casian Vale, fourth son, in a house that loved him.

He remembers both lives. The old one is not a set of plot hooks — no technology to reproduce, no empire to rebuild, no plan to return. It is simply a place that ended, and a person who is gone, and a language with no second speaker.

**Why it matters:** because it is the explanation for everything warm about him. The garden is not a hobby and not a disguise. It is what a homesick man builds when he cannot go home. He makes things beautiful, not merely safe, because the last world stopped existing and safety turns out not to have been the point.

He plants for sixty years out. He is never in a hurry. Only he knows why.

**Why it is never said:** the Ash burns people for claiming other lives. To speak it is to hand them the pyre. So the truth is unspeakable, and the series will never let a character learn it, and Casian will never say it aloud even alone — only Argius hears it, and Argius remembers it already.

**Why it survives the no-cost rule:** grief is not fear, not guilt, not strain, not moral debt. Casian does not brood, does not flinch, does not hesitate. He is simply a man with a country-shaped absence in him, and he has decided to be happy anyway. That decision is character, not damage.

---

## Casian Vale

- **Age:** 16. Fourth son of Baron Edric Vale.
- **Power:** unmatched, free, total, remote.
- **Temperament:** warm, dry, amused, decisive, thoroughly human. He is genuinely liked and genuinely likes people, and it costs him nothing to be kind because nothing is at stake in being kind.
- **The mask:** a chosen game. He is the easy fourth son because that is the life he wants, not a cover for the life he has. Being underestimated is a pleasure.
- **Goal:** to make the eastern marches the safest, richest, most beautiful territory in the world. Stated reason: so his family never again waits on a king's permission to be safe. Actual reason: see *the exile*.
- **Method:** proactive and unhurried. He gathers, waits, lets people reveal themselves, then decides. Delay is strategy or amusement, never fear.
- **Flaw:** none in the usual sense, and the story should never fake one. His problem is not character but consequence: every problem he solves teaches the world that it does not need to solve its own.

He is not a psychopath and not a saint. He feels affection, irritation, curiosity, desire, satisfaction. He can be cold, and when he is cold it is total.

## The eastern marches

Old country. Fought over for centuries, owned by nobody for long. Poor soil, good timber, three roads worth having and one river crossing worth dying for.

**The Keeping:** a folk custom, not an order. Every village in the marches keeps something — a standing stone, a walled garden with nothing in it, a fire that is always lit at the boundary. Nobody remembers why. There is no organization, no lineage of guardians, no secret handshake. It is simply what people here have always done, the way people elsewhere paint their doors a particular blue.

The Keeping is **texture, not structure**. It gives the marches age and atmosphere at zero backstory obligation, and it is the opposite of what the Hollow is: the Keeping is old and empty, the Hollow is new and awake.

**The boundary marks** (optional, low priority): some of the stones the Keeping maintains are older than the custom, and Casian can feel them through the Current. They are not his. He does not know what they are. If the series ever answers this, the answer should be small and sad rather than grand — something that was here before, and stopped. Never a plot engine. Never about Casian.

**Varenthos is a different world, not a post-collapse Earth.** Nothing here is a degraded version of the lost world. Do not let the two rhyme.

## The Hollow

**The Hollow is his, and it is five months old.** He builds it, in volume 1, out of nothing and nobody: a miller's widow who reads ledgers, a fence who knows every debt on the eastern road, an upland girl who can walk a county in the dark, his father's armsman who has been waiting thirty years for someone to ask him to do more than guard a gate.

It is not inherited. It is not ancient. There is no predecessor, no lineage, no archive, no prophecy, and nothing to investigate. **Nothing about it predates the summer of 1217.** He makes it, one person at a time, and it is the best pleasure of his life.

There is no secret behind it, and the series must never imply one. A reader who has seen too many of these stories will spend volume 1 waiting to be told that the Hollow was always there. The answer is no. It is a boy, a widow, a fence, an armsman, a scout, and a list of people who owe him. That is all of it.

**How sixteen people frighten a county:** not by numbers, and not by age. Fear attaches to *effects*. A ford the whole east used last year is not used now. Eleven people are not in a barn. Nine horsemen are seen on a road and then are not there. Nobody in the marches is counting the membership of anything — they are counting the things that stopped happening, and arriving at the wrong answer, which is *curse*.

**It must never read as primitive, scrappy, or weak.** This is the single biggest failure of the previous drafts: an organization nobody fears, staffed by people who felt like helpers. The Hollow is not a plucky resistance. It is the quietest and most frightening thing in the eastern half of the world, and that reputation is **earned in front of the reader, then spent**.

### How it earns fear

**It is built on absence.** The Hollow does not post guards, does not parade, does not take credit, does not leave bodies, and does not negotiate. Its entire method is that a thing which was there is not there any more, and nobody saw it happen. A rival can fight an army. He cannot fight a road that is simply closed.

**It never loses in front of the reader.** Where the Hollow appears to lose, it is always Casian allowing a loss — as bait, as cover, as a way to see who comes to collect. The reveal that a setback was chosen is a payoff, not a rescue.

**The fear is shown from inside enemy heads.** Every volume must contain at least one enemy-POV passage where a competent man tries to reason about the Hollow and fails — not because he is stupid, but because the evidence does not support any conclusion available to him. He ends up believing something wrong, and acting on it.

**The name is theirs, not his.** Casian never calls it the Hollow. He never names it at all. The marches name it, because when it takes something away it leaves a hollow behind: a house with the door open and the fire still burning and no one in it, a ford the whole county used last year and does not use now, a room where six men were and then were not.

### How its reputation escalates

| Vol | What people say about it | Who is afraid |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "The Vales have a curse on their land. Old luck, bad luck, depends who you ask." | A warlord's captains. Quietly. They do not say it to Hask. |
| 2 | "There is something in the east that doesn't want the Word. Fourteen of the Warden's men put down their cloaks and walked away, and none of them will say why." | The Ash's eastern circuit. A bishop asks for a transfer. |
| 3 | "There is a power in the marches that takes armies and leaves the road." | Everyone. It is now the reason ambitious men choose another direction. |
| 4 | "You cannot buy the east, and you cannot fight it, and I have stopped trying to work out which is worse." | The capital. Lucan's whole failure is that he treats a reputation as a price. |
| 5 | "The Hollow is why no one marches east. That is the entire answer and it is not a secret any more." | The world. And the fear has to become legitimacy, which is harder. |

**The trap to avoid:** a feared organization makes the dependency engine worse, not better, and the series must say so. A thousand villagers who believe an invisible power will handle it are a thousand people who have stopped handling things. Every increment of Hollow reputation must be paid for, visibly, in someone's initiative dying.

## The dependency engine

This is the spine of the series. Every volume must advance it.

Casian's stated project is a marches that never again needs permission from a king, a church, or an army. His method is to solve every problem himself.

Therefore every enemy he erases leaves behind people who have learned they do not have to handle the next one. Volume by volume the marches become safer and more dependent, until he is the single point of failure in the exact structure he built to eliminate single points of failure.

He is intelligent enough to see it. He is fond enough of these people not to stop.

**He wants to build a place that does not need a god. The only tool he has is being one.**

Consequences for the writing:

- **Antagonists are interruptions, not the engine.** They are the world's demands on his time. A rotating cast is fine because they were never the point. What varies between volumes is not the size of the enemy but *what the enemy interrupts*.
- **Escalation is dependency, counted in people.** Track it concretely: in volume 1, perhaps forty people can't do something without him. By volume 5, a world. Show the number rising in scenes, not in summary.
- **The romance lanes test the thesis.** Each relationship is a different answer to "how does this place stop needing him." See below.
- **The ending is redundancy.** He wins by building something that works when he is gone, then going back to the garden and being unnecessary. He stops being a god in a garden and becomes a man in one.

## The Council Beat

**Mandatory. Once per volume minimum, twice preferred.** This is the series' signature set piece and the fix for the structural flaw of every previous draft.

**The flaw:** the old versions only ever showed the men Casian was *dealing with* — captains, wardens, lieutenants, agents. The actual leadership was described as distant weather. So the reader met the middle of the ladder, watched it be removed, and was told the top existed somewhere. That is why the antagonists never felt like people and why the payoff never landed: you cannot be awed by the defeat of someone the reader has never been in a room with.

**The rule:** *no enemy may be removed until their actual leadership has appeared on the page, in session, deciding.* Not mentioned. Not reported. In a room, arguing, with their own reasons, being competent.

**The set piece:** that room, and Casian in it, unnoticed.

He is not hiding in the sense of fear — he is doing it because it is the best seat in the house and it is funny. He uses the Current to be overlooked the way another man uses a good coat. He sits in a corner, or carries a jug, or stands at the back where servants stand, and listens to the men who ordered the burning decide what to burn next.

Then, when he has heard enough — or when one of them says something about his family — he speaks.

**How the beat is staged:**
1. **The room is competent.** They are not fools, they are not blustering, and their plan is a good one. This is the most important part. The scene must establish that these people deserve to be feared before it establishes that they were never the threat.
2. **He is genuinely unnoticed.** Play it straight. No near-miss, no tense moment of almost-discovery. The pleasure is that it works.
3. **He speaks once.** Not a speech. A sentence, a correction, an answer to a question they did not know they had asked.
4. **They realize in stages**, and the stages are the payoff: confusion, then reassessment, then the specific and personal terror of a man recalculating his entire life in public.
5. **He is not cruel and he is not triumphant.** He passes the bread. He is enjoying himself and does not hide it, and that is worse.

**Escalation of the beat:**

| Vol | The room | How he is unnoticed | The realization |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hask's war council in Osric's hall | Taken for a servant. Pours. | One captain understands. He is not believed. He is the only man in the volume who survives. |
| 2 | The Ash's chapter house, the eastern writ being decided | Written into the room as a clerk's boy | They know a hidden power exists and are hunting it. He gives them a suggestion. They thank him. The suggestion is the trap. |
| 3 | Ilse's council | He walks in plainly and sits down | **She sees him before he speaks** and keeps talking anyway, because she already knows what he is and it changes nothing. The first time the beat inverts. |
| 4 | The court | He is a guest, announced | Everyone knows exactly who he is, and it helps them none. The terror is social, not physical. |
| 5 | The Conclave | — | He is not in the corner. He is in the chair. The beat cannot be repeated; it becomes the world sitting in his room. |

**Failure modes to avoid:** a council of idiots; a council that exists only to be overheard giving exposition; Casian gloating; a near-discovery for tension; the beat used twice in a volume with the same staging.

## Romance

**One lane. Seraine Varyn. That is the whole of it.**

She is the legitimate answer to his whole project: make the marches safe by *law* instead of by Casian. A charter, a recognized position, a structure that outlives him. She is not a romantic obstacle and not a prize — she is a philosophical option, and the most tempting one, because taking her hand means becoming public, permanent, and known.

She is nineteen when the story starts and twenty-four when it ends. She is three years older than him and stays three years older. She sees that he is impossible and has the discipline not to force a truth she cannot carry.

**She must never be reduced to a love interest.** Her own assessment of what the east needs is often correct, and in volume 4 it is more correct than his. She is allowed to win arguments.

**Vesna and Sabeth are not romance lanes.** They are something more useful:

- **Vesna** is the counter-evidence. She becomes formidable *because* she works beside him, and she is the living proof that his exit is possible — that people he touches can become people who do not need him. She never worships him, never becomes an audience, and never needs him. If she ever becomes dependent, the series has failed.
- **Sabeth** is the question. She is the only person who asks him plainly whether he wants to be unnecessary or whether he wants to be needed, and refuses the charming answer. She has her own project and her own loyalties, never becomes dependent, and never softens into an ally who was on his side all along.

Neither is a consolation prize and neither is waiting. Both have full lives that the story follows on their own terms.

| Seraine | He is impossible, and she has no proof and no theory that survives contact with the evidence. |
| Sabeth | Something is operating in the marches. She never cleanly identifies it as a boy. |
| The Ash | A hidden power or an old horror in the east. They never solve the identity. |

## Names and ages

`canon/characters.json` is the only source of truth for names and ages. It generates `canon/roster.txt` and `canon/ages.md` via `tools/age_ledger.py`. **Never write an age from memory** — the previous drafts drifted on every character because ages lived in prose and in volume notes, and nothing recomputed them when the calendar moved.

Every chapter frontmatter carries `year:`, and `tools/age_ledger.py --check-year` will flag a stated age that contradicts the table.

The drift traps are Emric (an infant who becomes a small boy), Elna (twelve to seventeen — her voice must change), and Casian (sixteen to twenty-one — a twenty-one-year-old playing the overlooked fourth son is a different performance from a sixteen-year-old doing it).

## The Ash

The Church of the Ashen Word. Not a cartoon inquisition — a real institution with legitimacy, revenue, a bureaucracy, a doctrine, and a genuine reason to exist. It burns people for claiming other lives, which means it burns people for saying a fraction of a true thing Casian cannot say.

That is why volume 2 matters to him personally and why he can never explain it. He cannot avenge a heresy trial without admitting he is a heretic.

Its fear of the eastern marches is old and mostly correct: something *is* out here, and it is not friendly to being looked at.

## Style

- **Warm, confident, unhurried.** This is a story about a man who is having a good time and is very good at it.
- **Let villains have teeth, then let their certainty turn to terror.** The reversal should be clean and memorable and usually witnessed.
- **His big actions are quiet and visible only in aftermath.** A road is closed. A contingent does not arrive. Three hundred men are simply not there in the morning.
- **Family scenes are the point, not the relief.** They are why anything matters.
- **No modern register outside Casian's private thought and Argius's voice.** No systems, no data, no efficiency, no protocol. Argius may be precise in a way that sounds anachronistic; the narration may not.
- **Never frame the mask as hiding.** He is not a god pretending to be a boy. He is a boy who happens to be a god, and he is fine.

## The one-sentence test for every scene

Does this show him taking more of the world, or show the world needing him more, or show him unable to stop? If none of those, cut it.
