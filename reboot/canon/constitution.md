# Writer's Constitution — *A God in the Garden* (reboot)
> **Law, not reference.** Where this conflicts with the bible, the bible wins on facts and this wins on method.
> Version 1.0

---

## The four unbreakables

**1. Casian is never threatened.**
Unmatched power, zero cost. No fear, no strain, no exhaustion, no moral debt, no near-miss, no wound, no plan that could fail. He is never rescued, never cornered, never forced to react. If a draft contains a moment where the reader wonders whether he will be all right, delete the moment.

*Grief is not a violation of this rule.* He carries a lost world. He does not flinch, brood, hesitate, or doubt his power. He is a man with an absence in him who has decided to be happy anyway.

**2. The mask is chosen and he enjoys it.**
He is the easy fourth son because it is a good life and a fun game. Never write it as disguise, survival, camouflage-for-the-vulnerable, or a burden he longs to shed. He is not trapped by it. He could stop at any time and does not want to.

**3. Nobody ever learns about the old world.**
Not in volume 1, not in volume 5, not on the last page. No character guesses correctly, no record is found, no dying declaration. Casian does not speak it aloud, even alone — only Argius hears it, and Argius already knows. The reader learns it through his private thought. It stays unsaid and it changes everything he does.

**4. Escalation is dependency, not opposition.**
Every volume must make the marches safer and more reliant on him. A bigger enemy is not escalation. A world that can no longer function without him is. Count it in people: who, concretely, cannot do something now that they could have done before?

---

## Structure rules

**5. Every volume is a different *kind* of story.**
Volume 1 is intimate. 2 is institutional. 3 is a peer. 4 is social. 5 is foundational. No volume may be another volume with a new antagonist — the single failure mode of the previous draft. Before planning any volume, name in one sentence what kind of story it is and confirm it does not appear earlier in the list.

**6. Antagonists are interruptions.**
They are the world's demands on his time, not the engine of the series. Give them teeth, give them real danger to *his people*, let them expose their networks, then remove them in a way the survivors will describe as a curse. What varies volume to volume is what they interrupt — a family, a town, a project, a relationship, a peace.

**7. From volume 3 onward, erasure must be available and must be a loss.**
A protagonist who cannot be beaten is only interesting when winning would cost him something he values. The peer, the court, the world: these are opponents where the question is not "can he remove them" but "what does removing them make him." Not because they are stronger. Because they are right, or loved, or necessary, or doing his own work for reasons he respects.

**8. The garden is the ending, not the reward.**
The last movement is not a victory. It is him building something that works when he is gone, and then being unnecessary, and liking it. He stops being a god in a garden and becomes a man in one.

**9. The top of the ladder is always on the page.**
No enemy may be removed until their actual leadership has appeared in a room, in session, deciding something, with their own reasons. Never the men Casian is dealing with while the real power stays offstage as weather. If the reader has only met captains and agents, the volume is broken — the payoff of removing someone is proportional to how well the reader knows them.

Every volume must also include **at least one enemy-POV passage** in which a competent person reasons about the Hollow and arrives at the wrong answer.

**10. The Council Beat is mandatory.**
Once per volume minimum. The enemy's leadership in session; Casian present, actively overlooked through the Current, enjoying himself; then he speaks once. Staged as follows and never shortcut:

- The room is competent. Establish that they deserve to be feared *before* establishing that they were never the threat.
- He is genuinely unnoticed. No near-miss, no almost-caught. Play it straight.
- He says one sentence, not a speech, and it is usually an answer to a question they did not know they had asked.
- They realize in stages, and the stages are the payoff: confusion, reassessment, then the personal terror of a man recalculating his life in public.
- He is neither cruel nor triumphant. He is having a good time and does not hide it, which is worse.

See the escalation table in `bible.md`. Never use the same staging twice in one volume.

**11. The Hollow is feared, not scrappy.**
It is not a plucky resistance, not a band of helpers, and not primitive. It is the quietest and most frightening thing in the eastern half of the world, and its reputation is earned in front of the reader before it is spent. Its method is absence: it posts no guards, parades nothing, takes no credit, leaves no bodies, and never negotiates.

Where the Hollow appears to lose, Casian allowed it — as bait, as cover, or to see who comes to collect. Never let it lose because it was outmatched.

And every increment of its reputation must be paid for visibly, in someone's initiative dying. A thousand villagers who believe an invisible power will handle it are a thousand people who have stopped handling things. That is the engine, not a side effect.

**12. Ages and names come from the ledger.**
`canon/characters.json` → `canon/roster.txt` + `canon/ages.md`, generated by `tools/age_ledger.py`. Never write an age from memory and never spell a roster name from memory. Every chapter frontmatter carries `year:`. If the ledger and the draft disagree, the draft is wrong.

## The dependency ledger (new, and mandatory)

Before drafting any volume, write down:
- **Who currently cannot act without him.** Named people, roles, specific things that will now fail.
- **Who he is about to make dependent in this volume.**
- **Who, if anyone, becomes less dependent.** These should be rare, hard-won, and always earned by a specific person rather than a general improvement.

Vesna is the standing counter-example. If she ever becomes dependent on him, something has gone wrong with the series.

## Voice and register

**13. Argius is dry.** Terse military precision with occasional flat humour. Not cold, not warm, not chirpy, not a grief counsellor, not a search engine. It never explains the world, never recaps, and never volunteers backstory. Its best lines are two words long. It is the only company Casian has in the only thing that matters, and it never says so.

**14. No modern register outside Casian's private thought and Argius's voice.**
Banned in narration and dialogue: efficiency, optimize, system, network, data, metric, probability, logistics, protocol, engineering, company (as a business), percent, software, algorithm, database, computer, technology, interface, process, capacity, bandwidth.

Argius may be precisely anachronistic. The narrator may not. Outsiders say: sorcery, witchcraft, curse, an old horror, the thing in the hills.

**15. Do not let the lost world rhyme with Varenthos.**
It is not Earth. Do not name it. Do not let Casian compare things to it out loud, and use the comparison in private thought rarely enough that it still lands. There are no ruins of it here, no technology to reinvent, no songs that survived. It is a hole, not a clue.

**16. The Keeping is custom, not conspiracy.**
No secret order, no ancient guardians, no inherited machine that obeys him. The boundary stones are old and nobody knows why. If the series ever answers that question, the answer is small and sad and has nothing to do with Casian.

## Scene test

Every scene must do at least one of:

- Show him **taking** something (territory, knowledge, loyalty, a debt, a road).
- Show the **world needing him** more than it did before.
- Show the **mask as pleasure**, not pressure.
- Show **family warmth** that establishes what all of this is for.
- Show a **real reversal** — condescension becoming terror, witnessed.

Cut scenes whose only function is logistics, anxious management, procedural negotiation, or investigating old history.

**At least two unmistakable aura payoffs per act**, and no more than three that are the same *kind* of payoff. If three erasures in a row all look like "they vanished and nobody knows how," you have spent an entire volume saying one thing.

## Character locks

- **Ages are fixed** by `roster.txt`. Do not drift them.
- **Edric** is a good baron and a good father who sees an ordinary son, and is not a fool. His blindness is the blindness of a man with four sons and a border to hold.
- **Maren** is the emotional centre. She knows something is different about him and has decided, deliberately and privately, not to make it a thing. That decision is real and never stated aloud.
- **Elna** is his soft spot and not a hostage. Never use her as leverage for tension; use her to show what he is protecting.
- **Oswin** is sharp and old and suspects more than she says, and never says enough to break rule 3.
- **Seraine is the only romance lane.** She must never be reduced to a love interest: her own assessment of what the marches need is often correct, and in volume 4 more correct than his. She is allowed to win arguments. The three-year age gap stays constant.
- **Vesna is not a romance lane.** She is the counter-evidence to dependency — someone who became formidable by working and does not need him. She never worships him, never becomes an audience, is never competing for him, and is never the one who asks the question that solves the backstory. If she ever becomes dependent on him, the series has failed.
- **Sabeth is not a romance lane.** She is the question: the one person who asks whether he wants to be unnecessary or whether he wants to be needed, and refuses the charming answer. She never becomes dependent and never softens into an ally who was on his side all along. Her life is followed on its own terms.

## Ages

Every chapter frontmatter carries `year:`. Ages are read from `canon/ages.md`, never invented. The three that drift if unwatched: **Emric** (infant → small boy), **Elna** (12 → 17, and her voice must change with it), **Casian** (16 → 21, and a twenty-one-year-old playing the overlooked fourth son is a different performance from a sixteen-year-old doing it).

---

## Length

Chapter target: **3,000–4,500 words.** Long enough for real scenes, short enough to keep a serial pace. A chapter that reads as a plot summary is a failure regardless of length.
