# Series Arc — *A God in the Garden* (reboot)
> Five volumes. Each a different *kind* of story. Each escalating dependency, not opposition.

---

## The shape of the whole thing

Casian Vale is sixteen, the fourth son, and the most dangerous thing alive. He has lost a world he cannot name and is building a new one in the eastern marches — a place worth living in, made by someone who watched a place stop existing.

The series is not about whether he wins. He wins every time. The series is about what winning does to the only life he actually wanted.

**Act shape across five volumes:**

- **I–II:** He builds. The world pushes back. He removes the obstruction. It works.
- **III:** He meets someone whose project he cannot simply remove, and the first real argument of the series happens — with himself.
- **IV:** The world offers him the clean answer: become official, become permanent, stop being needed *personally* by becoming needed *structurally*. It is the correct answer, and it is the one thing he does not want.
- **V:** He solves dependency the only way it can be solved — by making himself unnecessary — and then goes back to the garden.

## Volume 1 — *The Gardener*

**Kind of story:** intimate. A family, a border, a cruel man.
**Interrupts:** ordinary life.

A warlord named Hask Veyr and the baron who funds him look at the eastern marches and see neglected land and a sentimental house they can squeeze. They are wrong, and Casian lets them be wrong for a long time, because letting a man show you every friend he has is faster than asking.

**What he builds:** his own network, from nothing. A miller, a fence, a clerk, a girl from the uplands who turns out to be better at this than he is. No inherited order, no ancient machine. By the end of the volume there are perhaps forty people in the marches whose problems are now his, and he has arranged it himself, deliberately, one at a time, and enjoyed it.

**What he gives up:** nothing he can name yet. He thinks he is simply solving a problem.

**The Council Beat (mid-volume):** Hask's war council in Osric's hall. Casian is taken for a servant and pours wine for the men who ordered the burning while they decide what to burn next. One captain understands what has been in the room. He is not believed, and he is the only man in the volume who survives.

**The Hollow:** built here, from nothing, one person at a time — Maeve, Ansel, Vesna, Corin. Small, quiet, and already frightening, because it does not leave anything behind. The marches start calling it a curse before it has a name.

**Ending:** Hask and the baron are gone so thoroughly that the border calls it an old curse. The marches are safer than they have been in a century, and can no longer solve their own disputes, and Casian does not notice that the second sentence follows from the first.

**Exile layer:** he plants a tree that will not fruit for forty years, and Argius remarks that he will be here for all of them. It is the first sign of a man building for a permanence he does not expect to share.

## Volume 2 — *The Ash*

**Kind of story:** institutional. A legitimate power with a bureaucracy and a doctrine.
**Interrupts:** the peace he just made.

The Church of the Ashen Word comes east. Not a mob — an apparatus. A writ, a Warden, a bureaucracy, a budget, and a sincerely held belief that the marches are hiding an old horror, which is unfortunately true.

They burn people for claiming to have lived another life. Casian watches a hedge-healer die for a fraction of a true thing he can never say.

**The problem he cannot punch:** he can erase a hundred men and the institution continues, because it is not a man. And he cannot avenge a heresy trial without admitting he is a heretic.

**How he wins:** he does not defeat the Church. He makes it *cheap* to leave the marches alone — by finding what its eastern apparatus actually is (corrupt, funded, ambitious, and lying to its own Synod) and handing that to the people who would rather not know. The Church retreats not because it is beaten but because the marches become an unprofitable embarrassment.

**Dependency rise:** every community he protects from the Ash now expects to be protected. He does not create a following. He creates a *constituency*.

**What he gives up:** the possibility of ever being honestly known. If he had wanted to tell anyone, that door closes here.

**The Council Beat:** the chapter house where the eastern writ is decided. Casian is written into the room as a clerk's boy. They know a hidden power exists and are hunting it. He offers a suggestion. They thank him for it. The suggestion is the trap.

**The Hollow:** the Ash's eastern circuit starts losing men who simply put down their cloaks and walk away, and none of them will say why. A bishop asks for a transfer. This is more frightening to the institution than any defeat in the field.

**Ending:** the Ash marks the east forbidden ground and teaches a wrong story about why. The world's fear of the marches is now official policy, and it is Casian's own fault, and he finds this funny in a way that does not reach his eyes.

## Volume 3 — *The Peer*

**Kind of story:** a rival. Somebody else with power, and a reason.
**Interrupts:** his certainty that he is the only one doing this.

A woman named Ilse has forced the Current. Not inherited it, not been given it — forced it, the way you force a lock, and it is eating her alive. She knows. She has perhaps two years and she is spending them on a project: a road, a league, a small nation of people who will be safe from the kind of men who took her family.

Her methods are monstrous. Her goal is Casian's. She is not lying about any of it.

**The Council Beat, inverted:** Ilse's council. He walks in plainly and sits down. **She sees him before he speaks** and keeps talking anyway, because she already knows what he is and it changes nothing about what she intends to do. The first time the beat fails to work, and the failure is the point.

**Why she is the hinge of the series:** she is the mirror. Her power costs everything and she pays it without complaint; his costs nothing and he has never once had to decide what it is for. She is going to die for something he does on a Tuesday afternoon.

**And he can erase her.** Easily. That is the point. Doing it would prove her right about what he has become and would be the first real defeat of the series — a victory that costs him something he cannot get back.

**How it resolves:** he does not remove her. He does something worse and kinder: he takes her project away from her by completing it properly, publicly, under his own name bar nobody can trace — and lets her die believing it worked. Or: she makes him choose, and one of them has to lose the thing they wanted. *(Decision deferred to volume 3 planning. Two defensible endings; pick one and commit.)*

**Dependency rise:** the marches now hold a league, a road, and a treasury. Two hundred people, then a thousand, cannot act without him.

**What he gives up:** the belief that his power is free because it costs *him* nothing.

## Volume 4 — *The Court*

**Kind of story:** social. Wit, caper, masks, and an offer he cannot refuse cleanly.
**Interrupts:** nothing. **He chooses this.** That is the point.

Seraine has spent three volumes telling him the same thing: make it law. A royal charter would make the marches safe by statute instead of by Casian, and statutes outlive men. For the first time, the correct answer and the thing he wants are different, and he goes to the capital because it sounds like fun and because he is afraid of the answer.

The capital expects a border baron and an irrelevant fourth son and gets a boy who plays a season of gossip, forgery, and ambition like a card game he has already won.

**The Council Beat, social:** the room where Lucan's faction decides how to break the Vales. Casian is a guest, announced at the door, and everyone knows exactly who he is — and it helps them none at all. The terror is not physical. It is a room full of clever people realising that knowing the truth about him is worthless.

**How he wins:** not by erasure. Duke Lucan is disgraced and contained and lives, and his failure is not defeat but incomprehension — he is a man who believes every house has a price, and he dies still unable to work out what Casian's was.

**Dependency rise:** the marches become a chartered entity whose charter exists because Casian was amusing in a ballroom. A charter without him is a nice document. The east knows the difference.

**What he gives up:** he accepts the charter. He also discovers he loves the game. He has taken the legitimate answer and made himself *more* central rather than less, and Seraine notices, and says so, and is right.

## Volume 5 — *The Gate*

**Kind of story:** foundational. He sets the stage and the world answers.
**Interrupts:** nothing external. He calls it.

The Compact is large enough that powers who have never met him now have opinions about him. Casian does what he always does: he makes the choice before anyone can make it for him. He invites the world to Vale — not to be recognised, but to see, so that when the story is told it is not a story about a hidden horror in the hills.

A betrayal makes the final movement necessary rather than chosen, but by then the board is his.

**The Council Beat:** there isn't one. He is not in the corner any more. He is in the chair, and the beat becomes the world sitting in his room. Do not stage it again.

**How it resolves:** the last enemy is not a person. It is the dependency he built. He spends the final movement deliberately making himself unnecessary — teaching, handing over, building structures with names on them that are not his, and accepting that this means letting people fail at things he could fix in a second. That is the hardest thing he does in five volumes and it costs him no strength at all.

**Ending:** the world no longer needs a god. He goes home. The last chapter is a morning in the garden with roses and noise and an open gate, and he is not watching the road. He is a man in a garden. That is the whole title, resolved.

**Romance endgame:** decided at volume 5 planning, not before. All three lanes must resolve on their own terms — including any that do not resolve with him.

## Standing rules across the arc

- Casian is never threatened, never strained, never afraid.
- Nobody learns the old world, at any point, ever.
- Every volume makes the east safer and more dependent.
- Every volume is a different kind of story.
- Antagonists are interruptions. What they interrupt changes; the fact of interruption does not.
- Volume 3 onward: erasure is available and would be a loss.
- Argius remembers. Only Argius remembers.
- Family is the reason, not the stakes.
- The top of every enemy ladder appears on the page, in a room, deciding, before they are removed.
- The Council Beat runs once per volume and never twice with the same staging.
- The Hollow is feared from volume 1 and its reputation escalates; every increment is paid for in someone's initiative dying.
- The end is a garden, and it is not a reward.
