The briars along the eastern terrace were failing, and Casian Vale had no intention of arguing with his mother about why.

To anyone leaning on the ash fence, the reason looked plain enough: three weeks of cold April rain followed by forty days of dust, leaving the bushes spent on pale leaf instead of flower. An ordinary gardener would have blamed the drought and waited for spring. Casian had listened to his mother say as much over sunrise porridge. She had said the roses had their own stubborn blood, set a second bowl of cream beside his bread, and bustled off to the dairy.

Casian had smiled, thanked her, and kept his second opinion to himself.

*Root-scorch,* he thought, testing an upright cane between thumb and forefinger. *The runoff from the old pig-pen has soured the gravel beneath the terrace. It has nothing to do with the drought.*

"Speak with your tongue," Argius murmured in the dark behind his ears. "Or do not speak."

*I was pruning.*

"You were shaping clauses. It is an inefficient habit you indulge whenever a minor calculation proves correct."

Casian drew the small curved hook along the briar, notched out a knuckle of blackened pith, and let the dead wood drop into the willow basket at his boots.

He was sixteen years of age. Across the eastern marches, from the wharves at Harrowmere to the beacons at High Tor, anyone asked to list the sons of Baron Edric Vale would have named three without drawing breath. Aldric was the heir: grave, thick-waisted, and already burdened by estate ledgers. Gareth was the sword: a lieutenant of border horse who wore out two saddles a season and whose modest silence about his own skill only made tavern keepers talk more. Brennan was eighteen, handsome, quick-witted, and possessed of the gift of making every sheep-farmer believe their disputes were the only business he cared about.

Casian was the fourth. He was the boy who worked in the orchard, whose pony had a dull trot, and who had never once raised his voice at a tenant. In the marcher lands, where a lord’s worth was measured in lances or rent-chests, being known as an agreeable boy was the gentlest way to be forgotten.

It was an arrangement he had cultivated with great care, and he took immense pleasure in the work.

Fifty years ago, in his grandfather’s youth, the garden had been a rough plot with two sour damson trees and a ditch where pigs rooted. Over four summers, Casian had deepened the soil, terraced the slope with dry-stacked limestone, and coaxed sweet herbs and winter savory from the cold clay. Against the lime-washed eastern wall, he had built a cold-frame of timber and horn panes that had cost twenty silver pennies and half a cask of ale, keeping frost off his slips until May. The roses ran along the base of that wall, sixty paces of thorn and leaf, and today half of them were sick with blight.

Behind him, through the wide pantry doorway, the house stirred into its morning clamour. His mother’s voice rose over the slap of butter-paddles, offering a brisk judgment on yesterday's bake. Somewhere upstairs, heavy timber creaked, followed by a muffled crack and an abrupt, guilty stillness.

Casian clipped another dead shoot. "Elna," he said quietly.

The stillness in the doorway lasted two heartbeats.

"It tipped," said Elna, emerging into the sunlight.

She was twelve, with uncombed brown hair pinned by a twisted copper bodkin and a smear of meal cake on her collar. She held herself with the wounded dignity of someone whose innocence had been challenged before she had finished chewing.

"Did the earthen pitcher tip by itself?" Casian asked, not looking up.

"The table was crooked," she said, crossing the crushed gravel with deliberate steps. "And the cat was under the bench. If Aldric didn't leave his surveyor’s rods against the lintel, people wouldn't catch their skirts."

She dropped onto the broad gritstone bench by the herb-bed, her heels clearing the mint border.

"What are you cutting them for?" she asked, chin on her knees. "They have green tips."

"The pith is brown inside," Casian told her. He pared away a sliver of bark to show the dry core. "If you leave the dead wood on the cane, November frost gets into the split, and the root rots before Candlemas. You have to take the rot out while the sun is high."

She considered the cane with her head tilted. "Can you do that with men?"

Casian glanced sideways at her. "Only on market days, and only when Da isn't looking."

Elna gave a short snort of laughter. "Gareth says there are riders in the hills. He said three drovers from the north saw dust over the Black Crag on Tuesday."

"Gareth looks for dust because he has twenty fresh lances in the paddock and nothing to feed them but oats," Casian said mildly. He reached out and turned her hand toward the briar. "Hold the haft here. Squeeze until the edge bites through the knot, not the flesh."

She took the hook with both hands, her narrow fingers dotted with old berry-scratches. He kept his knuckles steady beneath her wrist while she sawed through a stubborn spur. The cane snapped free with a clean pop, and she tossed it into the basket with an expression of immense triumph.

"I could do the whole row," she announced.

"You could," he said, taking the knife back. "And then mother would ask why her syrup tasted like hedge-twigs. Go see if cook has scalded the cream crocks. Take a turnip to the bay pony on your way."

"The bay pony bites."

"The bay pony bites when you tease him with an empty sleeve," he replied. "Do not tease him."

She lingered for a moment, scuffing her turnshoe against the gravel, then ran back toward the porch with her smock flapping in the breeze.

Casian watched her vanish beneath the lintel. The garden settled back into its warm midsummer calm: the heavy drone of hoverflies over fennel, crushed mint underfoot, cool wall shade stretching across the ditch.

Then, through the ground beneath his boots, the world moved.

---

The perception was neither sound nor sight. It did not arrive with a flash of sorcery or the ringing of bells. Argius had once described it as a displacement in the substrate—the way a barge creates an invisible bow-wave on a slow canal, detectable on the opposite bank before the prow has cleared the reeds.

Casian called it the Current, and he had felt its threads since his seventh winter.

A mile and a quarter north-east, where the old drovers' track climbed out of the alder bottoms onto the spine of High Crag, a single horseman had halted.

The horse was a lean upland roan, blowing hard and crusted with salt-sweat. It had come twelve miles over the ridges since daybreak, skirting sheep-folds and keeping off the stone highway. The man in the saddle sat hunched against the morning glare, wearing a boiled-leather jack with iron studs filed flat so they would not catch the sun. He had a short bow cased behind his right knee and two empty goatskins lashed to his cantle.

He was looking down at the Vale manor.

From the crest of High Crag, the holding lay exposed like an open ledger: nine horses in the home paddock, three empty hay-wains by the barn, peat-smoke from the bakehouse hearth. A scout could count armsmen on the ditch, bushels under the slate roofs, and the minutes needed for horsemen to reach the road.

*A captain's man,* Casian observed silently. *Sent out of the eastern valleys to see how fat the sheep are before the harvest.*

"He is from the border raiders," Argius said within his skull. "Second tier. Equipment shows discipline, but lack of water reserves indicates he anticipated an easier descent through the gully."

*He intends to circle west toward the sheep-wash,* Casian thought. *To see if our cattle are herded within the stockade at dusk.*

"Removal is trivial," Argius stated, its tone devoid of enthusiasm or hesitation. "A severed spinal cord at this range requires minimal displacement. Cause of death will appear as a fall from the saddle onto granite."

*No.*

"He will report sixty head of cattle and sixteen mounted guards to a warlord in the hills."

*He will report nothing of the kind,* Casian replied.

He turned a severed cane over in his fingers, feeling the rough scales of the bark. He did not stand up from the bench. He did not raise his hand or close his eyes.

Across a mile and a quarter of sun-bleached valley, across three sheep-pastures, two stone walls, and a stand of coppiced ash, he reached into the Current and touched the roan mare’s left shoulder.

It was not a blow. It was merely the convincing suggestion of a stone slipping beneath a hoof, paired with an instinctual turn toward easier ground. The roan’s ears flicked back; the mare tossed her head, blew through her nostrils, and swung her forequarters three points south, drifting down the shaded sheep-track away from the valley rim.

A mile and a quarter out, the Current shifted. The mare’s balance swung downhill; Casian felt the sudden, involuntary tug of leather as the rider gathered his reins against the slip of chalk scree. For three seconds the scout fought the animal’s turn, his silhouette dark against the sun glare on High Crag, before the fatigue of twelve dry miles did the rest. The roan took the homeward track at an easy trot, carrying horse and rider down into the fold of the hills until the ridge stood empty once more.

In the walled garden, Casian dropped the dead briar into the basket and picked up the whetstone.

"The horse is five miles from its feed," Argius observed. "He will reach the camp past nightfall with an inaccurate survey."

*He will tell them the Vales are poor farmers who spend their coin on oats and sleep behind timber gates,* Casian thought. *Which is precisely what they should believe until the oats are cut.*

"You could erase their camp tomorrow."

*And then three other captains would ride out of the hills to see who owned the empty tents. We will have peace while we weed the ditch.*

He ran the whetstone down the curve of the hook, three short rasping strokes. The ridge above High Crag was empty again, save for the buzzards turning slow circles in the pale summer sky.

---

At noon, Lady Oswin Vale came down the flagstone path with her wool shawl pinned across her chest and her mouth set in a thin, pale seam.

She was seventy-one years old, possessed of knees that creaked like ungreased cart-axles, and had buried a husband, two brothers, and three border wars without ever using a walking stick. She halted at the end of the gritstone bench, shielded her eyes against the glare, and surveyed the butchered briars.

"You've butchered them," she said.

Casian wiped the blade on his leather apron. "I've trimmed the rot, grandmother."

"You've butchered them to the root," she repeated, taking her seat with the slow dignity of an old hawk settling on a roost. "They will put out suckers from the wild stock, and in three seasons you'll have thorny scrub that wouldn't shelter a hare. Not that I'll be here to see it. I shall be under the chancel flags, which will be considerably quieter."

"You have promised to be under the chancel flags every summer since my tenth name-day," Casian said mildly.

"I have been sensible since your tenth name-day," Oswin replied, not missing a beat. "There is a distinction. Your grandfather had the same foolish look when he planted that damson tree. A mule kicked him into a ditch the following Martinmas, and the tree never bore fruit that wasn't sour enough to turn milk."

Casian leaned against the lime-washed wall, letting the warm stone soak into his linen shirt. "The stone by the millrace is settling. Water has worked behind the footings since the spring flood."

Oswin turned her head, her faded blue eyes fixing on him with sharp clarity. "Did Edric see it?"

"Edric was looking at the price of barley."

"Your father looks at what can be written in a column and totaled before supper," she said. "If a wall does not fall on a horse, he assumes it will stand until Michaelmas. What do you intend to do about it?"

"Hob the mason's nephew owes three days of ditching for his pasture rent," Casian said. "We'll haul twelve cartloads of squared ragstone from the quarry next week, before the rain sets in, and pin the corner before it slides into the silt."

Oswin stared out across the garden. The honeysuckle along the lower terrace was beginning to droop in the heat, filling the courtyard with heavy musk.

"Your father is down in the meadow swearing at the haymakers," she remarked. "Gareth rode in an hour ago with a notched iron stirrup and three stories about brigands. Brennan is in the pantry telling the dairymaids that the Count of Varyn eats lark-pies with a gold spoon."

"Brennan is probably right about the spoon," Casian said.

"Brennan is an idiot, but people like him because he smiles while he robs them of their attention," Oswin said, adjusting her shawl. "Go down to the yard and be pleasant to your father. You are always pleasant to him."

"It costs very little to be pleasant."

"That," said Oswin, rising from the bench with a sharp snap of her knees, "is why it is the most suspicious thing about you."

She walked back toward the house, her turnshoes clicking softly against the gravel, leaving Casian alone with the roses and the buzzing flies.

*She sees one thing,* Argius murmured.

*She sees an old woman's reflection in a puddle,* Casian answered. *And she is too proud to admit she likes the garden.*

---

Supper at the Vale manor was served when the work stopped, which in August was rarely before the sun had dipped behind the western ridges.

The great hall was an old, low-timbered room, smelling of oak-smoke, tallow, dogs, and sour mash. A long trestle of rough-hewn ash ran down the center, its surface stained with forty years of spilled ale. Twelve sat at the high table when the household was assembled, and tonight every stool was filled.

At the far end, Aldric sat beside his wife Rowan, his brow furrowed over a scrap of parchment detailing sheep-tallow sales, while their infant son, Emric, sat strapped into a wicker chair between them. Emric was twelve months old, stout-legged, and occupied with beating a flat horn spoon against a wooden trencher in a furious rhythm that set the hounds twitching by the hearth.

Gareth sat three stools down, his sleeves rolled over corded forearms bruised from spear-drill. He was eating salt beef with his dagger, arguing with Brennan across the trenchers about horse-flesh at Harrowmere.

"He called him a roan," Brennan was saying, eyes bright with mock outrage. "A five-year-old gelding with clean hocks, and the rascal called him a roan! The beast was the exact shade of an unwashed cooking pot."

"An unwashed pot with good bone," Gareth retorted. "A marcher pony doesn't need to look like an archbishop's palfrey. If he has wind for thirty miles of moor and doesn't colic on wet hay, he's worth four silver marks."

"He's worth two marks and a skin of sour cider," Brennan laughed. "And the dealer knew it. He saw your spurs and put twenty shillings on the price."

Gareth scowled, but there was no heat in it. Beside them, Elna was quietly sneaking salt pork rind to the lurcher beneath the table, her face a mask of solemn innocence.

Maren Vale moved along the benches with a pitcher of small beer, refilling cups and cuffing Gareth lightly on the ear for resting his greasy dagger on clean linen. She was forty-five, her hair bound in bleached wool, her face lined by twenty marcher winters. When she reached Casian, her hand lingered on his linen shirt, her thumb brushing his collar with the brief, habitual pressure of a mother who had spent sixteen years deciding which silences she could afford to keep. She patted his shoulder twice, said nothing at all, and moved down the trestle to catch Emric's spoon before it found the butter crock.

At the head of the table, Baron Edric Vale wiped his mouth with the back of his wrist.

He was forty-nine, with iron-grey hair cropped close to his skull and the heavy, stooped shoulders of a man who spent ten hours a day in a high-cantled saddle. The marches had taken their toll on him: a crooked left finger from an arrow-glance at High Tor, a deep furrow between his grey eyes, and the perpetual knowledge that his barony lay four days' ride from help if the hill tribes ever united behind a single banner.

He struck the tabletop with the heel of his horn knife, and the hall fell quiet, save for Emric’s rhythmic drumming.

"Jory took a report from the drovers at the Ashgrove ford," Edric said, his voice flat and gravelly. "Four riders crossed the shallows three days back, heading west. They were mounted on good northern geldings, carrying light mail beneath their tunics, and they didn't stop to water at the tavern."

Gareth straightened on his stool, his hand dropping toward his belt. "Whose mark?"

"None," Edric said. "They wore grey frieze cloaks without badge or livery. The miller said they kept their chins down and paid for their hay in clipped silver from the southern mints."

"Bandits don't ride northern geldings," Gareth said sharply. "Those are raiders out of the high valleys. Let me take twelve lances and sweep the lower cut. If they're scouting sheep-runs, we can bottle them in the gorge."

"And if they are messengers from the Count of Varyn's bailiff?" Edric countered, his gaze steady. "Or factors buying wool for the cloth-guilds at Harrowmere? We do not ride across our neighbours' borders with drawn steel because four men failed to pass the time of day with a miller. We have twenty lances, Gareth. The Count has five hundred, and the King is two hundred miles away in his winter palace. We do not look for wars on empty roads."

He looked down the table until his eyes settled on Casian, who was quietly peeling an apple with a horn-handled knife.

"Casian," the Baron said.

Casian laid the apple skin neatly on the rim of his trencher. "Sir?"

"The east wall along the millrace. Hob says the corner is bulging."

"Two spans of the footing have slipped into the gravel," Casian said evenly. "The water worked behind the mortar during the June thaw. I spoke to Hob’s nephew this afternoon. We'll quarry twelve cartloads of ragstone on Monday and rebuild the pier before the autumn rains get into the ditch."

Edric held his gaze for a long moment, reading the steady line of his son's mouth. There was no hesitation in Casian's manner, no request for silver, no demand for armsmen. He simply had the work in hand.

"Take two mules from the lower barn," Edric said, his tone softening. "And do not let Hob charge me for lime we burned in our own kiln."

"I won't," Casian said.

The Baron picked up his ale cup, drank half of it, and turned back to Aldric to discuss wool-fells. Under the bench, Elna kicked Casian's boot with her turnshoe.

"You're a dreadful liar," she whispered out of the corner of her mouth. "You hadn't talked to Hob's nephew. You were pruning roses until noon."

"I talked to him through the fence while you were eating plum-cake," Casian murmured back, slicing off a wedge of apple and slipping it into her palm. "Eat your fruit and stop kicking my shins."

---

Later, when the candles had guttered and the hall had surrendered to the heavy breathing of sleeping men, Edric walked out along the eastern rampart.

Casian fell in beside him at the postern gate without a word. Since he was twelve, whenever the Baron walked the perimeter walls to smell the wind, the fourth son had walked three paces behind his shoulder, quiet as an owl.

The night air was cool and thin, smelling of crushed bracken and damp chill from the alder bottoms. To the north, the ridges of High Crag stood like jagged black teeth against a sky thick with stars. The manor below lay dark, save for a single slit of yellow tallow-light in the bailiff's lean-to.

"Jory's nervous," Edric said, his hands resting on the rough stone merlons. "He thinks the four riders were counting our gates."

"They were," Casian said quietly.

Edric turned his head, his grey beard catching starlight. "You think Gareth is right?"

"Gareth wants an enemy with a banner and a trumpeter," Casian replied. "These men won't bring either. A warlord who sends four riders in grey frieze isn't planning a siege. He wants to know how many carts we send to Harrowmere, how many cattle graze the lower meadows, and whether we lock the paddock gates when the moon is down."

Edric was silent for a long while. Down in the sheep-folds, a bell clinked faintly as a ewe shifted in straw.

"When I was twenty," the Baron said, his voice heavy with old fatigue, "I thought the marches were a place where a man could build a house that would stand forever. There were no courtiers here, no bishops whispering into the King's ear, no tax-gatherers with three clerks behind them. Just clean land, good stone, and the road to the sea."

He paused, resting his palms against the cold gritstone.

"It turns out what there is instead is nobody to help," he went on. "If the crops fail, we eat seed-corn. If raiders come, we hold our own gates or we burn. Gareth wants to conquer the hills, Aldric wants to balance the ledger, and Brennan wants everyone to think he is a prince. You're the only one of them that doesn't want something from me. It's restful and it worries me."

Casian looked at the old man's weathered hands. "I want the wall rebuilt before the wet."

Edric gave a short, dry huff of air that served him for a laugh. "Aye. That's what worries me."

He clapped Casian once on the shoulder, turned on his heel, and walked back toward the dark timber porch of the hall, his boots ringing hollow on the stone steps.

---

Casian waited until the postern latch clicked shut before he stepped through the wicket gate and took the narrow drovers' path that climbed through hazel scrub toward the high ridge.

He did not hurry. His boots made no sound on the dry chalk; he walked with the effortless, balanced grace of a creature entirely at home in the dark. The night was vast and silent, filled with the rustle of field mice in dry grass and the distant, lonely call of a curlew across the bogs.

At the crest of High Crag, he stopped where the scout's horse had halted that morning.

He stood in the exact indentation where the roan mare's hoof had cut the turf, turned his back to the moors, and looked down at Vale manor.

From here, the house was a fragile smudge of slate and timber cupped in the dark hollow of the valley. A single light burned in the solar window. The stone wall he had promised to repair looked like a thread of grey yarn dropped across the pasture. Nine horses, three hay-wains, thirty souls sleeping under thatch, and twenty miles of empty heath stretching between them and the nearest garrison.

To a warlord looking down from the hills, it was a prize waiting for a torch.

Casian stood motionless, his linen tunic stirring in the night wind.

He had watched a world end.

He did not carry a name for that place; this language had no words for the towers of blue light, the shining roads that crossed oceans without ships, or the quiet, terrible morning when the sky cracked open like glass and swallowed thirty million people in a single breath. That world was gone. It had left no ruins here, no scrap of iron, no song in the mouths of marcher crones. There was only Casian, walking an empty ridge with an ancient, damaged fragment of its mind coiled behind his eyes, carrying the memory of an extinguished civilization the way a traveller carries the dust of a burned city in the seams of his cloak.

He could not go back. He could not tell his father, or his mother, or the fierce, sharp-tongued sister who stole his apples. To speak of it was to invite the fires of the Ash, whose priests burned men for far lesser heresies.

So he had chosen this valley. He had chosen the stone bench, the sour soil under the terrace, the smell of damp sheep-wool, and the simple, stubborn life of a border holding that asked for nothing more than lime, timber, and honest bread.

He would make this place safe. Not with an army—armies drew the eyes of kings and bishops—but with an invisible, unyielding quiet that swallowed every hand raised against it.

He looked down toward the eastern corner of the garden wall, where the ground had been cleared and trenched. A young walnut sapling, no thicker than his thumb, sat resting in a bucket of damp moss inside the root-cellar, waiting for the morrow's spade.

*Forty years,* he thought into the dark. *Forty years before it yields a basket of nuts.*

"You will be here for all of it," Argius said.

Casian looked east toward the jagged black rim of the hills, where a warlord's fires flickered faintly against the stars, cold and distant. A country that did not yet know it had a new master.

*Yes,* he thought. *I will.*

He did not turn. He did not take a single step down the chalk track.

Through the Current, distance simply ceased to matter. The night air along the ridge compressed—a silent, seamless folding of a mile and a quarter of empty moorland into the space between two heartbeats.

The wind vanished. The cold chalk and the smell of bracken snapped shut, replaced by the warm, close dark of the great hall: dry oak timber, sheep-tallow, and the steady breathing of sleeping hounds by the hearth.

Casian sat alone at the long ash table, exactly where he had left his wooden cup.

He turned the cup around on the dark grain, once, without hurry, before the guttering candle could even register the draft.

Outside, past the wall and the road and the high ridge, the night came down over thirty miles of marcher country that had no idea who was sitting in the dark, keeping it safe.
