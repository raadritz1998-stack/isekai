The roses had come in badly this year and Casian Vale was of two opinions about it.

The first opinion was the ordinary one, available to anybody who leaned on the fence and looked: the spring had been wet and then suddenly it had not been, and the bushes had spent themselves on leaf instead of flower, and any man who had ever put a cutting in the ground could see the whole story in one bad bud. He had said so to his mother at breakfast. She had said that the roses did as they liked and so did he, and had put a bowl in front of him.

The second opinion was longer, and he kept it to himself, the way he kept most things.

*The third bush has root-burn,* he thought. *The soil under the east wall has gone sour and it has nothing to do with rain.*

"Say that again," said Argius, "and say it in a voice."

*I wasn't speaking.*

"You were thinking in sentences. You do that when you are pleased about something small."

Casian turned his head a fraction, which was all the acknowledgment Argius ever got in a garden with his sister in it, and went back to the roses.

He was sixteen. He was, by the count of anyone in the barony who had ever bothered to make one, the fourth and least of Baron Edric's sons, the one who did not ride well enough to be a credit and did not read well enough to be a worry, and who had somehow got the reputation of being pleasant company, which in the eastern marches was generally the last thing said about a man before he was forgotten. His brothers were better at the things that had names. Aldric was the heir and was careful about it. Gareth was the sword, and everybody in three counties knew it, including Gareth, who was modest about it in a way that made it worse. Brennan was the one people remembered, because Brennan remembered them.

Casian was the one who was in the garden.

It was a good garden. That was the thing nobody quite said out loud — it was too good, and it had become too good slowly enough that the barony had decided not to notice. The Vale holding sat on a low shoulder of land above the Harrow road, with the house's back to the wind and its face into the sun, and the walled garden ran along the eastern side where the light was best and the frost came last. In his grandfather's time it had been a kitchen plot with a plum tree in it. Now there were fourteen beds and a glasshouse that had cost more than a good horse and a walnut sapling he had been babying since spring, and there were roses along the whole eastern wall, and the roses had come in badly.

He picked up the knife and went along the wall cutting out the dead wood, and the morning did what mornings do.

Behind him, through the open door of the kitchen, the house was making the noises of a house with too many people in it. Maren Vale was saying something about the state of the bread and was not, in fact, saying it to anyone, which was one of the ways she talked to herself. The dog had got into the wrong room. Somewhere upstairs there was a thump and then the specific silence of a twelve-year-old deciding whether to confess to it.

"Elna," Casian said, without turning round.

The silence considered its options.

"It fell," said Elna, from the doorway.

"Did it fall on purpose?"

"It fell," she said again, with dignity, and came out into the garden with her hair half up and dirt already on her cheek, which was simply the state Elna was usually in. She was twelve and had been told to brush her hair and had clearly decided that the twelve-year-old who brushed her hair was not the twelve-year-old she wanted to be.

She sat down on the bench. It was the oldest bench in the county, in Casian's private opinion, and it had a groove worn into the seat that was exactly the width of a person who had been sitting there a long time.

"What are you doing to them?"

"Cutting off the parts that are dead."

"They don't look dead."

"They're not the parts you can see," he said. "That's the trick of it. The bit that's dying is in the middle and it looks fine from here. If I leave it, the whole arm goes."

Elna thought about this for a moment, which she did with her whole face.

"Can you do people like that?"

"Only on Thursdays."

She hit him with the flat of her hand on his arm, which was the correct response, and he allowed himself to be hit and then handed her the knife and showed her exactly where to cut, holding her wrist steady, and she did two of them wrong and one of them right, and was unreasonably pleased about the one.

Later, when he thought back on the whole of that summer — and he would think back on it, in the way that a man thinks about the last warm day before a winter he did not know was coming — this was the part that would come first. The bench. Elna's wrist under his hand, which was so small it was almost a joke, and the way she chewed the inside of her cheek when she was concentrating. His mother's voice in the kitchen. The walnut, four feet tall and ridiculous, doing nothing in the corner of the garden the way walnuts do for the first forty years of their lives.

The stranger on the ridge.

---

He felt the man arrive the way a person feels weather.

It was not sight, exactly. Argius had explained it once, a long time ago, in the flat uninterested voice it used for things it thought were obvious: a thing that moves through a country leaves a wake, and a wake is the same whether the country is water or air or a field of wheat, and Argius had access to the whole of the field. Casian had been seven. He had spent most of that year being quietly appalled at what he could do and then quietly getting used to it, which was, he would later understand, what a childhood is for.

The ridge was the high ground north of the road, a mile and a quarter out, where the drovers' track came over and the land fell away all along the Vale wall. A man had come up onto it from the east, on a horse that was tired, and had stopped where the track met the lip of the ridge, which was exactly the place a man would stop if he wanted to see the Vale holding without being on Vale land.

Casian had turned two dead roses over in his hand and considered him.

He was a scout. That was not a guess — Argius said it in the same breath it said anything, the way a man names a bird. He had the wrong coat for the road, a good knife, no water, and the specific stillness of a person who has been trained not to shift his weight while he looks at something. He had counted the horses in the yard. He had looked at the granary. He had, at some point in the last minute, looked at the smoke from the kitchens and estimated how many people were feeding themselves off it.

None of it was unusual. Scouts came over that ridge all summer — cutters, drovers, the occasional man who had heard there was work at Vale. That was not the trouble.

The trouble was that this one had come from Hask Veyr's country, and had come the long way round to avoid the road, and was now sitting on a horse being careful not to be in the way of anyone who might look up.

*He'll be back for the count,* Casian thought. *He didn't write it down, so he'll want to see the yard again with the carts in it.*

"Casian," said Argius.

*I know.*

"Say it."

*He isn't ours. He's looking for someone who is.*

"He is looking for a road and a number of horses. He will be in Harrowmere by dark and asleep by midnight, and if he is the sort of man Hask keeps he will not talk about the boy in the garden, because there was no boy in the garden."

*No.*

"There will be a lot of these."

*I know.*

He should, he supposed, consider it. There was an argument — which he would make to nobody — that a man who came up onto a ridge to count another man's granary was doing a thing that argued for a quiet ending, and he could do a quiet ending from a bench with a rose in his hand and no one would ever so much as suspect the weather.

But the man had been careful, and the count had been honest, and Casian had spent a great deal of the last sixteen years being pleasantly underestimated and had come to enjoy the shape of it, like a good coat. Killing a careful man for counting ears of wheat was the sort of thing that made a summer about something. And it was the first week of August.

He turned the dead roses over in his hand, cut the last stem, and then reached down the length of the ridge and turned the horse.

Not hard. That was the whole of it — you could not tell it had been done. The horse came round the way horses come round when something catches their eye at the edge of a field, and stepped off the lip of the ridge with its head down, and the man went with it, and by the time he had it gathered the sun was in his face and the Vale holding was a smudge of thatch behind a wall with a boy somewhere in it, quite possibly.

He would go back down the way he came. It was not compulsion, exactly, and Casian would not have called it that. It was a suggestion made in a language the man did not know he spoke, applied to nothing more profound than a tired horse's sense of which way was downhill.

The ridge was empty by the time Elna came back out with two cups and the news that their mother had said, specifically, that the two of them were to stay out of the kitchen until the bread was done.

"Who were you looking at?"

"Nobody. A rider."

Elna squinted at the ridge, where there was no rider.

"You look at nothing the same way Da looks at the rent rolls," she said. "It's not a compliment."

---

Oswin Vale came out at noon, when the day had gone white at the edges, which was her habit and always had been: she took the heat in the morning indoors and came out into the shadiest part of the garden when everyone else was going in.

She was seventy-one and did not use a stick, which the family had stopped arguing with some years ago. She sat down at the end of the bench, surveyed the roses, and said, "You've cut them too hard."

"I've cut them exactly right."

"You've cut them too hard and they'll come back from the bottom and in three years you'll have no shape at all. Not that it matters to me. I'll be dead."

"You've been dying since I was ten."

"I've been pragmatic since you were ten," said Oswin. "It's different."

She was the only one in the house he had ever been careful around, and had been since he was small — not because she suspected anything, but because she was the one person in the family with an eye that did not go soft in the middle. Maren saw him clearly and had decided, a long time ago and privately and entirely without him ever knowing, not to make it a thing. Edric saw a fourth son and was content. Aldric saw a brother. Gareth saw the boy he had taught to hold a sword badly.

Oswin saw a great deal and said about a tenth of it.

"Your father's in a mood," she said.

"About what?"

"About the road, or the tax, or your brother's boots. I stopped keeping track of the moods some years ago. You should go and be pleasant at him."

"I'm always pleasant at him."

"You are," she agreed. "It's the most suspicious thing about you."

She said that as though it were an observation about the weather, and did not look at him while she said it, and then she asked him whether the walnut was worth anything and listened to him explain why it was for a full minute before announcing that she doubted it and going back inside.

Casian sat with the walnut for a while after that.

*She sees one thing,* said Argius, eventually.

*One thing is a lot.*

"It is a good rule," it added, "to be boring at someone who looks."

*She doesn't look. She just decides.*

"That is worse."

---

The house filled up at supper, which was between six and whenever it finished, in the Vale way.

There were twelve at the table if everyone came, and everyone usually came. Aldric and Rowan, and Emric, one year old and in the mode whereby any object in the world could be dropped from a height and then looked at. Gareth with a knuckle gone swollen from the yard. Brennan back from Harrowmere and talking about it, who had the gift — genuinely, Casian thought, it was a gift — of being interested in people's furniture and their sons and their winter, so that men who did not like each other told him the same thing within an hour and were each surprised later to have said it. Edric at the head, looking grey and steady. Maren down the side where she could get up and down to the kitchen forty times. Oswin, who ate like a small bird and drank like an old soldier.

And Elna, wedged in the middle, arguing with Brennan about a horse.

"— because you said he was a bay and he is not, he's more of a —"

"He is a bay, Elna."

"He is the colour of a pot."

"The colour of a pot," said Brennan, with dignity, "is a well-known shade."

"Maren," said Edric, in the pause, and everyone stopped and looked at the head of the table, because that was how the baron spoke, once and then not again. "I will be at the Harrowmere cut on Thursday. Who's coming?"

"Me," said Gareth.

"Me," said Brennan, and then, catching his father's eye, "sensible to be seen."

"Then stop being seen by me. Casian."

Casian had been eating with his eyes on the table and the whole eastern marches laid out behind them, which was a habit he had and could not break and had long since decided not to be troubled about.

"Casian," said his father again. "The east wall's coming down at the corner by the millrace. Jory says a fortnight if it wants doing before the wet."

"It wants doing," said Casian. "I'll take Aldous's boy and do it next week. The stone's already cut."

"There's stone cut?"

"There's been stone cut since spring."

Edric looked at him for a moment and then said, "Good," and went back to eating, and that was the whole of the exchange, and it was, Casian thought, a perfect illustration of the arrangement that had kept him comfortably invisible for sixteen years. He was useful in the way a good knife is useful — available, unremarkable, not requiring a conversation. Nobody ever asked the knife what it thought.

Under the table Elna kicked him, as though she had heard.

"You're next to Da at the harvest," she said out of the side of her mouth. "You'll have to say something clever."

"I'll say something about roses."

"Do not talk to him about the roses."

"Gareth's boots, then."

"Casian."

"Fine. I'll talk to him about Brennan. He's very popular at the moment."

Afterwards his father walked the wall, which he did not do often and had done more in the last two years, and Casian fell in beside him without being asked, which was the sort of thing that had never needed asking between them.

The east wall was the good wall. It ran from the corner of the house down to the millrace and turned north, and standing at the north end of it a man could see the road for three miles and the ridge beyond that, and it was for exactly that reason that the wall had been built there and not where it would have been easier.

"Jory had a man in from the Ashgrove road," said Edric.

"Anything?"

"Riders. Four, three days back, going west, not stopping." His father's hands were behind his back. "Could be nothing. There's a great deal of nothing on that road."

"And if it isn't nothing?"

"Then it isn't ours," said Edric, "and that's the whole of it. Ashgrove is under Harven, Harven is under Varyn, and Varyn is a hundred miles from here and has his own troubles. I write to the Count and the Count writes back that he is grateful for my concern." He stopped at the corner and looked out at nothing in particular. "When I was your age I thought the marches were a place where a man could get things done, because there was nobody to stop him. It turns out what there is instead is nobody to help."

Casian said nothing, because the true answer was long and the true answer was not available.

"Your brother thinks I should send to Varyn again," said Edric.

"Gareth thinks you should send to Varyn with a hundred men behind the letter."

His father made a sound that was almost a laugh and then thought better of it.

"You're the only one of them that doesn't want something from me," he said. "Do you know that? Aldric wants to be trusted with it. Gareth wants to be used. Brennan wants me to be pleased with him and can't help it. You don't want anything. It's restful and it worries me."

"I want the wall finished before the wet."

"Mm," said Edric. "That's what worries me."

They stood there a while. Down past the millrace the water made the noise it made, and the last of the light was going off the thatch, and somewhere in the yard a dog was arguing with something that had probably been a rat.

"Four riders going west," Casian said. "Not stopping."

"Not stopping."

"They've stopped somewhere."

His father looked at him sideways for a second longer than the remark deserved, and then said, "Everyone stops somewhere," and turned back toward the house, and the moment closed over like water.

---

The light had gone the colour it goes at the end of an August day, and the noise at the table had resolved into its usual three-featured shape: Brennan and Gareth arguing, Maren and Rowan talking across them about something else, and Emric, who had been given a spoon and was beating the table with it in the rhythm of a small and terrible god.

Casian sat in the middle of all of it with his hands round a cup and was, for no reason he had ever been able to put into a sentence, entirely content.

He had seen a world end. He had been somebody else, in a country with no name in this language, and had lived and had died there, and Argius carried the pieces of it the way a man's pocket carries the pieces of a stone he picked up and forgot. It was not a thing he could say. It was not a thing anybody could be told. There were people who burned other people for a great deal less, and they had been getting better at it.

So he could not explain why he had put in a garden. He could not explain the walnut, or the glass, or the fact that he had spent a full month of his life working out how to get the water to the east wall without a boy having to carry it. He loved his family, and that was easy and had never needed explaining. He loved this house, which had been good to him before he could be worth anything to it.

He also, quietly, wanted to make the whole eastern marches into something that could not be taken away.

It was not a plan yet. It was the shape of an appetite — the way a boy who has lost something thinks about a locked door. He did not have words for it, and if he had had words he would not have used them at the supper table, and if anyone had asked him what he meant to do with his life he would have said something about the roses and been telling something close to the truth.

Later, when the house had gone quiet in the way it did around the second hour after the candles, he walked up the drovers' track to the top of the ridge.

He did not do this often. There was no need — the whole country was in his hands the way a book is in a man's hands, and he could read any page of it from the bench. But there was a difference between reading a page and standing on it, and he had learned, early and by himself, that the difference mattered to him. It was probably the only sentimental thing about him.

The track came over the lip where the land fell away and the Vale wall lay along the bottom of it like a line drawn by somebody careful. The house was a dark shape with two lights in it. The yard was a darker one. Somewhere in the middle of all that, his mother was asleep with her hand under her cheek, and Elna was asleep with her mouth open, and his father was probably awake.

This was where the man had sat his tired horse. Casian stood in the exact spot, because he liked to know how a thing had looked from the other side, and looked down at his own house the way a stranger would.

A wall. A granary. Nine horses, if you counted the carters'. Smoke, going up steady, which meant no one was going anywhere. A barony worth a raid, if a man had no imagination and a great many men.

He had spent a great deal of his life deciding not to be seen. It had been easy, and it had been pleasant, and it had never once been because he was afraid.

He was beginning to think, though, that there was a difference between not being seen and not being *counted* — and that the country he had been quietly keeping, without ever announcing it, was full of men on ridges with a number in their heads.

The night was warm. Somewhere out east, past the ridge and the road and the last of the Vale fields, in a country he could have looked into if he had wanted to and had not, yet, wanted to, a warlord's horses were eating the grass off somebody's winter pasture.

*Not this week,* Casian thought. *Next month, maybe. When I know their names.*

He went back down the hill.

*Forty years,* he thought. *The walnut. Forty years before it does anything.*

"You will be here for all of it," said Argius.

Casian turned his cup around on the table, once, without hurry.

*Yes,* he thought. *I will.*

Outside, past the wall and the road and the ridge, the night came down over a country that did not know it had a new owner.

It was not, yet, untrue that nothing had happened.
