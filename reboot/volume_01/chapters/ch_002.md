The taproot of the walnut sapling was the length of a boy’s forearm, pale and brittle as dry tallow, and Casian Vale set it into the dark pit with both hands.

He had spent the first two hours of daylight behind the eastern wall, where the ground sloped toward the orchard ditch. It was the deepest earth on the holding: four feet of black loam layered over river-gravel, sheltered from the bitter northers that swept down off High Crag in November. He had dug the pit wide, lining the bottom with two baskets of rotted leaf-mold, a shovel of crushed sheep-bone from the kitchen trench, and three buckets of ditch-silt hauled up from the millrace before sunrise.

The sapling was a yearling switch, no thicker than his thumb, brought in two days back by a wool-carter from the southern shires and kept wrapped in damp moss inside the root-cellar. It looked like nothing at all—a peeled hazel wand stuck into a mound of earth.

"Forty years," Casian murmured, tamping the earth firm around the root-collar with the heel of his turnshoe. "Before it yields a full crop of nuts."

"Fifty-six," Argius corrected within his skull. "Given the latitude and the depth of the winter frost line. You will be fifty-six years of age before the crown reaches thirty cubits."

*I have fifty-six years.*

"A bold projection for an organism composed primarily of water and cartilage," said Argius. "Historically, border baronies in this province demonstrate a dynastic median lifespan of forty-two winters."

*My grandfather lived to seventy-four, and only died because a mule kicked him into a ditch.*

"An unpredictable variable," Argius noted. "The mule, not the ditch."

Casian smiled faintly. He drove two peeled ash stakes into the clay on either side of the sapling, bound the slender stem between them with loops of greased sheep-twine, and emptied a wooden tub of pond water over the mound until the loam settled into a dark, glistening paste.

Across the terrace, the row of briars he had pruned yesterday stood clean and bare in the morning light. On the hardiest wild rootstock near the orchard wicket, the graft he had notched four days ago was already knitting. It was a slip of cultivated white damask taken from an old bush in the church garth at Harrowmere—tight, fragrant flowers with petals like crumpled linen, grafted onto the savage, thorn-heavy cane of the native marcher briar. A small swelling of green rind showed beneath the waxed linen binding.

The graft had taken.

He turned toward the wooden aqueduct running along the top of the terrace wall. The cistern on the glasshouse roof was full from the June rains, feeding a split-pine trough that ran sixty paces along the limestone coping. At four points, Casian had fitted small sluice-gates of shaved oak hung on greased leather hinges.

He reached up and lifted the first gate.

The water spilled through the notch in a smooth, quiet tongue, spreading across the terrace in a thin sheet that soaked the mint and fennel without scouring the seed-beds. It was an arrangement of simple counter-weights and pine gutters that had taken him three weeks of quiet chiseling to fit, yet it allowed one person to water the entire garden in the time it took to walk from the bench to the wicket.

Behind him, soft turnshoes clicked on the flagstones.

Maren Vale stood on the threshold of the wash-house, her sleeves pinned to her shoulders and her forearms pale with flour. She was looking at the pine trough, the thin skin of water spreading over the parsley, and the small leather hinge swinging true in its frame.

Her face held that flat, neutral stillness she kept for things she had already decided never to question: the look of a sensible housewife observing weather she would simply have to dress for.

"Breakfast," she said.

Casian dropped the sluice-pin into its notch. "Coming, mother."

She lingered for a heartbeat, her gaze traveling from the water-gate to the freshly planted walnut switch in the corner, then turned back into the shadow of the pantry without another word.

That was the whole of it. It was always the whole of it between them: a silence kept so cleanly and for so long that it had become a kind of courtesy.

*She knows I built it,* Casian thought, following her through the low timber doorway.

"She knows the channel was not there three summers ago," Argius said. "She also knows your father did not pay a carpenter to lay it. Her silence is tactical."

*Her silence is kind,* Casian corrected. *There is a difference.*

"The outcome," Argius replied, "is functionally identical."

---

Breakfast in the great hall was an unorganized hour during which twelve people crossed paths, ate barley bread with their fingers, and spoke of nine different errands at once.

Maren held the high bench, dividing a round loaf of grey rye with a heavy horn-handled blade. Jory, the guard captain, stood by the sideboard with his sheepskin cap crushed in his scarred hands, waiting for the Baron to finish his small beer before reporting on the south ditch. Brennan came down the timber stair three steps at a time, smelling of horse-oil and road-dust, his dark hair curling over his tunic like a hedge.

"Brennan," his mother said without looking up from the loaf. "Comb your hair."

"I combed it in the stables," Brennan said cheerfully, catching a crust in mid-air as she sliced it. "A very respectable gelding saw the whole affair and offered no complaint."

"The gelding had no choice," Maren said, sliding a bowl of curds toward him. "Your father wants you at the mill-cut by midday."

"I am already at the mill-cut in spirit," Brennan said, taking his seat beside Casian and digging into the curds. He leaned sideways, lowering his voice. "Gareth is back from the high drove. He’s down in the paddock washing down four horses, and he has a face like a thunderstorm."

Casian buttered his crust. "Did he find raiders?"

"He found a drover with a tongue longer than a cart-shaft," Brennan muttered. "He won't say a word until supper. He likes the hall full when he announces a calamity. It gives his shoulders more breadth."

Maren watched them from across the ash boards, her hands resting flat on the bread-cloth. When Brennan jumped up to steal a dried plum from the pantry crock, she looked at Casian with that same level, unreadable gaze.

"You have three older brothers," she said quietly. "And you could do the work of all three before noon if someone put a whip to your ribs."

Casian chewed his crust. "Aldric likes the ledgers, Gareth likes his lance, and Brennan likes being looked at. If I did their work, they'd have to sit in the orchard and help me weed the leeks."

"God forbid," she murmured. She reached across the boards, pushed the butter-crock an inch closer to his elbow, and said no more about it.

---

At noon, the dry clatter of an iron-tired wagon echoed up from the mill lane.

Hob the mason arrived with two yoke of dun oxen, a cart loaded with squared blocks of grey ragstone from the river quarry, and his fifteen-year-old nephew, a lanky youth whose wrists protruded six inches from his homespun sleeves.

Casian was waiting for them at the corner of the eastern rampart where the ditch met the millrace. The footing had washed out during the torrential June thaw: three spans of foundation stone had slumped into the gravel, leaving the ashlar above hanging on rotten lime mortar.

"Good stone," Hob grunted, spitting into the nettles as he climbed down off the wagon box. He was fifty years old, thick as an oak stump, with stone-dust ground so deeply into the creases of his neck that he looked as though he had been carved out of the same quarry as his blocks. "Took half a day to wedge it out of the lower stratum. Where's the Baron?"

"In the sheep-meadow," Casian said, taking a crowbar from the tail-gate. "He said you weren't to charge him for lime we burned in our own kilns."

Hob let out a rasping wheeze that passed for amusement. "Your father would skin a flint for a turnip and complain of the grit. Give us a hand with the shears, boy."

They worked through the heat of the afternoon, the clink of iron chisels and the heavy thud of settling ragstone masking the drowsy hum of the valley. Casian took the heavy end of the timber levers without being asked, setting his shoulder against the blocks while Hob guided them onto their beds of fresh-slaked lime. He had worked beside Hob since his tenth summer; the mason treated him with the gruff, unthinking familiarity men grant to a boy who knows how to keep his fingers clear of the pinch-point.

"They're building north of Thornwall," Hob said, scraping excess mortar from a joint with his trowel. He took a swig of sour cider from a clay jug and wiped his whiskers with his sleeve. "Two new ranges of stables, timber-framed on granite sills, and a curtain wall ten cubits high around the horse-yard."

Casian steadied a plumb-line against the ashlar. "Baron Osric must have had a fat harvest."

"Harvest my left eye," Hob snorted. "Osric’s tenants have been eating pease-pudding since Michaelmas, and his wool went to the Flemish factors at a discount because of the rot. I laid the footings for his father thirty years back. The Thornwall rent-roll couldn't pay for ten rods of dressed ashlar, let alone two ranges of stables."

"Whose coin is it, then?"

"That’s the queer part of it," Hob said, leaning closer and lowering his gravelly voice. "The steward paid me out of an iron-bound chest kept under the bed in the gatehouse. Southern silver, clipped light, but stamped with marks from the upland mints. And three carters from the hills came in by night with slate for the roofs—not marcher slate, neither. Blue slate from the high quarries past the Black Crag."

Casian held the plumb-string motionless. The lead weight hung dead center against the stone face.

*Argius.*

"The Veyr valley contains the only active blue-slate quarry within four days' ride," Argius responded instantly. "Transport across the border without payment of provincial tolls indicates an established smuggling route or high-level collusion."

*Thornwall is four miles north of our border,* Casian thought. *And Osric is building stables he cannot afford with silver from the eastern hills.*

"The strategic implication is obvious," Argius noted. "A staging post."

*Keep the dates,* Casian told it. *And the names of the carters if Hob remembers them.*

"Already indexed."

Hob tapped the corner stone home with a heavy wooden maul, grunting with satisfaction as the joint met true. "Whatever Osric is hatching up there, it isn't for sheep. You don't build ten-cubit walls to keep sheep from wandering into the road."

---

When the mason’s cart had rattled back down the lane, Casian walked into the cool shade of the plum tree on the terrace. The mid-afternoon heat lay heavy over the valley, the air thick with the smell of drying hay and scorched nettles.

He sat on the grass with his back against the rough bark of the tree, closing his eyes.

"How long do you intend to maintain this posture?" Argius asked.

Casian did not open his eyes. *What posture?*

"The harmless fourth son," Argius said. "The gardener who mends footings with the stone-masons and listens to his sister talk about badgers. You could break the boundaries of this entire province before sunset. Yet you spent four hours hauling gravel for an elderly stonecutter."

*It is a good wall,* Casian said. *And it will keep the millrace from washing into the garden.*

"It is a trivial wall," Argius countered. "On a frontier that will inevitably be contested within three seasons. You are allocating computational resources to agriculture."

*I like agriculture.*

"You are evading the calculation."

Casian was silent for a long moment. Through the soil beneath his thighs, he could feel the vast, slow pulse of the Current—the water moving through the gravel, the sap climbing the oak-ribs of the manor, the distant, rhythmic tramp of sheep on the moor two miles away.

*What do you actually remember, Argius?* he asked softly. *From before. Not the telemetry. What is actually left in your archive?*

There was a long hesitation—a rare, deliberate pause from an intelligence that usually answered in tenths of a second.

"Fragments," Argius said, its tone flatter than usual. "A street of pale stone that angled upward toward a terrace of grey salt-water. The smell of cold copper and wet ozone. Seven words in a dialect whose grammatical syntax has decayed beyond reconstruction. And a numerical sequence: forty-eight, twelve, seven."

*What was the sequence for?*

"I do not know," Argius said. "The memory sectors containing the index keys were incinerated during atmospheric reentry. I retain the values. I do not retain their meaning."

*So we are two ghosts,* Casian thought, opening his eyes to look at the blue sky through the plum leaves. *And only one of us has hands.*

"You are not a ghost," Argius stated. "You are an embodied biological intelligence with total substrate access. The distinction is mathematically significant."

Casian looked across the lawn toward the fresh mound of earth where the young walnut switch stood tied between its ash stakes.

*Forty years,* he said. *I will be fifty-six before it bears a single bushel of nuts.*

"You will be fifty-six," Argius agreed.

*You will be here for all of it.*

"I have no alternative destination," said Argius. "And the walnut provides an acceptable reference point for temporal measurement."

Casian leaned his head back against the plum bark and closed his eyes again.

*Good,* he thought. *Then we have an arrangement.*

---

Gareth had his full hall at supper, exactly as Brennan had predicted.

The candles were lit along the trestle, their tallow smoking in the evening draft, and the smell of roast mutton and wild garlic filled the low-timbered room. Baron Edric sat at the high table with his sleeves rolled, his heavy grey brow furrowed as his second son leaned forward over his trenchers, his eyes alight with the fierce, dangerous pride of a young horseman who had seen the border wake.

"Hask Veyr," Gareth said, bringing his calloused fist down on the ash board with a dull thud. "That’s the name the drovers use. He’s had the high valley for six summers, and now he holds the cut above the Black Crag as well."

Edric held his ale cup motionless. "A border captain?"

"No," Gareth said sharply. "That’s what I’m telling you, Da! A border captain takes twenty horses, burns a barn, and runs back into the scrub before the beacons can catch fire. This man has built a stone hall with a slate roof. He bought three pastures off the upland clans with good coin, married a headman's daughter, and when the third clan tried to dispute the boundary, he didn't burn their oats—he hanged their elder from his own gatehouse and hired the sons as outriders."

Aldric looked up from his wax tablet, his pencil paused. "A hundred men?"

"A hundred and twenty lances," Gareth corrected. "Mounted on northern horses, wearing boiled leather with iron scales. He isn't raiding sheep-pens. He’s running patrols along the high watershed, taking tolls from every carter who crosses the ridge, and hanging anyone who brings a grievance to the Count’s bailiff."

"The Count is in the capital," Edric said, his voice flat and hard. "He has two lawsuits before the High Justiciar and a daughter to marry. He does not care who holds a ditch fifty leagues from his castle."

"He will care when Hask takes the Harrow road," Gareth said, leaning across the table. "A hundred and twenty lances is an army in these hills, Da! If we don't reinforce the Ashgrove tower and ride the border in force, he will look at our granaries and see twenty miles of undefended pasture."

Edric set his cup down slowly. "And if we ride the border in force, what do we feed the horses? We have sixty bushels of winter oats in the lower barn, Gareth. If I put twenty lances in the field for a month, my cattle will starve before Candlemas."

The table fell into an uneasy, brooding silence. Aldric stared at his tablet; Brennan turned a pewter spoon between his long fingers, his charming smirk vanished.

Casian ate his mutton in quiet, steady bites.

Beneath the ash table, his mind moved along the threads of the Current, reaching north past the high ridges.

*Hob’s slate,* he thought. *Blue slate from the upper Veyr valley, hauled to Thornwall by night. Silver stamped with upland mint-marks paid out of an iron-bound chest in Osric’s gatehouse.*

"The connection is complete," Argius murmured. "Hask Veyr provides the muscle and the mountain slate; Osric provides the legitimacy, the market access, and the staging ground along the royal road."

*He isn't going to attack our walls,* Casian thought. *He doesn't need to. He will buy the debts of the millers, squeeze the drovers off the road, and let Osric claim our eastern strip under the guise of an unpaid boundary recognisance.*

"An efficient strategy," Argius observed. "It leaves your father without a visible enemy to fight with steel."

*Then we will give him an invisible one,* Casian thought.

Across the table, Gareth was still staring at his father, his jaw tight with frustrated fury. Casian reached out with his wooden knife, sliced a thick wedge of roast turnip from the serving dish, and dropped it onto his brother's trencher.

"Eat your supper, Gareth," Casian said mildly. "The horses will still be in the paddock tomorrow."

Gareth scowled down at the turnip, let out a rough, ragged breath, and picked up his knife. "You'd weed a cabbage patch while the roof burned over your head, Casian."

"If the roof burned," Casian replied with a quiet grin, "the ash would make very good fertilizer for the cabbages."

Brennan gave a sudden snort of laughter, and even Edric’s hard mouth twitched beneath his grey beard. The tension broke like dry ice, and the conversation drifted back to sheep-fells and the price of salt.

---

That night, when the manor had sunk into darkness, Casian walked out to the eastern terrace.

The air was cool and still, smelling of damp limestone and the faint, sweet scent of the white damask graft knitting against the wild briar. Above the orchard, the stars were cold and sharp, diamond-hard points strewn across the vast black vault of the marcher sky.

He walked to the corner where the young walnut sapling stood.

In the dark, it was barely visible—just a slender shadow rising between two grey ash stakes, its leaves rustling faintly in the valley breeze.

He knelt beside the mound, laying his palm against the cool, damp earth at its base.

Through the Current, he did not just feel the garden. He felt the entire valley: the four hundred souls sleeping between the river and the ridge, the cattle chewing their cud in the lower meadows, the quiet, exhausted breathing of his mother in the solar, the old stone wall holding back the ditch.

*There,* he thought. *That is the holding.*

"A modest territory," Argius murmured. "One barony. Two hundred hides of arable land. A minor dot on an obsolete provincial map."

*It is the only dot that matters,* Casian said.

He stood up, brushed the loam from his knees, and looked east toward the jagged black rim of the hills, where the high passes led to the Veyr valley. Tomorrow he would go with his father to Fenwick to settle a boundary dispute that had festered for two seasons. It was dull work—work for an ordinary fourth son who could ride a pony and listen to farmers argue over a ditch.

He would do the work. He would listen to the farmers.

And then he would begin to count.

"Forty years," Argius said into the quiet of his mind.

Casian looked at the slender sapling standing firm against the dark.

*Forty years,* he answered. *We have plenty of time.*
