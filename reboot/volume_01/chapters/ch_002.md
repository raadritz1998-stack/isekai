The graft took four days to show, which was three days faster than it had any business doing, and Casian knew better than to say so out loud.

He had done it on the seventh of August, in the cool part of the morning, with a knife his grandfather had owned and a strip of linen cut from a shirt that had gone past saving. The old rootstock by the east wall was the hardiest thing in the garden and had survived two bad winters and one worse gardener. The scion was from a rose that no longer existed anywhere in the world except as a cutting in a jar by the kitchen window — a rose from a place he had never seen with these eyes and would never see again, and which he had described once to Argius in a voice so flat that Argius had declined to comment on it.

He had not told anyone what it was. There was nobody to tell. A man who says *this is a rose from my other life* in the eastern marches at the end of a long summer is a man who has just handed somebody a rope.

So it was just a rose. It was a rose with small, tight, dark-red flowers and a scent that came and went, and when his mother had asked where the cutting came from he had said, "Off a hedge down by the Harrow road," and she had said, "Hm," in the way she said it when she was filing something away.

On the eleventh he went out before the sun was over the wall and found the bud swollen and the linen still tight, and stood there with the empty watering can in his hand for longer than the job required.

*It took,* he thought.

"It took," said Argius. "You are standing in a garden looking at a stick."

*It's not a stick. It's a rose that doesn't exist any more anywhere except here.*

There was a pause of the kind Argius used when it was deciding whether to be kind.

"That is a large thing to put in a stick," it said.

---

He did the watering, which was a performance he maintained for the household's benefit and his own — the east wall's water came by a gutter and a cistern he had built two years ago, and the whole arrangement was a good deal more elegant than it looked, and the elegance was the part nobody in the family had ever been told about. If he had simply said *the garden waters itself now* his father would have asked how, and Casian would have had to invent a carpenter.

The cistern was fed off the roof of the glasshouse and ran along the top of the east wall in a channel of split pine, and at four points there were little gates with leather hinges that a person could open with one finger. It had taken him a month to work out and it had cost almost nothing, and it meant that an old woman and a girl of twelve could water fourteen beds in the time it took to walk the length of them.

He opened the gates in order. The water went where it was supposed to go.

*You could have done this in an afternoon,* he thought.

"I could have done it in an afternoon," said Argius. "It would have taken a year for your father to stop being afraid of it. You did it correctly."

*I did it slowly.*

"You did it in a way that looks like luck. That is not the same as slowly."

Behind him, in the kitchen doorway, Maren Vale was standing with her sleeves pushed up and a bowl against her hip, watching him open the last gate.

He had not heard her come out. He almost never did — she moved like a woman who had spent twenty-six years carrying things to people who did not notice her arriving.

"Morning," he said.

"Mm." She was looking at the channel along the wall, and the little leather gate swinging shut on its hinge, and the water going down the bed in a thin sheet rather than a flood. She had a particular expression when she was looking at something she had already decided not to ask about: a small, flat, entirely neutral interest, like a woman looking at weather she would have to live with.

"Breakfast," she said, and went back inside.

That was the whole of it. That was always the whole of it.

*She knows something,* he thought, and it was not the first time, and it was not the first time he had decided to leave it alone.

"You are fortunate," said Argius.

*I know.*

---

Breakfast at Vale was not a meal so much as an hour in which people were permitted to be in the same room while doing nine other things.

Maren had command of one end of the table and a manner of dividing things out that had never been explained and was perfectly understood: whoever came in got bread, and if they wanted something else they had to say why. Jory came in with his hat in his hands to report that the east wall's corner stone was cut and ready and the mason wanted to be paid before the wet and not after, and was given bread and told to say so to the baron. Brennan came in late, cheerful, and covered in dust from the Harrowmere road, and was given bread and an opinion about his hair.

"He's a good boy," said Maren, when Brennan had gone up to wash, in a tone that suggested she was continuing an argument with somebody who was not in the room.

"He is."

"Your father thinks he's idle."

"Brennan's never been idle in his life. He just won't do anything that isn't his idea." Casian tore his bread. "It's not the same, and Da knows it, and it annoys him more than idleness would."

His mother looked at him for a moment with the flat neutral interest she used for the water channel.

"You've got four brothers and you can do all four of their work," she said. "You've just decided not to."

"It's a lot of work, being a brother."

"Mm," said Maren, and passed him the salt, and did not ask.

He was thirty-one years from her grave and he would remember that morning all of that time, though he did not know it then: his mother's elbows on the table, the salt, the bread, the dust on the floor that Brennan had brought in and would not sweep up, the ordinary weight of a room where nothing at all was happening.

*She's the only one who could ever have found me out,* he thought. *She could have found me out a hundred times. She decided not to, and she has never once said so, and she will never say so, and I don't know what to do with that.*

"You are not going to say anything."

*No.*

"Why not?"

*Because she took off her shoes once in twenty-six years and she did it to me, and I'd repay her by putting a thing on her she can't put down.*

"That is a better answer than most of yours," said Argius.

*I have many good answers.*

"You have many answers."

---

The mason came at noon with a cart and his nephew and the corner stone, and the job took four hours because Casian had already cut the footing before the wet last year and had told nobody.

He worked alongside them, which was not usual for a baron's son and was not remarked upon either, because he had done it since he was old enough to lift a thing and people had long ago stopped finding it strange. That was the advantage of starting early and never stopping: a boy who works with the men is invisible in a way a boy who watches them never is.

The mason's name was Hob. He was fifty and had built half the walls in the eastern marches and he talked the whole four hours about a job he had done at Thornwall the summer before.

"Fine stone," he said, "and bad pay. The steward there is a man who has to be asked twice for everything and the first answer is always no. They're building on the north side. New range, new stables, a good deal of it. I asked who was paying and he said the baron was and I said the baron was not, because I know what the baron of Thornwall has and it is not that."

"Rich harvest?"

"Rich harvest my eye," said Hob. "It's a hired man's work and there's nobody hired. It's the cheapest stone in three counties and they wanted it cheaper." He tapped a course level with the flat of the trowel. "There's money moving in that town that isn't coming out of that town. That's all I know and I know it because I've been paid out of a purse that was too full."

Casian held the level while Hob set the last course.

*Argius.*

"He is right," said Argius. "He is right and he does not know why, which is the most reliable kind of informant."

*Write down the date he was there.*

"Already done."

---

In the afternoon heat he worked in the shade of the plum and Argius, who did not need to be anywhere and never had, asked him a question it had not asked before.

"How long do you intend to be here?"

Casian sat back on his heels.

*Here?*

"In this house. In this county. Waking up in a bed in a barony nobody has heard of, making a wall before the wet, being the fourth son." A pause. "You have not thought about it in some time. I am asking whether that is a decision or a habit."

He looked at the garden. He had built most of it. The glasshouse, the channel, the fourteen beds — the whole thing had been made by him slowly, in a way that looked like luck, over sixteen years, in front of people who had watched him grow and had seen nothing.

*Both,* he said. *It's the best life I've ever had and I'm not finished having it.*

"The other one was not finished either."

Casian said nothing for a while. The plum made its noise in the wind. Down in the yard somebody hit a bucket with something.

*What do you actually have?* he asked. *Not the shape of it. What's actually still there.*

Argius took its time. That was rare.

"Fragments. A street that sloped. The smell of a thing I cannot name. Seven or eight words with no grammar attached to them, which I can say but cannot fit together. A number." Another pause. "The number is important and I no longer know what it was for. I am aware that this is a poor archive and I am aware that you are asking a question I cannot answer and would like me to."

*I'd like you to answer it.*

"I would too," said Argius.

And there it was, laid out on a warm afternoon over nothing at all: two exiles, and only one of them could say the word *street* in a language that had no other living speaker, and both of them had already understood that it was going to disappear twice.

*The walnut,* said Casian, after a while. *Forty years. I'll be fifty-six before it does anything.*

"At which point it will be a tree and you will be an old man with a tree."

*Yes.*

"That is the plan."

*That's the plan.*

"You will be here for all of it."

Casian brushed the soil off his hands.

*Yes,* he said. *I will.*

He said it the way a man says a thing he has already decided and has not yet made peace with, and Argius, who had been alive in one form or another for a great deal longer than the walnut would ever be, did not say anything else about it for the rest of the evening.

---

---

Gareth came back off the east patrol on the thirteenth with four men and a story, and told it at supper because he was twenty-one and had never in his life kept a good story for a better moment.

They had gone out to the Ashgrove road and up onto the watershed and had found nothing, which was normal. But at the second night's camp a drover had come in off the hill and had sat with them and had talked, and the talk had run on to the eastern hills and the men who held them.

"Hask Veyr," said Gareth. "That's the name. He's had the upper Veyr valley for six years and the last two he's had the next one as well."

"Had it how?" said Edric.

"Bought it, then married into it, then killed the man who complained." Gareth reached for the bread. "He's not a raider. That's the thing. A raider takes what's there and goes. This man's been paying for land with coin and keeping the men who sold it, and he's got a hundred and something under him and he's got a hall with a slate roof."

"A hundred men is a barony," said Aldric.

"A hundred men is a barony," Gareth agreed, "and he hasn't got one, and nobody has said a word about it because the upper valley is Harven's and Harven is a hundred miles from the upper valley and has never once been there."

Edric turned his cup round.

"And when a man has a hundred men and no title," he said, "what does he do with them?"

"Nothing, if he's sensible."

"And if he's not?"

Gareth chewed. "Then he finds a road worth taking and a house worth taking and he takes it, and afterward everyone says the marches are a hard country and always were."

There was a silence at the table of the kind that happens when a family has been told the shape of a thing it would rather not look at.

Casian ate his bread and said nothing, and thought about four riders going west, not stopping, and about a steward in Thornwall on a horse bred in the eastern hills, and about the fact that a man with a hundred men and no title has to keep feeding them.

*He's not going to raid my father's land,* he thought. *Not directly. He's going to buy a friend first.*

"He will buy a friend," said Argius, quietly, "and the friend will be your father's neighbour."

*Say it out loud and I'll laugh.*

"Do not laugh. Your father is watching."

Casian did not laugh. He passed the salt to his mother, and asked Gareth about the drover's dog, and the table went back to the shape it had been in before.

---

That evening Elna sat on the wall and asked him whether it was true that their father had been a soldier, which he had not, and then whether it was true that the eastern hills were full of men who ate people, which was not true either but had been true of some men somewhere, and then, third, with the directness that made her the best company in the house, whether he intended to stay.

"Do you mean tonight?"

"You know what I mean."

"Where else would I go?"

"Brennan says you could go to the capital and be a secretary to somebody and do very well," she said, "and that you won't because you're lazy."

"Brennan is the cleverest man in this county and half of what he says is furniture."

"Are you lazy, though?"

"Not in the ways that count."

"Which ways count?"

Casian considered the question, which was a good one, and considered his sister, who was twelve and had already, without being taught, learned the trick of asking the second question instead of the first.

"The ways where you have to be somewhere," he said.

The answer satisfied her, or at least occupied her, and she stayed on the wall until the light went and told him about a badger, and he listened to the whole of it as though there had never been any other world.

---

After supper, with the house going quiet, he walked out along the east wall and stood where the graft was, in the dark, where he could not have seen it even if there had been anything to see.

*One rose from a country with no name,* he said. *That's all that's left of it and it's four feet tall and it's in a walled garden in the back of beyond.*

"That is not what is left of it," said Argius. "What is left of it is standing in the garden talking to itself."

"That is not all that is left of it," said Argius.

*I didn't mean — *

"I know what you meant." A pause. "It was not a bad line. It was inaccurate."

Casian laughed, once, quietly, in a garden at night, with a house full of people behind him who thought they knew what he was.

Tomorrow he would go to Fenwick, because there was a letter on his father's desk about a boundary stone and a man nobody could reason with, and because his father would read the letter out at supper and get nowhere with it, and because Casian had been looking at the eastern marches for sixteen years and had recently begun to feel that the looking should start being something.

---

He was halfway back to the house when he stopped.

There was a thing he did, some nights, that had no name and that he would not have described to anyone. He stood still and let the country come in.

It was not looking. Looking was a thing you did with eyes and eyes were for the top inch of the world. This was more like standing in a river and knowing the river — the whole of it at once, from the gravel to the far bank, without turning your head. The Vale fields. The road. The mill race going down to the wheel. The twelve sleeping people in the house behind him and the four who were not sleeping, and the dogs, and the horses in the yard with their heads down.

He never got tired of it and he never got used to it, which he thought was probably a good sign, and which he could not have explained to anyone if his life had depended on it, so he had never tried.

*There,* he thought. *That's the shape of it. From the ridge to the mill, and nothing in between that isn't mine to look after.*

"There is a great deal between the ridge and the mill," said Argius.

*I know.*

"You do not know. You have never once counted it." A pause. "You have the whole of the eastern marches in your hands and you have spent sixteen years looking at fourteen beds. This is not a criticism. It is a description of a man who has been well fed and has not yet looked up."

Casian stood in the dark for a moment longer than the night required.

*What would I count?*

"People. A hundred and forty in this barony and four thousand in the hundred square miles around it, and every one of them with a thing they cannot do alone." Argius's voice was very flat, the way it was when it was being careful not to instruct. "You have never needed any of them. That is the whole advantage of you and it is also the whole difficulty of you."

*And if I did need them?*

"Then you would have made a beginning," said Argius, "instead of a garden."

Casian walked back down the hill, and the house had two lights in it still, and somewhere behind the wall a dog decided he was a stranger for exactly one bark and then remembered.

*Tomorrow,* he thought, *I'll start counting.*
