Fenwick was eleven miles east and slightly north, and it was technically a barony and practically a hill with a fort on it and a great deal of sheep.

The Vales had been riding over to Fenwick for four generations, in the way that neighbouring houses do: for funerals, for the loan of a wagon, for the settling of things that had got out of hand between tenants. It was a small, cold, well-kept place. Baroness Calla had run it for six years, since her husband had died of a fever he had caught doing exactly what he was supposed to be doing, and in that time the Fenwick rents had gone up two years running and the mill had been repaired twice, which told anyone who knew how to read such things that she was better at this than most men in the county.

Casian rode over with the letter in his coat and a bag of cuttings he had no intention of planting, because a man who arrives on an errand and stays for an afternoon needs a reason to be in a garden.

The errand was a stone.

It was one of the old ones — an unworked pillar about waist high, standing at the corner of the Fenwick lower field where it met the Common, in a place with no fence and no obvious reason for anything to be. Two men had been arguing about it since spring. On one side was Calla's tenant, a farmer named Reed, who held the lower field and wanted the stone to mean the boundary of it. On the other was a man from the Common side named Tolsey, who had held the grazing for thirty years and wanted the stone to mean something else.

The trouble was that the stone had moved.

It was eleven paces from where everyone remembered it. Reed said it had always been eleven paces further east. Tolsey said it had been where it was. Two men who had grown up within a mile of each other and had walked past the thing ten thousand times could not agree on a fact, and each of them was entirely honest, and neither of them was lying, and that was the whole of the problem.

---

He had come the long way, up over the shoulder and down through the birches, because the road from Vale to Fenwick ran for four miles alongside the Common and there was nothing to see on it that he had not seen two hundred times.

The long way had three things on it. A waystation where the drovers watered, which was half a roof and a stone trough and no wall at all. A stand of old thorn where a man had been hanged for sheep-stealing in his grandfather's time and where nobody tied a horse. And, at the top of the shoulder, the whole of the eastern marches laid out below like a table somebody had set and then walked away from: the Vale fields small and tidy, the Harrowmere cut going north, the Common rolling off east, and beyond all of it the hills that were not gentle.

He stopped there, because he always stopped there, and looked at it for a while.

*Four riders went west on that road three days ago,* he thought. *They stopped somewhere, and nobody stopped them, and nobody will.*

"Someone will," said Argius.

*Who?*

"Nobody, this year," said Argius. "You are describing the problem correctly. I am agreeing with you."

Casian turned his horse and went down into Fenwick.

Calla met him in the yard. She was thirty and dressed for the weather and did not pretend to be pleased to see a boy of sixteen sent in place of the baron.

"Your father wrote that he would come himself."

"He's at Harrowmere about the cut. He'll come Thursday if you'd rather wait."

"I'd rather have it done." She looked at him. "He sent you."

"He sent the letter, and I read it, and I came." Casian swung down. "I'm good with stones."

That got him a look which he filed away: not contempt, exactly. Assessment. A woman who had learned to be careful about men who were charming, because charming men had cost her the last six years.

They walked the boundary together with the two tenants and a boy to hold the horses, and for the first hour Casian did what he did at every gathering, which was to be pleasant, and stupid in the right places, and to ask the questions a boy asks so that men answer them at length and think they are being kind.

Reed was frightened. That was the first and most useful thing. He was not angry about the stone, or not only; he was a man with one field and a bad year and a stone that had moved without him moving it, and he had been waking up at night about it. Tolsey was not frightened but he was something adjacent to frightened, which was stubborn, which is what a man becomes when he has been told he is being unreasonable by everyone for six months.

Somewhere around the second hour, standing in the long grass and not listening to either of them, Casian put his hand on the pillar.

He had not needed to touch it. He had known where it belonged from the yard, the way a man knows the height of his own door. But there was a thing he had found out about himself at nine and had never quite stopped doing: he liked to touch the ground where he was going to work, the way a carpenter runs a thumb along a board before he cuts it. It made no difference to a single thing. It was simply his.

Under his hand the grass moved in the wind and the wind was the wind.

*Well,* he thought. *Let's have it properly.*

He stood there with two men arguing behind him and let the whole of it come in. Not the way a man looks at a field and sees a field — the way a man opens a ledger and sees every transaction that ever went through it at once. The fall of the ground, the old ruts under the turf, the drainage, the place where the drove road had been before the Common was fenced, the winter it froze, the tree that had stood at the corner and had been cut for a mast forty years ago and had left a root under the grass that was still there and was still doing something.

It was not a vision and it was not a trance. It was quiet. The men behind him were still arguing about the hedge. A curlew went up off the Common and turned twice and went down again.

And somewhere in all of it, in a corner, in the way that a man notices a burnt patch in a ledger he has read a hundred times, was the thing that had moved the stone.

*Argius.*

"There is a fall in the ground under it," said Argius. "The old line is eleven paces east. Your Reed is correct to within a foot."

*And the stone?*

"The stone has been lifted and set down. It did not walk. It is very heavy and it did not walk."

*How long ago?*

"Two seasons. Perhaps three. The grass has taken it."

Casian took his hand off the pillar and looked at the two men, who were now arguing about a hedge that had also, at some point, moved.

*Who moved it?*

"That," said Argius, "is not a question the ground answers."

---

The thing about the stone was that it was a Keeping stone, and the Keeping was the one thing in the eastern marches that everybody did and nobody discussed.

Every village in the east kept something. Vale kept the walled garden and, at its eastern gate, a flat stone with a hollow worn in it that had been there since before the house. Fenwick kept this pillar and two others like it, and the custom on the Fenwick side was that a stone was not to be moved by any man, for any reason, and that if a stone was moved then the person who moved it should put it back and say nothing about it, and nobody in living memory had ever had to test what happened if they did not.

Nobody could say why. It was not religion, exactly. The Ash had missionaries who came through twice a year and they had long ago given up on the Keeping, and the priests said privately that it was older than anything they had a word for and publicly that it was harmless foolishness that no honest man need trouble himself about.

It was simply what people here had always done, the way people elsewhere painted their doors a particular blue.

And eleven paces is a long way to carry a stone that heavy.

---

The other thing about the Keeping was the fear, and nobody in the eastern marches had ever once spoken about it in a plain way, and Casian had only twice in his life watched it happen.

Once, when he was eleven, a man had taken the flat stone from the east gate of the Vale garden to level a doorstep, and had been persuaded to put it back before the week was out by three separate neighbours who each came on their own and each of whom would have sworn afterwards that they had simply happened by. Casian's father had not been one of the three. His father had said, when he heard of it, "Good, that's done," and had gone back to the rent rolls, because a man of forty-nine with four sons and a border does not spend his evenings thinking about a stone.

The other was a boy on the Harrowmere road who had broken a Keeping post for firewood in a hard frost, and who had come back a year later, well and cheerful, and had been asked about the frost and had said he did not remember it. Which was not, on its own, an interesting fact. The interesting fact was that everyone who heard him say it went quiet for a moment, and then changed the subject, and did not go back to it.

Nobody talked about it because there was nothing to say that would survive being said out loud. It was the sort of thing that had been going on in the marches for so long that the marches had grown a habit of not looking at it directly, the way a man with a bad shoulder does not swing it.

And whatever had moved the Fenwick stone had done it in the dark, in the grass, in two seasons, and had not been afraid.

That, more than the eleven paces, was what bothered Casian.

---

By afternoon Casian had settled it, and he had settled it without any of the party understanding that a settlement had occurred.

He did it in the field, in the sun, with the two men and the baroness standing over him, and he did it by asking Reed a question about his father, and Reed's father a question about the winter the river froze, and Tolsey a question about where the old drove road had come before the Common was fenced, and then standing in a particular place and saying, "There, then," as though the matter had always been obvious and they had simply been standing on the wrong bit of grass.

The stone went back eleven paces. Nobody had to be told it was a compromise because nobody had been asked to give anything up. Tolsey got his grazing and Reed got a boundary that ran where it had always run, and both of them walked away believing they had won, which is the only kind of peace that lasts.

Calla watched him do it and said nothing at all until the tenants had gone.

"You knew where it belonged before you got off the horse," she said.

"I'm good with stones."

"You asked Tolsey about the drove road and he told you where the Common gate used to be, and then you walked to the place a man would put a gate, and then you were certain." She was not smiling. "You could have said all of that at the start and saved two hours."

"Then Reed would have had to be wrong in front of Tolsey."

"He was wrong."

"He was wrong about a stone and right about the land." Casian shrugged. "There's no way to give a man one of those in front of the other one and keep him. He'll remember today as the day he was right about the boundary."

There was a pause.

"You're sixteen," said Calla.

"So they tell me."

"Don't." She held up a hand. "Don't be charming at me, I'm not in the mood for it and it's the least interesting thing about you."

Which was, he thought later, the first time anyone in the eastern marches had said a true thing to him in about four years, and it was the reason he did what he did next.

---

He walked the wall with her at dusk, because she asked him to, in the tone of a woman who had decided to be direct and was braced for a boy to be embarrassed about it.

Fenwick's wall was older than Vale's and lower, and the light went along it in a way that made the whole hill look like a thing that had been shaped by hands and then left alone for a very long time.

"Do you know what the stones are?" she said.

"No."

"Nobody does. My husband thought they were markers for a road that's gone. My grandmother thought they kept the hills in their places and she was not a foolish woman and she was not joking." Calla looked out at the dark. "In the spring, there was a man who turned his team at the Fenwick stone because his oxen would not go past it. Would not go past. Five men and a whip and they would not. He walked them round the long way and lost a day and never complained about it and has not brought a team up that road since."

"And you think the stone did that."

"I think the oxen did that. I don't know why." She turned to face him. "What I know is that a boundary stone moved eleven paces in two seasons, and nobody saw it, and the two men nearest it are frightened, and the man who is most frightened is frightened of something he won't say out loud, which is not the kind of fear a farmer gets about a boundary."

Casian looked at the pillar, standing where it belonged now.

*Argius.*

"She is estimating," said Argius. "She is doing it correctly, from insufficient information, and she has arrived at approximately the right conclusion for approximately the wrong reasons. She believes there is a person. There is. There is you, and you did not move the stone."

*Someone did.*

"Yes."

*Find out.*

"Later," said Argius. "Not tonight. Tonight you have a bag of cuttings and a woman who has decided to take you seriously, and the second of those is rarer."

---

He gave her the cuttings, which was the reason he had brought them, and she took them with a look that said she had known there would be an angle.

"They're from the old rootstock at Vale. They'll take anywhere and they won't die," he said. "The east side of your wall gets the light. Put them in and forget them and in three years you'll have something worth looking at."

"Why?"

"Because you asked me a straight question about a stone and did not once ask me whether I was old enough to answer it."

Calla turned a cutting over in her fingers.

"You're a strange boy," she said. "I don't mean that as a compliment and I don't mean it as an insult. I mean that I have been running a barony for six years and I can usually tell within a morning whether a man is worth the trouble of knowing, and with you I cannot tell at all, and it is going to bother me."

"I'd rather that than the other thing."

"What's the other thing?"

"Being worth the trouble," said Casian. "It's the first step to being trouble."

---

---

He told his father about it the next morning, in the study, standing, because the study was a standing sort of room.

Edric listened to the whole of it without interrupting, which was one of the things that made him a good lord. He had a habit of putting his fingers together and looking at the middle distance while a man talked, and it meant exactly what it looked like: he was hearing you and deciding what to do, in that order.

"Reed and Tolsey both content," he said at the end.

"Both think they won."

"That'll hold for a year and then one of them will remember it differently." Edric turned a page over. "It was well done. You might have told me you were going."

"You'd have come."

"I would have come."

"And it would have taken a week and Reed would have spent the week being certain that whatever was decided would be decided against him, because a baron coming to your field means a baron is going to decide something." Casian put his hands behind his back. "It's settled. It's settled the way it needed to be settled and if I'd done it your way it would have been settled the other way."

His father looked at him for a while.

"That is either insolence or the truest thing anyone has said to me this year," he said, "and I am too old to enjoy the ambiguity."

"Sorry."

"You're not."

"No," Casian agreed. "I'm not."

Edric made a sound that was almost the laugh he almost never gave, and then he picked up the letter from Fenwick and read it again, and his face went back to being grey.

"There's a thing in it about the stone," he said.

"The stone had been moved. I put it back."

"Yes." Edric set the letter down. "And when a stone is moved in this county, things are said. I've heard it all my life. I heard it from my father and he heard it from his, and none of us have ever once seen a thing that would stand up in front of a magistrate." He rubbed his eyes. "It's the sort of thing a man is tempted to think about on long evenings and it has never once put bread on this table. Do you understand me?"

"I think so."

"Say it back."

"You want me to look at the road and the rent and the walls," said Casian, "and not at the stones."

"I want you to grow up in a country that isn't frightened of its own fields," said his father. "That's the whole of my ambition and I've had thirty years to do it and I've managed about a mile of it."

He went back to his papers. The conversation was over in the way conversations were always over with Edric — not dismissed, but concluded.

Casian stood in the doorway a moment longer.

*He's wrong,* said Argius.

*He's not wrong. He's just decided which questions he can afford.*

"That is not the same as not being wrong."

*No,* Casian thought. *It isn't.*

---

He rode home in the dark with the letter unanswered in his coat and the horse picking its own way.

*Someone moved it and nobody saw,* he thought. *That's three seasons. That's before all of this.*

"It may be nothing," said Argius. "There are men who move stones. They are called thieves and they are usually poor."

*In the middle of the night, without oxen, eleven paces, and the grass grown over it and the man who owns the field not knowing?*

"Then it is not nothing."

*No.*

They went on for a while. The road was pale. Somewhere off to the left the Common rolled away into the dark, and out beyond that were the hills that were Hask's country, and Casian found that he was thinking about them in a way he had not thought about anything before: not as a place he had been told about, but as a thing he would eventually have to know.

*I want the whole east,* he thought, and it surprised him, because he had never put it that plainly even to himself. *Not to rule. To know. So that when a stone moves in the night I am the one who knows why.*

"That is a large appetite," said Argius.

*Yes.*

"It will not stay that size."

Casian rode on.

*No,* he said. *I don't suppose it will.*
