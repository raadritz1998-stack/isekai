Fenwick lay eleven miles north-east across the Common, perched on a chalk spur above the sheep-runs: a stout timber keep behind a low curtain of dry-stacked limestone, surrounded by thirty tenant cottages and three thousand sheep.

The Vales had ridden the path between the two holdings for four generations. They shared funerals, the loan of heavy ditching wains, and the routine disputes that arose whenever marcher cattle strayed across unhedged heather. Baroness Calla had held the manor for six years, ever since her husband had died of an autumn lung-rot caught while clearing a blocked mill-dam. In those six years, the Fenwick rent-chests had grown heavier, the stone granaries had gained fresh slate roofs, and the tenant arrears had dropped by half. She was thirty years of age, sharp-tongued, and widely recognized by the bailiffs of the eastern marches as a woman who could spot four clipped pennies in a bag of fifty silver shillings.

Casian rode out through the Vale postern before the mist had cleared from the rye. He carried his father’s sealed letter in his tunic and a damp hemp sack of white damask rose cuttings tied behind his saddle cantle.

The errand was a boundary stone.

It stood at the eastern hem of the Fenwick lower meadow, where the arable land gave way to the rough gorse of the Common: an unworked pillar of grey gritstone, waist-high and moss-bitten, with neither carving nor tool-mark to show who had set it in the peat. For three months, two men had been close to blood over it. On the Fenwick side was Calla’s tenant, an anxious, thin-faced farmer named Reed, who held forty acres of oats and claimed the stone marked his ditch line. On the Common side was an upland grazier named Tolsey, who had run sheep over the heather for thirty seasons and claimed the turf belonged to the parish drove.

The difficulty was that the stone was not where it belonged.

It stood eleven paces west of the old sheep-ditch. Reed swore by the bones of his grandfather that the pillar had always stood eleven paces further east. Tolsey swore by his mother’s grave that it had stood exactly where it was since the year of the Great Frost. Both men were sober, both were honest churchgoers, and neither was lying.

That was the heart of the matter. Stones in the marcher clay do not walk on their own legs.

---

Casian took the high track through the birch scrub rather than the highway, letting his dun pony pick its way over the chalk flint.

From the crest of the watershed, the eastern border lay spread beneath the pale morning sun like an open map. To the south, the Vale chimneys sent up their thin blue coils of peat-smoke; to the north, the Harrowmere road ran like a pale ribbon between the marshes; and beyond the Common rose the dark, serrated ridges of the high valleys where the hill clans grazed their horses.

Casian halted the pony on the ridge, resting his hands on the wooden pommel.

*The stone moved,* he thought. *And nobody saw the oxen that hauled it.*

"The weight of a gritstone pillar of that volume is approximately four hundred pounds," Argius remarked in his mind. "A displacement of eleven paces across rough peat requires two draft animals and an iron-shod sledge, or four adult laborers equipped with timber levers."

*Yet Reed swears he heard nothing, and his cottage is three hundred paces away.*

"The local population attributes the event to the Keeping," Argius said. "A convenient sociological category for unexplainable physical phenomena."

*It is not convenient for Reed,* Casian thought, nudging the pony down the slope. *He has three daughters and ten acres of oats that are about to be trampled by Tolsey’s wethers.*

Calla met him in the cobbled yard at Fenwick, standing beside the water-trough with her woolen skirts pinned up from the muck and a leather tally-book tucked beneath her arm.

"Your father’s letter said he would ride over himself," she said, squinting up at him against the glare.

"Da is at the Harrowmere cut," Casian said, swinging down from the saddle. "The bailiff wanted three days on the timber tolls. He said he would come Thursday if you would rather wait."

"Thursday the oats will be ripe and Tolsey’s sheep will be in the middle of them," Calla said briskly. She looked him up and down, noting his plain linen tunic, the dirt on his cuffs, and the easy, unhurried posture of a fourth son sent on an elder brother's errand. "You are sixteen."

"I am sixteen," Casian agreed. "And I brought cuttings from the Vale terrace."

Calla’s mouth tightened, though whether from irritation or reluctant amusement was impossible to tell. "Do not be charming at me, boy. I have three bailiffs, two stewards, and forty tenants who try to be charming whenever the rent is four days late. It is the least interesting thing about you. Did your father tell you how to settle this?"

"He told me to listen to both of them, measure the ditch, and keep the lances in their scabbards."

"A very sensible command," she muttered, turning toward the paddock. "Come along, then. They have been glaring at each other over the hedge since daybreak."

---

The boundary stood in a hollow between two low chalk ridges, where the sweet green turf of the pasture met the brown wire-grass of the Common.

Reed was waiting beside his ditch, wearing a frieze coat with patched elbows, twisting a greasy wool cap between his knobby, dirt-stained hands. Tolsey sat twenty paces away on an overturned ale-keg, chewing on a dried clover stem, flanked by two burly sons armed with hazel quarterstaffs. The stone stood between them in the heather, sunken four inches into the peat, looking ancient, indifferent, and entirely immovable.

"He moved it," Reed burst out the moment Calla stepped onto the turf, his voice trembling with the thin, brittle fury of a man on the edge of ruined crops. "He brought a team up from the drove while the moon was dark, Lady Calla! The stone stood by the dead thorn when my father broke this ground! Eleven paces into my rye, and now he wants to run his ewes over my headland!"

"I moved nothing," Tolsey growled, rising from the keg. He was a broad, barrel-chested man with grey stubble covering his jaw like iron filings. "I've grazed these slopes since the old Baron was a stripling. The stone has stood in that hollow since before your father had breeches to his name. If your rye has crawled toward my sheep, you can plow it back where it belongs!"

"Quiet, both of you," Calla said, her voice cutting through the wind like a shear-blade. "Master Vale is here representing his father. We will hear what he says."

Both men turned to look at Casian. Their faces fell in unison: the bitter disappointment of two grown marchers who had expected a baron in mail and were handed an agreeable lad in turnshoes.

Casian did not apologize. He nodded pleasantly to both men, tied his pony to a gorse bush, and walked over to the pillar.

He laid his bare palm against the weathered gritstone.

The Current moved beneath his fingers.

It was not a vision, and it held no scent of incense or sorcery. It was simply the deep, silent memory of the earth laid bare to someone who held its substrate. He felt the layers of the ground: the topsoil packed with heather roots, the grey gravel washed down by ancient floods, the rotting stump of an ash tree that had fallen forty summers ago, and the old sheep-ruts buried beneath eight inches of turf.

And beneath the stone, the truth was as clear as a line drawn on vellum.

*Argius,* he thought.

"Confirmed," Argius responded. "The subsoil beneath the current position exhibits recent mechanical compaction. Turf integrity was disrupted within the past twenty to twenty-four months. The original footing lies eleven paces east-north-east, where a depression in the gravel stratum matches the base profile of this monolith."

*Was it hauled?*

"Negative. No furrowing in the intermediate peat. No drag-marks. No sled runners. The stone was lifted vertically, displaced laterally through the air, and deposited in its present orientation."

Casian kept his hand on the cold stone for two heartbeats longer.

*A Current-user,* he thought. *Or something older than the barony.*

"The displacement exhibits minor structural resonance," Argius noted. "Consistent with a localized kinetic release. Not natural erosion. Not livestock."

Casian took his hand away and turned back to the men. His face was entirely bland.

"The wind is cold off the Common," he said mildly. "Master Tolsey, when did you last clear the sheep-wash in the bottom?"

Tolsey blinked, thrown off his stride. "The sheep-wash? Five summers back, when the alder fell across the weir."

"And where did your wethers water before the alder fell?"

"Along the low gully," the grazier answered, gesturing with his clover stem. "Past the dead thorn."

Casian turned to Reed. "Your father plowed this lower strip forty years back. Where did he turn his team when he reached the end of the furrow?"

"By the hawthorn," Reed said, frowning with suspicion. "He always turned by the hawthorn. He left six yards of headland so the ditch wouldn't cave under the coulter."

Casian walked six paces past the stone, stopped in the heather, and tapped the turf with the toe of his boot.

"The hawthorn was grubbed out twenty years ago," Casian said, looking from one man to the other. "And the ditch was recut three yards wide after the flood. But the gravel seam under this grass runs straight from the weir to the dead ash on the knoll. Look at the sheep-path, Master Tolsey. The grass grows darker along the seam because the water drains faster. If your ewes cross the seam, they get the foot-rot in the bog."

Tolsey stared at the strip of darker heather. "Aye... the fluke gets them if they feed in the hollow."

"And if Reed plows past the gravel seam," Casian continued, looking at the farmer, "his oxen will sink to their hocks in the clay, and he'll spend half his harvest pulling his wains out of the mire."

He walked five paces back toward the boundary stone, grasped the rough gritstone with both hands, and set his shoulder against it.

Beneath the rough homespun of his sleeves, the Current flared—silent, invisible, and absolute.

To Reed and Tolsey, it appeared that the boy gave a tremendous heave of his shoulders, his face red with exertion, straining against four hundred pounds of solid stone. In reality, Casian merely provided the outward theater; the Current reached into the peat, loosened the compacted clay, lifted the pillar an inch above the subsoil, and set it eleven paces back into its ancient, rightful hollow.

The stone settled into the earth with a heavy, muffled thud that sent a puff of dry lichen-dust into the breeze.

Casian stepped back, wiping his brow with the back of his forearm and breathing with convincing roughness.

"There," he said, dusting off his palms. "The stone stands on the gravel seam where it stood when the hawthorn was green. Reed keeps his forty paces of oats. Tolsey keeps his dry grazing for the ewes. And neither of you has to buy a round of ale for the Baron's bailiff to write it in a parchment roll."

Reed stared at the stone, then down at the darker grass of the seam, his narrow face slack with astonishment. "It... it aligns with the weir. It runs dead true to the dead ash."

Tolsey grunted, scratching his stubbled jaw. He looked at Reed, looked at the stone, and then spit into the grass. "Fair enough. The boy is right about the foot-rot. I lost four wethers in that hollow two years back, and I blamed the murrain."

He turned on his heel and waved his sons away toward the sheep-folds. Reed lingered for a moment, muttered an awkward, sheepish word of thanks to Lady Calla, pulled his cap low, and hurried off along his furrow.

Both men walked away convinced they had won their point, which was the only kind of victory that left the boundary intact.

---

Calla stood by the stone long after the two farmers had disappeared over the rise. The wind whipped her grey cloak around her ankles, teasing loose strands of dark hair from beneath her linen wimple.

"You knew where the footing was," she said, without turning her head.

Casian was leaning against his pony’s saddle, checking the girth. "I saw the gravel seam."

"You did not look at the gravel seam until you had your hand on that stone for half a minute," she said, turning to face him. Her hazel eyes were sharp, cold, and entirely unconvinced. "And when you leaned your shoulder against that pillar, you moved four hundred pounds of gritstone out of four inches of settled peat as though it were a bag of grain."

"The ground was wet," Casian said mildly. "The clay gives when you rock it."

"Do not lie to me, Casian Vale," she said, her voice dropping into a dangerous, measured quiet. "I have buried a husband, paid off two mortgages, and held this hill against three different cousins who thought a widow could be frightened out of a hall. I know what a boy of sixteen can do with a shovel, and I know what a stonecutter can do with a crowbar. That stone did not walk into that hollow because you leaned on it."

Casian held her gaze. He did not smile, and he did not look away. The mask of the agreeable fourth son remained smooth, but beneath it, the quiet stillness of the Current settled like deep water.

"It is back where it belongs, Lady Calla," he said softly. "The boundary is whole. The tenants are content. Is there anything else the Baroness of Fenwick requires of my father?"

She looked at him for a long, silent moment, the wind fluttering her cloak.

"Do you know what the Keeping is?" she asked abruptly.

"A custom," Casian replied. "We keep the stone, the ditch, and the fire."

"No," Calla said, walking over to the pillar and looking down at the dark circle of earth where it had sat an hour ago. "It is not a custom. In the spring, three weeks after the thaw, a carter brought an eight-ox team up the Common road with six tons of dressed timber for my sheep-pens. When the oxen reached this pillar, they stopped."

She looked up at him.

"They did not balk," she said, her voice hushed against the rustle of the gorse. "They did not rear. They dropped to their knees in the mud and began to tremble. The carter used his whip until the hide broke, and the lead bullock would not rise. He had to unyoke the entire team, haul the wains back down the hill by hand, and take the drove road six miles around the watershed. He lost two days, and he has refused to haul timber for me since."

"Why did the oxen stop?" Casian asked quietly.

"Because something walked across this grass two winters ago," Calla said, staring across the empty moor toward the dark eastern hills. "Something that did not have four hundred pounds of stone in its pockets, and did not care who owned the oats. And whatever it was, it lifted that pillar, moved it eleven paces, and set it down in the dark. And until today, I thought it was still here."

"And now?"

"Now," she said, her eyes fixing on Casian with an intensity that had nothing of the tavern or the court in it, "I think there is something far worse sitting in my neighbour’s garden, pretending to be an agreeable boy who weeds leeks."

Casian looked at the cuttings tied behind his saddle. He untied the hemp sack, walked over to her, and placed it into her hands.

"They are white damask," he said gently. "Plant them along the east face of your terrace wall, where the frost comes last. They need three buckets of water a week until Michaelmas, and then you may leave them to the rain."

She looked down at the bundle of damp twigs in her hands, her fingers tightening on the rough hemp.

"Why give me these?"

"Because you asked an honest question," Casian said, swinging up into his saddle and gathering the reins. "And because my mother says a walled house with no flowers looks like a prison."

He turned the pony toward the lane, lifted his hand once in salute, and rode down off the chalk hill into the afternoon shadow.

---

The night caught him halfway across the Common.

The road was a pale chalk furrow running between black walls of gorse and heather. Above him, the sky was a deep, velvet indigo, brilliant with the cold fire of autumn stars. The air had turned sharp, smelling of peat-smoke, dried bracken, and the approaching frost.

Casian let the pony walk at its own pace, his hands resting on his knees.

*The stone,* he thought into the silence. *Who moved it, Argius?*

"The kinetic trace is non-standard," Argius replied, its voice dry and measured. "It does not match human Current-manipulation. The field geometry was diffuse, lacking the concentrated vectors of deliberate intention."

*Like an animal?*

"An animal does not displace a rectangular gritstone pillar precisely eleven paces along a cardinal axis," Argius said. "It was deliberate, but unconscious. A wake, not a grasp."

*A thing passing through the marches,* Casian thought, looking east toward the high, jagged rim of the hills where the border clans kept their watches. *Two seasons ago. Before Hask Veyr built his slate hall. Before four riders crossed the Ashgrove ford.*

"A variable," Argius agreed. "Unindexed."

Casian rode on through the dark, listening to the steady, rhythmic crunch of the pony’s hooves on the dry flint. For sixteen years, he had been content to keep his garden—fourteen beds, a wall of roses, a water-channel, and a family that slept safely beneath timber rafters.

Now the world was beginning to crowd against the edges of his ditch.

*I want the whole east,* he thought. The thought was not born of ambition; it was born of the same cold, stubborn necessity that had made him clear the dead wood from the briars yesterday morning. *Not to wear a crown. Not to sit in a council hall. But so that when a thing moves in the dark, I am the one who knows its name.*

"That will require more than an orchard," Argius murmured.

*Then we will plant more than an orchard.*

---

He gave his report to his father the next morning in the study behind the solar.

It was a narrow, high-ceilinged room smelling of sheep-tallow, old parchment, and iron gall-ink. Baron Edric sat at a rough oak writing-desk with his spectacles perched on his crooked nose, his heavy grey brow furrowed over a leather-bound folio of tenant arrears.

Casian stood by the open casement, watching the rooks wheel over the orchard in the crisp morning air.

"Reed and Tolsey shook hands?" Edric asked, dipping a split-goose quill into the ink-horn.

"They shared a jug of cider before I left," Casian said. "The boundary runs where it always ran. Reed keeps his headland, and Tolsey keeps his dry grazing."

Edric scratched a line across the parchment, blew on the wet ink, and laid the quill down. He took off his spectacles, rubbing the red marks on the bridge of his nose with two scarred fingers.

"Calla sent a rider at first light," the Baron said, his grey eyes fixed on Casian. "She said the dispute was settled in less than two hours. She also said you were an insolent youth who needed six months in a garrison saddle to teach you respect for your elders."

Casian smiled faintly. "She was very polite to my face."

"She is never polite to anyone," Edric said, leaning back in his heavy oak chair. The leather groaned beneath his weight. "She said you moved the stone with your own hands."

"I used a lever, sir. A pine spar from the hedge."

Edric held his gaze for a long, quiet moment. The room was silent save for the ticking of an old water-clock on the shelf and the distant clink of the smith’s hammer in the courtyard.

"There was a line in her letter about the Keeping," Edric said slowly, his voice dropping into that gravelly, guarded register he used when speaking of border trouble. "She said the stone had been walked."

Casian did not move. "People say many things when the crop is late."

"Aye," Edric said, resting his weathered hands flat on the desk. "They do. I have heard those stories since I was small enough to hide under this table. Stones that weep when the frost comes. Fields where oxen will not plow. Roads that turn a horse around in the fog."

He looked at Casian, his tired, lined face heavy with thirty years of marcher fatigue.

"None of it ever paid a tax," the Baron said quietly. "None of it ever mended a thatch or stopped an upland spear from finding an armsman's belly. The priests call it old foolishness, and the bailiffs call it an excuse for short rent. But the fear is real, Casian. It gets into the bones of the tenants like marsh-ague. When a man believes his own field is haunted by an old horror, he stops weeding his ditch. He stops sowing his rye. He waits for the curse to take him."

Edric leaned forward, his grey eyes piercing and fierce.

"I do not care about the stones," he said. "I want you to grow up in a country that is not frightened of its own fields. That is the whole of what I have worked for, and I have spent thirty years to win about ten miles of it."

Casian looked at the old man's bowed shoulders, at the crooked finger broken in a forgotten skirmish, at the grey hair thinning at the temples.

"The stone is back in its hollow, Da," Casian said softly. "The field is quiet."

Edric stared at him for a moment longer, then let out a long, ragged sigh that sounded almost like relief. He picked up his spectacles, set them back on his nose, and pulled the rent-roll toward him.

"Good," the Baron muttered, taking up his quill. "Tell Hob to finish the millrace pier before Sunday. And stay out of Calla’s orchard. She has three cousins who would love an excuse to ride over with drawn steel."

"Yes, sir," Casian said.

He stepped out into the bright sunlight of the courtyard. High above the valley, the buzzards were circling the chalk slopes of High Crag, riding the morning thermals over an ancient, silent land that was only just beginning to wake.
