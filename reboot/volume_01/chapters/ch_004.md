The Vale mill stood a mile below the house where the race came down off the shoulder of the hill, and it had been grinding the barony's grain since before the Vales had the barony.

Maeve Corran had run it for four years. Her husband had run it for nineteen before that and had died of a bad chest in the winter, and she had taken it over in the week after the funeral without asking anybody's permission and had never once, in four years, failed to have the stones turning when the harvest came in.

Casian had known her his whole life in the way a boy knows the people who work his father's land. She was forty. She had a hard face and a dry way of saying things and a habit of looking at a man's hands while he talked, which most men found unsettling and which Casian had understood, from about the age of nine, to be a form of courtesy: she was checking whether you did anything.

He walked down on the fourteenth because Jory had mentioned at breakfast, in passing, in the way a man mentions a thing he does not want to be the one to have said, that the mill was going to change hands.

---

The mill was not going to change hands. That was the trouble. It was going to stay exactly where it was and grind exactly what it ground and the only thing that would change was who got the money, which in Jory's view, and in most people's view, was a distinction without a difference and not worth a scene over.

Casian stood at the mill door for a while before he went in.

Through the Current the mill was a dense little knot of things: grain in, flour out, the race, the wheel, the stones, four generations of repairs, and a set of accounts that existed in three places at once — in the tally sticks, in a book, and in Maeve Corran's head, and the head was the accurate one.

*Show me the debt,* he said.

"It was a good debt and then it was sold," said Argius. "Two years ago. Her husband borrowed against the mill in the bad harvest and the lender was a Harrowmere man and the lender sold the paper."

*To who?*

"A merchant in Thornwall. His name is Rennick. He buys paper in the east. He buys a great deal of paper in the east."

The name meant nothing to Casian. Thornwall meant a fat little barony on the other side of the Harrowmere cut, run by an old man named Osric who had been at his father's table twice and had once given Casian a coin for reciting something, which Casian had done, badly, on purpose.

*How much is the paper?*

"Four hundred and twelve marks. The mill is worth perhaps six hundred on a good day and the day is not good. Rennick has offered to forgive two hundred of it for the mill and the race outright."

*That's theft with a clean face.*

"That is what a paper is for."

---

He went in.

The inside of the mill was dim and full of flour dust and the noise was not as bad as people said, once you were in it. Maeve was at the stones with her sleeves tied back and her forearms white to the elbow, and she looked up and saw who it was and did not stop what she was doing.

"Master Casian."

"Mistress Corran."

"If your father wants the rent early he can want it."

"He doesn't."

"Then you're here about the paper, and you're the fourth person this month, and I'll tell you what I told the others, which is that it's my paper and my mill and I'll lose it in my own time."

Casian sat down on a grain sack, which she permitted by not objecting, and said, "I'm not here to offer you anything."

"That'll be a first."

"I'm here to ask you something and then I'll go."

She stopped the stones. That took a moment and a wedge and a shoulder, and she did it herself, which was itself an answer to a question he had not asked.

"Ask."

"How much of the four hundred and twelve is actually owed, and how much of it is Rennick's arithmetic?"

Maeve looked at him for a long moment, with her eyes on his hands.

Then she went to the shelf and took down a book, and opened it on the table, and turned it round.

"There," she said. "There's the tally. There's what my husband borrowed. There's every payment I've made since, in my own hand, because he could not be trusted with a figure and I could."

Casian read it.

It took him a while. The book was forty pages of a life: a bad harvest, a good one, a cow sold, a roof, a funeral paid for in three parts. She had recorded everything, in a small square hand, and at the back there was a page on which she had set out the debt as it stood and then — in the margin, in a smaller hand still, as though she had written it late at night and hoped nobody would read it — the debt as it should have stood.

The difference between the two figures was one hundred and thirty-one marks.

"He's been charging the interest on the interest," she said. "I've told three men and they've all said the same thing, which is that a paper is a paper and a miller's book is a miller's book, and that if I go before a court with this I'll have spent everything I have to be told I was right about half of nothing."

"It's not half of nothing. It's a hundred and thirty-one marks and it's fraud."

"It's a hundred and thirty-one marks and it's a merchant in Thornwall and I'm a widow with a mill." She closed the book. "So. Now you know. And you'll go home and your father will say the thing he always says, which is a good thing and I don't hold it against him, and it won't help me."

"What does he always say?"

"That the law is slow and the law is what we have." Maeve wiped her hands. "It's a fine thing to say in a hall. In a mill it's a way of saying no."

---

Casian rode to Thornwall that afternoon, which was twenty-two miles, and he did not tell anyone where he was going.

He did not go to see Rennick. He sat in the market square with a pie he did not eat and read the town the way he read a garden.

Thornwall was not a bad place. It was a fat place, which is different. The market was good, the road was good, there were four money-changers in a town that should have had one, and the whole of it was arranged around a baron who had been careful for thirty years and had never in that time been careless in a way that cost him anything.

By the end of the afternoon he knew three things.

Rennick's paper was not Rennick's. It had come from a factor who did not live in Thornwall and who had come into town twice this year and gone up to the castle both times.

Rennick's shop was one of eleven on that street, and six of the eleven were, in one way or another, carrying paper that had come in the same direction.

And Osric's steward kept a horse that had been bred in the eastern hills. Not bought. Bred. Casian knew it because he had seen forty horses out of that country and there was a way they stood.

*That's a long way for a steward to go for a horse.*

"It is," said Argius.

*Find out who he bought it from.*

"That will take some days."

*Good. It should.*

---

He came back to Vale that night and his grandmother was sitting up.

Oswin Vale objected to the practice of going to bed before the house had settled, on the grounds that a woman who went to bed early heard nothing worth hearing. She was seventy-one and had said this for fifty years. She was in the good chair with a candle and no book, which meant she had been waiting.

"You'll have been to Thornwall," she said.

"Yes."

"And bought a debt you cannot afford."

"Almost."

"The almost is rude."

Casian sat down facing her, because she liked to be faced.

"Did Brennan tell you?"

"Brennan doesn't know what he lent you for and he is excellent at not knowing, which is the most useful thing about him. Your father told me. He came in at supper and could not sit down." She turned her cup. "He will not say a word to you, because he is a good man and good men do not scold a son for being generous. He will simply be awake at midnight for a month."

"That's worse."

"That's the whole art of being a parent and he has never once been told." Oswin sipped. "You have a purse of eighteen marks and a miller who cannot pay and no land to your name. That is not a great deal. It is more than your brothers have spent in their lives, and it belongs to somebody else." She set the cup down. "So. Will you tell me what you are doing, or will you tell me a fine thing that sounds well and go to bed?"

Casian considered for a moment, and considered the old woman, and gave her a version that was true as far as it went.

"There's a decent mill at Vale and a bad man in Thornwall and a paper in between, and if I did nothing the mill would change hands and nobody would die. That's the size of it." He shrugged. "I did something."

"Hm." Oswin looked at the candle. "And when the next bad man comes, will you do something then too?"

"Probably."

"And who pays?"

"I do."

"With what?"

Casian said nothing.

"With what?" she said again, and answered herself. "Attention. You've a mind on you that puts me in mind of the east wall — plenty left, more than a boy should have, and no mortar still." She drank the last of it. "You'll go on spending it and it will seem to you that the ones you spend it on are the ones it concerns. It concerns you. Mark that. Every time, it concerns you, in the small hours, in a room with no one in it."

She got up then, in her way, which was all at once and without a hand on the chair.

"I'll not ask you what you are," she said. "There's a lot of men who'd ask and there's no answer that stays good, and I've had seventy-one years and one thing I know for certain, which is that a woman who empties a purse for a miller is either a fool or a good deal worse than one, and you've never once been a fool."

She went to the stair and stopped.

"You bought my purse," she said. "It's the finest thing I ever did with it and I'll want you to say so at my funeral and not before."

---

He got back to the mill three days later with a purse and a writ.

The writ was real. It was a warrant of satisfaction, drawn and sealed by a man in Harrowmere who did that sort of work for a fee and did it well, and it said that the debt of four hundred and twelve marks owed by the mill at Vale to Rennick of Thornwall had been satisfied in full, and it said so in the flat and boring language that officials use when they want a thing to be true.

He had bought the paper. That was all he had done. He had paid Rennick four hundred and twelve marks — the whole of it, including the hundred and thirty-one that was fraud, because a man who argues about a figure in a market leaves a man with a story about the argument, and Casian did not want Rennick to have a story. He wanted Rennick to have money and no paper and a vague sense that something had happened to him in an afternoon.

The money had come from three places. There had been a purse from his grandmother four years ago that he had built eight times over and had never once spent, and which now had a great deal less in it. There was the sale of two horses he had bred himself, which had gone to a buyer in Harrowmere for more than they were worth and less than they would be worth in three years. And there was a loan from Brennan, who had asked no questions and had looked at him for slightly too long.

Maeve read the writ twice.

"Where did you get four hundred marks?" she said.

"I had some and I borrowed some."

"You're sixteen."

"I'm aware."

"You're sixteen and you've bought my mill."

"I've bought the paper on your mill. You still owe me four hundred and twelve marks and I'm going to be a great deal worse about it than Rennick ever was." He sat down on the grain sack. "You'll pay me eighteen marks a quarter for six years and the interest will be nothing, and in six years the paper burns and the mill is yours and nobody in Thornwall has a claim on you again."

Maeve stood very still.

"That's not a loan," she said. "That's a gift with a story attached."

"It's a loan. It's a very good loan for you and a very bad one for me and I'm making it because I want something."

She put the writ down. "There it is."

"I want the book."

"The book."

"The one at the back. The one with the margin." Casian nodded at the shelf. "I want to be told what's in it. Not once. Every quarter, when the tallies come in. I want to know what the mill knows."

Maeve looked at him with her eyes on his hands.

"You want a spy," she said.

"I want a woman who can read what a merchant is doing by looking at his grain." He met her eye. "There are eleven shops on that street in Thornwall carrying paper that came from one place. I know that and nobody in this county knows it, and I only know it because I sat in a market square for six hours and looked at a road. You would have known it in a morning from a tally stick."

There was a long silence in the mill, with the dust turning in the light.

"My husband," said Maeve, "used to say that the trouble with this country is that the men who can read a number never get to decide anything, and the men who decide things can't read."

"I'd like to fix that."

"You'd like to fix it as far as it suits you."

"Yes," said Casian. "That's true and I'd rather say it than have you work it out later."

Maeve picked up the writ again and read it a third time. The mill was quiet now, the stones stopped, and in the quiet the wheel outside had a different sound — a slow tick at the bottom of its turn, where the flume leaked and always had.

"Six years," she said, mostly to herself. "Six years and I'll be forty-six and the paper burns and the mill is mine."

"Unless you sell it."

"I'll not sell it." She put the writ inside her sleeve. "My husband died in the room above the stones and I've kept them turning every harvest since and there's no man in this county who'll tell me a miller's widow can't." She looked at him. "I'll say a thing to you now and then I'll not say it again. There's three sorts of people come into a mill. There's the ones who want flour, and there's the ones who want the money, and once in a long while there's one who wants to know how the wheel works. I've had one of those in four years and he was nine." She nodded at the sack he was sitting on. "You've sat down. Most men stand. That's the whole of what I think of you and it's worth nothing yet."

"Eighteen marks out of the quarter is too little," she said. "Make it twenty and I'll keep the book for you, and if you ever ask me to write a thing in it that isn't so, we'll be done and I'll say why."

"Twenty."

"And I'll want to know who the eleven are."

"I'll tell you when I know."

"Bring me a list," said Maeve Corran, "and bring it in your own hand, and don't send a boy."

---

He walked home along the race in the last of the light, and the count in his head was one.

*One person,* he thought. *That's the whole of it. Twenty-two miles and four hundred marks and three days and I have one person.*

"That is not a bad rate," said Argius.

*It's the first thing I've ever built that isn't a wall.*

"It is also the first thing you have ever built that can be taken away by somebody else's decision. She can die. She can change her mind. She can be bought by a better offer."

*I know.*

"You are not afraid of that."

*No.* Casian put his hands in his pockets and watched the wheel turn in the dark below him, throwing its shadow up the bank. *I don't think I'm capable of being afraid of it. But I've noticed that I would mind.*

"Then mind," said Argius. "It is the only accounting you do that is any good to you."

---

That night at supper his father said, "I hear you bought a debt in Harrowmere."

"Yes."

"And paid it at Rennick's counter in Thornwall, in coin, in front of the man's clerk."

"Yes."

Edric put down his knife.

"Casian."

"I know."

"Do you?" His father's voice was not angry. It was the voice of a man who had been surprised by one of his sons and did not enjoy the sensation. "Because a man who buys a debt quietly is doing business, and a man who buys it in a coin in front of a clerk is making a point, and points get remembered in Thornwall."

"I wanted it remembered."

"By who?"

Casian looked at his father across the table, at the good grey careful face of him, at the whole of the reasonable world he represented, and said the truest thing he could say that would also fit in the room.

"By Rennick," he said. "He should know that the east pays its own."

Edric looked at him for a long moment.

"Your grandmother gave you that purse," he said at last, "and she will be insufferable about this."

"She already is."

"Mm," said his father, and picked his knife back up, and that was the end of it — except that later, going out, Casian felt his father's eyes on his back and knew, with the certainty of a man who had spent sixteen years being invisible in exactly this house, that the hiding was getting harder and would not get easier.

*He'll be watching now,* he thought.

"He will," said Argius.

*Good thing I have practice.*

"That," said Argius, "is not the good thing you think it is."
