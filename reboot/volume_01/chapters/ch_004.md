The Vale mill stood a mile below the manor house, where the millrace rushed down off the eastern shoulder of the hill in a foaming sluice of brown water. It was a broad, low-hipped building of river-shingle and dark adzed oak, vibrating to the deep, gut-shaking rumble of two French burr stones that had been grinding the valley's grain since the reign of King Malric.

Maeve Corran had kept the wheel turning for four years. Her husband had managed the mill for nineteen before that, until an autumn chest-rot had put him under the churchyard sod. In the week following the burial, Maeve had tied back her hair, hoisted the grain sacks herself, and never once asked the Baron's steward for assistance. She had kept the gears greased, the stone teeth sharp, and the flour bins dry through four hard harvests.

Casian had known her since his seventh summer. She was forty years of age, with grey streaks in her black hair, skin dusted chalky white with rye flour, and a habit of watching a man’s hands while he spoke. Most tenants found her silence unnerving; Casian knew it for what it was: an honest working woman checking whether a visitor knew the difference between a crowbar and an axe.

He walked down the mill lane on the fourteenth of August, while the morning grass was still wet with dew.

The millrace was running high, churning white around the mossy oak sluice-posts. Inside the timber shell, the air smelled of warm pulverized grain, damp river-weed, and the pungent tallow used to grease the great iron-bound drive shaft. Maeve stood on the grinding platform above the meal-troughs, a wooden scoop in one hand, her linen apron powdered with meal to the waistband.

She glanced up as his boots hit the floorboards, recognized him without breaking rhythm, and dumped another scoop of grain into the hopper.

"Master Casian," she shouted over the groan of the stones.

"Mistress Corran," he called back.

"If your father sent you down for the Martinmas rent, tell him the barley is still in the field."

"He didn't send me."

"Then you're here about the paper," she said, leaning against the timber hopper and wiping a floury wrist across her brow. "You're the fourth man this month. I told the bailiff, and I told the factor from Harrowmere: it’s my debt, it’s my mill, and I'll see it out in my own time."

Casian sat down on an unopened sack of seed-wheat near the bin.

"I didn't come to offer advice," he said.

"That would be a novelty."

"I came to ask one question, and then I'm riding to Thornwall."

Maeve paused. She looked at him with those pale, sharp eyes, reading the quiet posture of a boy who had sat on that same wheat sack since he was ten. She reached out, caught the release lever, and shoved the heavy ash wedge into the wheel-brace.

The great oak shaft slowed with a groaning shudder. The roar of the burr stones died to a low, rhythmic hum, leaving the mill filled with the wet slap of the river against the outer wall.

"Ask," she said.

Casian leaned forward, elbows on his knees. "How much of the four hundred and twelve marks is real coin, and how much is Rennick’s arithmetic?"

Maeve did not blink. For three seconds, the only sound in the mill was the slow drip of the headrace leaking through the pine flume.

She walked to the timber sideboard beside the flour-chute, unlocked a small iron-strapped box with a brass key kept on a cord about her neck, and pulled out a leather-bound tally ledger. She laid it on the meal-chest between them, turning it so the binding faced him.

"There," she said, tapping the rough calfskin with a flour-whitened knuckle. "Every bushel ground since my husband took sick. Every silver penny borrowed to buy the new ash shaft after the spring flood. Every clipping paid back to the Harrowmere factor before he sold the debt across the border."

Casian turned the pages slowly.

The handwriting was neat, angular, and uncompromising. It was the record of an unsparing life: three cows sold after the hard winter of '14; twelve shillings paid for lime; forty pence for a new iron spindle; and at the bottom of the final sheet, two numbers written side by side.

The first was Rennick’s demand: four hundred and twelve silver marks, compounded each quarter at the rate of three pennies to the shilling.

The second was written in the narrow margin, in ink so pale it looked as though it had been set down before sunrise: two hundred and eighty-one marks.

The difference was one hundred and thirty-one marks.

"He charges interest on the interest," Maeve said, her voice dropping into a dry, bitter flatline. "And three marks for every letter his clerk rides over with. I showed it to the magistrate at Harrowmere. He told me a sealed bond is a sealed bond, and that if I took a merchant from Thornwall before the Count’s court, I’d spend twenty marks on lawyers to be told I was a widow who signed a paper."

"It isn't a debt," Casian murmured, tracing the margin with his thumb. "It's a trap."

"It's the mill," she said simply. "Rennick offered to tear up the parchment if I deed him the race and the building and stay on as his hired miller at eight pence a week." She closed the ledger with a sharp slap. "I’d sooner throw the stones into the ditch and take my smock to the workhouse."

"Do not throw the stones into the ditch," Casian said, rising from the grain sack. "Keep the book safe. I will be back before Friday."

She looked at him with an expression that was neither hope nor gratitude, but the fierce, weary skepticism of a woman who had seen too many well-meaning boys make promises they could not pay for.

"Thornwall is twenty miles," she said. "Mind the bog on the lower road."

---

Casian rode out on the dun pony an hour before noon, taking the eastern drove road across the parish border.

Thornwall was a town that had grown fat without ever growing great. It sat where the royal road crossed the river into the high hills: a bustling cluster of slate-roofed taverns, wool-sheds, and timbered merchant houses huddled beneath the square stone keep of Baron Osric. Unlike the windswept holdings of the Vale barony, Thornwall smelled of roasted mutton, sour beer, and river coal. It had four money-changers' booths on the high street, two notary shops, and an ostentatious gatehouse with freshly carved heraldic shields.

Casian left his pony at an alehouse near the north ditch and walked the market square on foot.

He wore his plainest grey woolen tunic, looking like an ordinary farmer's son sent to buy a sack of salt. Through the Current, he did not look at the faces; he looked at the movement of goods, coin, and paper.

In three hours of quiet observation from the steps of the butter-cross, the town laid out its pattern.

Rennick’s counting-house sat on the north side of the square, a two-story timber house with iron grilles over the lower casements. It was one of eleven merchant offices along that row, and through the Current, Casian could feel the quiet, unseen web connecting six of them: parchment bonds carrying the same notary mark, silver shillings bearing the same clipped southern edge, and carters arriving by night with blue slate from the high Veyr valley.

In the middle of the afternoon, the Baron’s steward rode into the yard of the Black Bull.

He was a stout man in a fur-trimmed surcoat, riding a high-crested grey gelding with lean hocks and wide, intelligent eyes. It was a northern mountain mount—bred for navigating shale and scree, the kind of horse raised only in the high passes held by Hask Veyr’s outriders.

*A mountain gelding in a low-country town,* Casian thought. *And Osric’s steward carries a purse filled with silver from the same mint as the raiders on High Crag.*

"Collusion confirmed," Argius murmured in his skull. "Rennick is not acting as an independent lender. He is acquiring land titles and milling rights along the Vale border using funds provided through the Thornwall treasury."

*He wants our water-gates,* Casian observed. *If they hold the millrace at Vale, they control the flow of grain from the entire eastern valley.*

"An effective economic siege," said Argius. "How do you intend to neutralize the paper?"

*By paying it.*

"That is economically inefficient. The debt contains one hundred and thirty-one marks of verifiable fraud."

*If I dispute the fraud before a bailiff, Rennick has an argument, a lawsuit, and a reason to talk,* Casian replied. *If I hand him four hundred and twelve marks in coin across his counter, he has nothing but silver and an empty drawer.*

He walked across the square to the counting-house.

The clerk was a thin, balding man in a threadbare wool hood, dipping an iron quill into gall-ink while Rennick himself, a portly merchant with yellow teeth and a fox-skin collar, counted stacks of silver groats on a baize-covered table.

Casian set a heavy leather sack onto the baize.

The sack hit the oak with a dull, heavy clink that silenced the clerk’s pen.

"The bond of Maeve Corran," Casian said calmly. "Four hundred and twelve silver marks for the Vale mill and race."

Rennick stared at the sack, then looked up at the quiet, grey-eyed youth standing before him. A merchant's mind worked quickly: he looked for a sword, looked for a sheriff's badge, looked for a legal summons, and found nothing more than an unhurried boy with dirt on his turnshoes.

"Who are you?" Rennick asked, narrowing his small, greasy eyes.

"The coin is from the Vale manor," Casian said, untying the leather thong. "Count it. Give me the canceled bond and the warrant of satisfaction with your seal, or call the town bailiff to witness the tender."

The clerk looked at Rennick; Rennick looked at the heavy purse of silver. A merchant does not refuse lawful coin tendered in front of a clerk without forfeiting his charter.

It took forty minutes to weigh the silver. Every groat was good marcher minting. When the last stack was tallied, Rennick dipped his seal into hot red wax, stamped the parchment release with an ill-tempered scowl, and pushed the canceled bond across the table.

"The woman is a pauper," Rennick spat. "She cannot pay two shillings on the quarter. You've bought a broken wheel, boy."

Casian took the parchment, folded it neatly, and tucked it into his breast.

"The wheel turns very well," he said. "Good day, Master Rennick."

---

He returned to Vale late that evening, the night wind cold against his collar.

The great hall was dark and silent, save for a single tallow lamp burning on the sideboard where Lady Oswin Vale sat wrapped in her wool shawl. She had no book and no embroidery; she sat with her chin resting on her knuckles, watching the door as the latch lifted.

"You've been to Thornwall," she said, her voice dry as parchment in the quiet room.

Casian closed the heavy door behind him. "Yes, grandmother."

"And you've emptied your purse."

"I have."

Oswin pointed with a crooked, veined finger to the low stool opposite her. "Sit down."

Casian sat.

"Your father came in from the cut an hour after sundown," she said, her faded blue eyes fixing on him with relentless clarity. "He looked at the sideboard, saw that your grandmother's legacy was gone from the iron chest, and could not eat his soup. He will say nothing to you, because Edric believes a father should not meddle with a son’s inheritance. He will simply lie awake until cockcrow wondering which of your brothers will have to bail you out of debt."

"I didn't spend it on dice," Casian said mildly.

"I know what you spent it on," she replied sharply. "You spent it on Maeve Corran’s mill. Brennan came through the buttery three hours ago smelling of ale and told me you borrowed fifty marks from his horse-purse yesterday without giving him an excuse." She leaned forward, the tallow light catching the deep lines around her mouth. "A boy of sixteen who buys a widow's mortgage with four hundred marks of good silver is either an utter fool or something far more dangerous. You have never been a fool."

Casian met her gaze. "The mill was about to be taken by an agent of Thornwall."

"And what is that to you?" she demanded. "The marches are full of men who lose their mills, their cows, and their cottages. If you pay the debts of every fool between here and Harrowmere, you will be a beggar in rags before you have a beard!"

"I am not paying every debt," Casian said softly. "Only the debts that touch our water."

Oswin held his gaze for a long, quiet moment. The anger in her face did not fade, but it shifted, hardening into something darker and far more sober.

"You think you are spending silver," she said, her voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "You are not. You are spending attention. You have a mind like an iron pry-bar, Casian. You put it under a problem and you heave, and you think the only people who feel the weight are the ones you choose to help."

She stood up from the chair with a dry pop of her knees.

"It will concern you," she said, pausing at the foot of the timber stair. "Every time you lift a man out of a ditch, you tie his rope around your own neck. In ten years, you will not be able to walk from the hall to the orchard without twenty men waiting to tell you which way the wind blows."

She turned and climbed the stair into the dark, her turnshoes whispering on the treads.

---

The morning sun had barely cleared the willows when Casian returned to the mill.

Maeve was at the hopper, her hands covered in white dust. When she saw him step through the door, her eyes dropped immediately to his belt, expecting a bailiff’s man or an eviction notice.

Casian walked to the meal-chest, pulled the sealed parchment from his tunic, and laid it flat on the wood.

"The debt is satisfied," he said.

Maeve did not touch the paper. She stared at the red wax seal of the Thornwall merchant, her throat working as she swallowed hard.

"Where did you find four hundred marks?" she whispered.

"I had an inheritance from my grandmother," Casian said, leaning against the timber frame. "And fifty marks from Brennan’s horse-trade. The paper is mine now. You owe nothing to Thornwall."

Her hands gripped the edge of the meal-chest until her knuckles showed white through the meal. "And what do I owe you?"

"Eighteen marks a quarter," Casian said evenly. "For six years. No interest. At the end of six years, the paper is burned in your kiln, and the mill belongs to you and your heirs forever."

Maeve looked at him, her hard, lined face rigid with disbelief.

"That is not a loan," she said, her voice trembling with a rare, fierce anger. "That is charity from a lord's son who wants to feel noble before dinner."

"It is not charity," Casian said, meeting her gaze with an unblinking stillness that stopped her breath. "I want something in return."

She straightened her back, her chin coming up with the defensive pride of an upland woman. "What do you want?"

"The ledger," Casian said. "The one with the numbers in the margin."

She frowned, bewildered. "The book? You paid four hundred silver marks for an old tally book?"

"I want you to keep the records for the entire eastern road," Casian said, his voice quiet, steady, and commanding. "Every wain of grain that crosses from Thornwall. Every merchant who buys wool on credit. Every cart of mountain slate that moves down the drove road by night. You understand numbers, Mistress Corran. You know what a merchant means when his tallies do not balance."

He pointed to the shelf above the desk.

"You keep the mill," he told her. "You grind the grain, you take your toll, and you pay me eighteen marks on the quarter-day. But when I ask you what the accounts mean, you tell me the truth. You tell me who is buying, who is selling, and whose silver is behind the paper."

Maeve stood motionless for a long while. The river slapped rhythmically against the mill-wheel outside, a slow, eternal pulse beneath the floorboards.

She looked at the canceled parchment with its broken Thornwall seal. Then she reached out, picked it up, and slid it carefully inside her apron pocket.

"Twenty marks on the quarter," she said, her voice steady and clear. "Not eighteen. I am a miller, not a debtor on parish alms. I will pay twenty marks four times a year, and the paper burns in five years instead of six."

Casian smiled faintly. "Done."

"And if you want the truth from the ledgers," Maeve added, stepping up to the grinding platform and taking up her wooden scoop, "you bring me the ink and the parchment yourself. I will not have your father's bailiffs poking their noses into my mill."

"You have my word," Casian said.

He turned and walked out into the bright August sunlight.

He took the path that followed the millrace up toward the manor. The Current moved beneath his boots, humming through the water, the turf, and the roots of the willow trees.

*One,* he thought.

"A single human unit," Argius noted in his mind. "A miller's widow with a talent for bookkeeping. A costly acquisition for four hundred and twelve silver marks."

*It is not an acquisition,* Casian thought, looking up at the high ridge of High Crag standing sharp against the blue sky. *It is a foundation. She was going to lose her wheel to an upland warlord's silver.*

"She is now dependent upon your political protection," Argius observed. "If your father learns the origin of the funds, or if Thornwall discovers the fraud was bypassed, she cannot maintain possession without your intervention."

Casian paused at the garden wicket, his hand resting on the latch.

*Then I will intervene,* he thought.

He pushed the gate open and walked toward the terrace, where his young walnut sapling stood tied between its stakes, waiting for forty years of sun.
