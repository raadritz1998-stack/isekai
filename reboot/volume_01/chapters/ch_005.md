Mirra Cott had been at the wash-trough since the first grey light broke over the alder bottoms.

She was fifty years old, with red, calloused knuckles from thirty winters of boiling river-water and lye, and she had washed for the Vale manor kitchen since she was twenty. She knew every linen sheet, every wool tunicle, and every grease-spot on Baron Edric’s hunting coats. She had taught Elna to whistle through two fingers behind the dairy, she had held Casian’s head still while the apothecary stitched his split brow after a fall from the orchard wall, and she had never once asked the Baron for more than her six silver pence a week and a sack of barley at Michaelmas.

Her son Jon was in the lower paddock with the goat, nineteen years old, broad-shouldered and brown from the summer haying. He was to be married at harvest to Sennet, a carter’s daughter from Ashgrove who had a laugh that carried across three pastures.

"Jon," she called out, wringing a grey wool tunic over the wooden tub. "That south corner of the thatch wants three bunches of fresh reed before the September rain."

Jon set his wooden rake against the fence. "The reed is still drying in the ditch, mother. The thatch will hold until the barley is in."

"A bad corner in August is a drowned bed in October," she told him, tossing a clean linen rag at his chest.

Jon caught it with a grin, shaking his head. "You have been predicting a drowned bed every autumn since I had teeth."

"And two winters back you slept under a soaked sheepskin," she retorted, wiping her wet forearms on her sacking apron.

The laugh died in his throat. He turned toward the gully, his brow furrowing.

The sound had come from the drovers' track: the slow, deliberate clop of iron-shod hooves on dry river gravel.

Nine riders came up out of the alder bottoms. They did not gallop, and they did not blow horns. They rode in single file at an easy walking pace, their mounts lean and high-crested, wearing boiled-leather jacks with dull iron scales. Each man carried a short hunting bow cased behind his knee and a light ash lance tipped with darkened steel.

The man in the lead sat on a heavy grey gelding. He was thirty-seven years of age, with a broad, weather-beaten face, deep creases around his pale eyes, and the quiet, immovable seat of an armsman who had spent twenty years in border saddles. There was flour-dust on his left cuff from an early morning bake, and a small copper ring through the lobe of his left ear.

He halted his horse ten paces from the wash-trough, his hands resting loose on the high wooden pommel.

"This is Vale land," Mirra said, stepping in front of the tub. Her voice was thin, but it did not waver. "Baron Edric’s holding."

"I know whose land it is, mistress," the man said mildly.

"Then you have no business in this ditch."

The man looked past her toward Jon, who stood frozen by the goat-pen with his hand closed tight around the haft of the ash rake.

"Your boy bought an iron hunting knife off a peddler three weeks back," the man said. His voice was calm, conversational, devoid of anger. "Down at the crossroad tavern. He paid two clipped groats for it. A good knife, with an antler grip."

Jon’s face went white beneath the summer tan. "I bought it for the butchering."

"You did," the rider agreed. "And while you held the blade to the light, you told the peddler that the Vale manor had nine horses in the home yard, twenty lances in the paddock, and that the fourth son spent his afternoons weeding leeks."

Jon took a step back, the blood draining from his lips. "I... I was speaking to a tinker."

"You were speaking to one of my scouts," the man said. He looked down at the horse's mane, picking off a dry burr with his thumb. "He gave you a knife. You gave him an inventory. That is the trade, lad. That is how the hills learn where the fences are thin."

"Tolan," one of the rear riders muttered, glancing uneasily back toward the highway. "The valley is awake. Let’s have it done."

Tolan swung down from the saddle. He landed lightly for a big man, his boots making only a dull thud in the dry grass. He walked to the haystack beside the byre, rested his calloused palm against the dried clover, and looked up at the thatched roof of the cottage.

"Burn the stack first," Tolan said, his voice flat and level. "Then the byre. Then the thatch."

"Tolan," the rider said again, leaning over his horse's neck. "The woman and the boy are standing in the yard."

Tolan did not turn round. "If you leave two witnesses to ride to the Vale manor, the Baron’s horsemen will be across the ford before we reach the cut. If the holding is black ash, they will spend two days arguing about whether it was wolves, drovers, or a stray lightning strike."

"They're farmers," the rider said.

"They're Vale's people," Tolan replied, stepping back toward his grey gelding. "Burn it all."

Mirra reached out and caught Jon’s sleeve, pulling him toward the rear ditch with all the strength left in her arms.

"Run," she whispered, her voice cracking. "Jon, run for the timber—"

A mounted man cut across the grass, blocking the lane with his lance held low.

Tolan climbed back into his saddle, gathered his reins, and looked down at the pommel of his saddle. Behind him, dry hay caught with a sharp, crackling roar, sending a thick column of greasy yellow smoke climbing into the pale summer sky.

He did not look at the yard again.

He pulled a small horn tally-tablet from his pouch, uncapped a small lead stylus, and notched two straight lines into the edge.

*Thirty-one,* he thought, listening to the crackle of burning pine behind him. *Thirty-one since the high valley took the name.*

He turned the grey horse toward the hills and rode out at a walk, not looking back.

---

At the Vale manor, Elna was sitting on the eastern rampart with her heels swinging over the ditch when the smoke showed.

It was not the pale blue haze of a kitchen chimney or the thin grey mist of a turf-kiln. It was thick, black, and oily, boiling up above the crest of the ash woods five miles to the south-east, leaning heavily toward the river under the noon breeze.

She stared at it for three heartbeats, her breath catching in her throat, before she scrambled down off the stone coping and hit the gravel at a run.

"Mother!" she screamed, bursting through the kitchen porch. "Mother, there's fire on the Ashgrove road!"

Maren was at the flour-bin, her arms white to the elbow. She did not drop her scoop. She froze, her head snapping toward the open window, her grey eyes locking onto the dark plume rising beyond the orchard wall.

"Elna," Maren said, her voice dropping into that cold, terrifying calm she used when a child took sick. "Where is your father?"

"In the lower barn with the carters."

"Run to him. Tell him Ashgrove is burning. Gareth! Gareth, to the yard!"

Gareth was already through the armory door, his leather brigandine half-buckled over his linen shirt, his broadsword strapped to his hip. His face was stark white, his eyes wide and fierce. He had seen a burned holding once before, as a boy of fifteen at Fenwick, and the memory moved him faster than an order.

"Jory!" Gareth roared into the courtyard. "Saddle eight! Take the lances and the water-kegs! Move, you bastards!"

Elna tried to follow him into the yard, but Gareth caught her shoulder with one hard, heavy hand and shoved her firmly behind the timber doorpost.

"Inside," he snapped, his voice rough as broken slate. "Inside the hall, Elna. Do not look out the gate."

---

Casian was in the walled garden when the smoke rose.

He stood beside the young walnut sapling, the pruning hook in his hand, feeling the world shift beneath his boots.

Through the Current, the distance was five miles. It was not a sight; it was the sudden, violent rupture of an established order. A hearth that had burned for thirty years went cold in a single flash of heat; two living pulses—the steady, rhythmic beat of a woman who had washed his linen, and the quick, terrified flutter of a boy who had split his kindling—were extinguished like grease dropped onto a glowing hearthstone.

He had felt the horsemen cross the ford an hour ago. He had known they were armed. He had known they carried light lances and boiled leather.

And he had stayed in the garden.

He had stayed on the stone bench, weeding leeks, because it was not yet his war. Because his father had thirty armsmen. Because the border was twenty miles long and he was a boy of sixteen who liked his peace.

*Argius,* he thought. The word was cold, hard, and devoid of breath.

"Nine mounted raiders," Argius said inside his skull. "Unit identity matches the upland scouts operating out of the Veyr valley. Total casualties: two human biological entities, one dwelling, one storage structure, five head of livestock."

*I could have turned their mounts at the ford.*

"You could have severed their cervical columns at twelve miles," Argius responded, its voice completely flat. "You did not possess actionable tactical intent. You chose non-intervention to preserve operational camouflage."

*I was weeding leeks.*

"A factual statement," Argius said. "The holding is destroyed. The raiders are withdrawing north-east along the dry gully."

Casian looked down at the iron pruning hook in his hand. His knuckles were white against the horn haft, but his hand did not shake.

*They killed Mirra,* he thought. *She washed my shirts when I had four teeth.*

"She was an asset of this holding," Argius noted. "The death is a strategic debit."

*It is not a debit,* Casian said, his mind dropping into an icy, boundless quiet that reached across five miles of valley like frost across a pond. *It is a debt. And I am going to collect every ounce of it.*

---

The cart returned at four in the afternoon.

The yard was dead silent save for the creak of the dry iron axle and the heavy, exhausted breathing of the two draft oxen. Gareth walked at the head of the team, his mail coif pushed back onto his shoulders, his hands and tunic smeared with black charcoal soot. He did not look at anyone.

Maren stood on the stone steps of the porch, her apron clean, her hands clasped tightly against her stomach. Edric walked beside her, his face grey as river slate, his fingers knotted in his belt.

The cart stopped before the kitchen door.

Inside the bed lay a single shape wrapped in a coarse wool horse-blanket, tied with hemp twine.

"Where is Jon?" Maren asked, her voice barely a whisper above the wind.

Gareth stopped. He looked at his mother, looked at the stones between his boots, and shook his head once.

"There wasn't enough to bring home, mother," Gareth said, his voice cracking like dry wood. "They trapped them in the byre and threw pitch onto the thatch."

Maren closed her eyes for two long seconds. Her jaw set so hard that the cords in her neck stood out like iron wire. She did not weep. She stepped down off the porch, walked to the cart, and laid her bare palm against the scorched horse-blanket.

"Bring her into the small buttery," Maren said, her voice perfectly steady. "Fetch clean linen and lavender water. No one washes her but me."

Casian stood beside the paddock fence, watching them carry the blanket through the door.

He waited until the yard had emptied, until the armsmen had led the oxen away to the ditch, and then walked into the buttery.

The room smelled of cold stone, vinegar, and the sharp, choking stench of burned wool. Mirra lay on the oak salting table beneath a clean sheet of bleached flax. Her face was untouched by the flames—pale, lined, and peaceful—but her grey hair was scorched at the temples, and her calloused hands were frozen into claws that had tried to tear through a burning timber wall.

Casian stood over her for a long time.

He remembered her singing a foolish song about three crows while she rubbed lye into his grass-stained knees. He remembered her setting a warm bowl of toasted oats beside his cot when he had the winter ague.

*The fault was not Jon's,* he thought. *The boy had a knife and a tongue. The fault was mine. I had the Current, and I sat on a bench.*

"Self-recrimination is non-functional," Argius said softly.

*I am not recriminating myself,* Casian replied. *I am recording the ledger.*

He reached out and smoothed a strand of scorched grey hair back from her temple.

"Sleep well, Mirra," he murmured. "The wash will be done."

---

He found Jory in the guardroom behind the gate, sitting on a bench with a map of the marches spread out over a barrel head.

The guard captain looked up as Casian entered, his scarred face haggard and lined with fatigue.

"The Baron wants twelve lances on the road by midnight," Jory said gruffly. "We’re reinforcing the Ashgrove tower."

"The Ashgrove road is empty," Casian said, walking to the barrel and looking down at the ink-drawn parchment. "The raiders didn't take the highway. They came down the alder gully and went back along the high cut."

Jory frowned, squinting up at him. "How do you know that?"

"Look at the map," Casian said, his finger coming down on the parchment with absolute precision. "There are four known roads through the marches: the Harrowmere cut, the high stone highway, the Fenwick drove, and the low valley path."

His finger traced a line across the open Common, following the contour of the high ridge where no highway was marked.

"There is a fifth," Casian said.

Jory leaned forward, staring at the blank parchment between the contour lines. "That's open heather, Master Casian. Nothing crosses the Common but sheep."

"Sheep, and wains loaded with stolen grain," Casian countered. "A dry drove over the chalk spine that connects the high Veyr valley directly to the Thornwall road, bypassing every toll-gate and beacon tower in the province. That is the road the nine riders used. That is the road Hask Veyr uses to move his plunder into Osric's markets."

Jory stared at the blank line on the map, his jaw slackening as the pieces aligned in his mind.

"God above," the captain whispered. "A fifth road. Right under our noses."

"Find out who grazes the Common along that ridge," Casian instructed, his voice cold and quiet. "Find out who sells them oats, who shoes their horses, and which merchants in Harrowmere hold their paper. I want every name on that drove before the week is out."

Jory looked at the sixteen-year-old boy standing before him. For the first time in six years, the guard captain did not see Baron Edric's easy, agreeable fourth son. He saw something cold, vast, and terrifying looking back through grey eyes that had never learned to flinch.

"Yes, Master Casian," Jory said.

---

Supper that evening was a ghost of a meal.

The family sat at the long ash table, but no one ate. Even Emric was silent, sitting on Rowan’s lap with his thumb in his mouth, sensing the heavy pall that hung over the hall.

Edric stood at the head of the table, his fists resting flat on the wood, his eyes dark with an old, familiar fury.

"I have written to the Count of Varyn," Edric said, his voice ringing harsh against the rafters. "I have written to Baron Harven. I have dispatched a messenger to the High Sheriff at Harrowmere. We will demand an inquest and an armed levy."

Gareth slammed his knife into his trencher. "An inquest! Da, two of our people were burned alive in their own byre! By the time the Count reads your letter, the snow will be in the passes and Hask Veyr will be laughing at us from his slate hall!"

"And what would you have me do?" Edric shouted, his voice cracking with thirty years of marcher helplessness. "Ride twenty lances into the high valleys? He has a hundred and twenty mounted men behind stone walls! If we ride into those hills without the Count’s banner, we are rebels, and we will be slaughtered in the gorges before we sight his gate!"

The Baron leaned forward, his shoulders trembling with exhaustion.

"The law is slow," Edric said, his voice sinking into a quiet, agonized plea. "The law is slow, but it is what we have. It is all that keeps this valley from becoming a slaughterhouse."

Casian sat three stools down, his hands folded in his lap.

"The law is what we have," Casian said mildly. "Father is right."

Gareth glared at him with furious disgust. "You would say that. You’d sit in the garden and let them burn every holding between here and the sea."

Casian did not answer. He met Gareth’s glare with a calm, unblinking stillness that made the older brother turn away first.

---

After the hall had cleared, Maren caught Casian in the darkened corridor outside the kitchen.

She stood with her back against the timber wall, her face pale in the moonlight, her arms crossed over her chest.

"You agreed with your father," she said.

"He needs to believe the letters matter," Casian replied quietly.

"I have known for twenty years that the letters are worth nothing," Maren said, her voice dropping to a fierce, ragged whisper that shook him to the bone. "They write their letters, and they hold their inquests, and the women of this valley bury their sons in the rain."

She reached out, her rough, soap-scented fingers taking his chin, forcing him to look directly into her eyes.

"I know what you are doing, Casian," she whispered. "I do not know how you do it, and I will never ask. But hear me now: whatever you do to those men in the hills, do it so that I am never sat in this kitchen being told about it by a man with a hat in his hands. Do you hear me?"

Casian felt the warmth of her hand against his skin, the fierce, unyielding love of a mother who had chosen her weapon.

"I hear you, mother," he said softly.

"Say it back."

"You will never hear it from a man with a hat in his hands," Casian promised. "You will hear it from me."

She looked at him for a long heartbeat, nodded once, and slipped into the dark kitchen, leaving him alone in the hall.

Casian walked out through the postern gate and climbed to the top of the eastern wall.

The night was freezing, the autumn stars sharp as daggers above the black rim of High Crag. Out across the Common, five miles to the south-east, the embers of the Ashgrove holding still glowed—a tiny, pulsing red eye in the vast black wilderness of the marches.

*A fifth road,* Casian thought, looking east toward the hills where Tolan and his riders slept.

"The map is expanding," Argius murmured.

*The map was always this large,* Casian said, his hands resting on the cold stone merlons. *I was simply looking at the roses.*

He looked down at his home, at the slate roofs, at the quiet windows where his family slept.

*Thirty-one,* he thought, recalling the number Tolan had notched into his horn slate. *He counts his dead.*

Casian turned his face to the cold wind blowing off the high moors.

*I will give him something else to count.*
