Mirra Cott had been up since the light, because the light was when there was work and there was always work.

She was fifty and she had washed for the Vale kitchen for thirty years, riding up twice a week in summer and once in winter, and she had a way of describing the family to her neighbours that was neither flattering nor unkind and was mostly true. The old lady was hard and had a good heart under it. The baron did not know her name for the first four years and then one day knew it and never forgot it again. The little girl was a menace. The fourth boy was the one to watch, and when people asked her why she said because he watches, which she knew was not a proper answer and did not have a better one.

Her son Jon was in the yard with the goat, nineteen years old, brown as a nut, and going to be married at harvest to a girl from Ashgrove called Sennet who had a laugh you could hear across two fields.

"You'll want to be getting the thatch seen to before the wet," Mirra told him, which she had told him every week that summer.

"The thatch is fine."

"The thatch is not fine. The thatch has a bad corner and a bad corner in September is a wet bed in October."

"The thatch," said Jon, with the enormous patience of a nineteen-year-old, "has a bad corner, and I will see to it, and you will tell me you told me so, and I would like that to happen in October and not now."

She threw a cloth at him. He caught it, because he was quick, and it was a good morning, and it was the last one.

---

The riders came off the Ashgrove road a little after noon, and there were nine of them, and they were not in a hurry.

That was the thing Mirra thought about afterwards, in the part of her mind that was still working: they were not in a hurry. They came up the track at a walk. One of them was singing something and stopped when they saw the yard, not because he was ashamed, but because he had come to the part where it mattered to listen.

A man on a grey horse came to the front of them and sat looking at the holding — the house, the byre, the stack, the goat, the boy standing in the yard with a cloth in his hand.

"This is Vale land," Mirra said.

"I know it is."

"Then you'll be on your way."

The man considered her. He had a broad, tired, careful face, and he was perhaps thirty-five, and there was flour on his sleeve from a morning that had involved bread, which she noticed and would remember, because it made the whole of the rest of it worse.

"You'll have had a man through here," he said, "three weeks back. Selling knives."

Mirra said nothing.

"Your boy there bought one," said the man, without heat. "A good knife and he paid too much for it. He told the man there were nine horses at the Vale yard and a new stone at the gate." He looked at Jon, and his voice was almost gentle. "He'll have told you that he said it proud, and he will have said it proud, because a boy will say a thing proud. But he told it. That's the whole of it. That's why I'm here."

Jon had gone the colour of ash.

"I didn't know," he said.

"No," said the man. "Nobody knows, that's the thing about it."

"Tolan," said one of the riders, behind him, in a tone that was trying to hurry him.

"Give me a moment," said Tolan.

He got down off the grey horse. He was a big man and he landed soft, and he walked to the stack and put his hand on it the way a man checks a wall he is going to have to explain later, and then he said, without raising his voice, "It has to be all of it. If it's part of it, they'll say it was a raid. If it's all of it, they'll say it was the marches."

"And if the people are in it?" said the rider.

"Then they're in it."

Mirra Cott took her son's arm and pulled him back toward the house, and the thing she would have said, if there had been time, and if she had thought it would do any good, was that she had washed the shirts of the man who owned that wall for thirty years and that there was a woman up at the Vale who would hear about this and would be sorry, and that being sorry was worth nothing at all.

The house went up first. It went up fast, which she had not expected, and it took a long time for the roof to come down, which she also had not expected.

---

The smoke was visible from the Vale wall at a quarter past the hour, and Elna saw it first, because she was on the wall.

She did not know what it was. She was twelve and she had never seen a house burn and it took her a moment to put the shapes together — the grey going up, the way it was not going straight up but leaning, the fact that it was in a place where a house was. By the time she had worked it out she was already running.

Maren was in the kitchen and heard the whole of it from her daughter's mouth in a jumble, and did the thing she had done all her life, which was to take one deep breath and ask the one question that sorted everything.

"Is anyone in it?"

"I don't know."

"Then go and find your father," said Maren Vale, and Elna went, and Maren stood in the middle of the kitchen with her hands still in the bread and looked at the smoke coming up over the east wall for four long seconds before she followed her daughter out.

Gareth was in the yard with the horses before anyone told him to be. He had ridden out to a fire once before, at Fenwick, in his fifteenth year, and had come back white and quiet and had not talked about it, and something in the way he moved now showed that he remembered exactly what a burned holding looked like and did not want his sister to see it.

"Elna. Inside."

"I want to —"

"Elna." He said it once, without heat, in a voice she had never heard him use, and she went inside.

---

Tolan counted them himself, which was not his job and which he had done at every burning for six years.

That was his private rule and he had never explained it to anyone: a man who gives an order counts what it cost. He walked the yard twice. He looked in the byre, which had burned badly and had one corner standing, and he looked in the lane where the boy had run, and then he went and stood by the wall and was quiet for a while.

"Nine horses at the Vale yard," said one of the men, behind him. "That's what the knife-seller said. Nine horses and a new stone at the gate."

"There's nine horses at that yard and there's a wall and forty men within a mile of it," said Tolan. "You'll note we're not at the yard."

"Give it a week and we will be."

"Give it a week and we'll be dead in a lane," said Tolan, without heat. "The Vale baron is a careful man and he's got a son who's a sword and the whole county knows the son's name. You don't take a walled yard off a man who's expecting you. You take the farms first, and you make him come out, and then you have him in a field instead of behind stone."

"And the chieftain says different."

"The chieftain has a hundred men and no title and half of them are being paid out of a purse that isn't his," said Tolan. "He says a great many things."

There was a silence behind him, which he had expected, and which was the reason it was worth saying.

He got back on the grey horse and looked at the yard once more and then did not look at it again. On the road back he did not sing with the others. He had, some years ago, stopped being able to do it on the day of a thing, and had found that it came back to him about a week later, and he had given up trying to work out whether that was a flaw in him or the last honest thing he had.

*Two,* he thought. *A woman and a boy. And a goat and a stack and a roof.*

He did not have a word for what he felt about it and would have been embarrassed to be asked. It was not guilt — guilt was for men who had been wrong, and he had not been wrong, and the boy had told a stranger about his horses. It was closer to arithmetic. He carried a number around with him now that had begun at nothing six years ago and was, this afternoon, at thirty-one.

He was aware that at some point the number would be the only thing he had. He did not know what he would do then.

---

Casian was in the garden when he heard, and he was still in the garden when Gareth went out with eleven men and Jory and a cart, and he was in the garden when the cart came back.

That was what he would remember later: that he was standing among the roses with a pair of shears in his hand, at the hour he had chosen, doing the nothing he had chosen to do, and it had taken eleven men and a cart and four hours.

He had felt the holding go out. That was the phrase his mind arrived at and he did not like it and did not correct it — a holding, a place with sheep on it and a boy and a woman in it, going out like a lamp. He had felt it at the moment the roof went, from a distance of five miles, the way a man might feel a door close in another room of his own house.

He had done nothing.

*Argius.*

"There were nine men," said Argius. "You could have held the horse of any one of them."

*I know.*

"You did not know what it was until the smoke."

*I know.*

"You are not going to be told anything comforting, so I will not try."

*Good,* said Casian, and found that his hands were perfectly steady, which was not a surprise and was, in a way he could not have described to anyone, the worst part of it.

---

They brought Mirra back in the cart because Maren insisted, and she was laid in the small room off the kitchen where the family laid its dead.

Jon was not in the cart. There was not enough of Jon to be in a cart, and Gareth, who had found him, had not let anyone else go into what was left of the yard.

Casian went in and looked at Mirra for a while.

She had washed his shirts for sixteen years. She had taught Elna to whistle, badly, over the objections of everyone. She had once, when he was eight and had fallen off a wall and split his eyebrow, held his head still with both hands while Aldous put the stitch in, and had told him a long and entirely false story about a man who had a cow that could count, and he had believed the cow story until he was eleven and had been pleased to have believed it for so long.

He had known all of that before today. He had known it the way a man knows the shape of his own hand.

*She was mine to look after,* he thought, *and I did not look.*

"She was Vale's tenant," said Argius. "Her protection was your father's duty and he sent no one to the Ashgrove road because it was not, on any map he keeps, a dangerous place."

*It was mine.*

"You had not decided that yet. You have been deciding it for two weeks."

*Then I decided too slowly.*

"Yes," said Argius. "That is exactly true and you will remember it, and you will not be given a way to put it down."

Casian stood there with the smell of the burned thing still in his clothes and arrived, without any drama at all, at the arrangement that would govern the rest of his life.

He did not decide to revenge himself on Hask Veyr. He would, later, and it would be enjoyable and thorough. But what settled in him in that room was much quieter and much worse than a wish to punish.

It was the realisation that he could not see everything, and that he had been living as though he could, because everything he had ever wanted to see had been on his own land, and his own land had been a walled garden, and the whole of the eastern marches was not.

*I can be anywhere,* he thought. *I cannot be everywhere at once unless there is someone there to be.*

He went out of the room and asked Jory to send for Brennan, and for the man who kept the books at Harrowmere, and for anyone in the county who knew who bought and sold grain on the Ashgrove road.

Jory wrote it down and then stood there with the slate in his hand.

"Master."

"Yes."

"That's a list of men who'll want paying."

"Yes."

"And you've no money that I know of, and the mason wants settling for the holding, and I've been told by your father to come to him and not to you." Jory said it all in one go, which was his way, and then looked uncomfortable. "I'm not refusing. I'm telling you what I'll be asked."

"I know what you'll be asked." Casian put his hands in his pockets. "You'll be asked whether I'm spending the barony's money, and the answer is no, and you'll be asked whether I'm spending my own, and the answer is no, and you'll be asked what I am spending and you'll say you don't know, and that will be true and it will be the only comfortable answer you give all winter."

"Master —"

"Jory. When you were twenty-two and a man came up to the Vale wall in the bad year, did you want someone to write a letter?"

Jory's face changed.

"No," he said. "I wanted eleven more men and a dry night."

"Then you know exactly what I'm doing," said Casian, "and you know why I can't put it on a slate, and I'd be grateful if the next thing you say is which road the grain goes out of this county on."

Jory looked at the slate, and then put it in his coat, and said, "Thornwall. It all goes through Thornwall, and it's gone that way since before I was a boy, and no man in this yard has ever asked why."

"Thank you."

"I'll want that written down somewhere before we're done," said Jory, and went out.

---

Supper was a silent meal, and Maren sat down, and nobody ate very much, and Edric came in late and stood at the head of the table and did not sit.

"Nine men," he said. "On Vale land. In daylight. In the middle of the harvest."

"Da —" said Gareth.

"I have not finished." His voice was the one he used for the rent rolls, which was worse than shouting. "I have written to Harven. I have written to Varyn. Harven will write to Varyn and Varyn will write to Harven and in eleven weeks somebody will be told to go and look at the place where it happened. That is the law. It is slow, and it is what we have, and I will not have a son of mine chasing a hundred men into the hills on his own account because his blood is up."

"I wasn't going to —"

"You were. I know your face." Edric put his hands on the chair back. "We build the holding again. We put a wall on the Ashgrove road and a man on the wall. We do not go into the hills this year."

There was a pause. Then Casian, who had said nothing at all since the cart came back, said, "Who says the hills?"

Everyone looked at him.

"You said not this year," said Casian, mildly. "I agree. Not this year."

His father looked at him for a long moment, and in that moment something passed between them that nobody else at the table understood — a warning, delivered and received.

"Good," said Edric. "Then we are agreed."

They were not agreed. Casian ate his supper and was pleasant and asked Brennan about the fair at Harrowmere and did not once look at the empty chair where Mirra had sat when the washing was done and Maren had made her eat.

Afterwards, Maren found him in the passage.

"You'll not go into those hills," she said. It was not a question and it was not the tone she used for questions.

"No."

"Look at me."

He looked at her. She was forty-five, and her hands were red from twenty-six years of hot water, and she had been the person in this house who could always tell when he was lying since he was four years old and had denied eating the last of a thing he had very much eaten.

"Not this year," he said. "I told Da. Not this year."

"That is not what I asked you."

"No," Casian agreed. "It isn't."

Maren Vale stood in the passage with her arms folded and looked at her fourth son for a long moment, and the thing that passed across her face then was not fear, and Casian would spend a great deal of his life not being able to decide what it had been.

"When your father writes his letters," she said, "I want you to know that I know what they're worth. I've known for twenty years. I have never once said so at that table because he needs to believe the law is what we have and he is a better man for believing it." She straightened his collar, which did not need straightening. "But you and I are not going to pretend to each other. So I'll say the one thing and then you'll go to bed. Whatever you do, do it so that I am never sat in a kitchen being told about it by a man with a hat in his hands. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Say it back."

"I'll do it so you hear it from me," said Casian.

His mother looked at him a moment longer, and then nodded, once, and went into the kitchen and shut the door, and Casian stood in the dark passage for a while listening to his mother not crying, which was somehow much worse than if she had been.

Later that night Elna came and found him on the east wall.

"Was it because of the knife?" she said. "Gareth said Jon bought a knife off a man and told him things."

"Yes."

"So it was Jon's fault."

"No." He said it quickly, and then again, more gently. "No. A boy selling knives has one job and it is to get people to talk to him. Jon talked, because he was proud of his house and his horses and because he was nineteen." He looked at his sister. "The fault is entirely with the man who sent the knife."

Elna thought about this.

"Da won't do anything," she said.

"Da will do everything that a good man in his position can do," said Casian, "and it will not be enough, and it will make him ill, and I would like you to be kind to him about it."

"Are you going to do something?"

The honest answer was yes, and it was not available.

"I'm going to make sure it doesn't happen again," he said.

"That's not the same thing."

"No," said Casian, and put his arm round his sister. "It isn't. But it's the thing I can do."
