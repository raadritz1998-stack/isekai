The Harrowmere fair was held nine days after the smoke cleared from the Ashgrove road, and Brennan decided his younger brother was going whether he wanted to or not.

"You have been standing in that walled garden looking at a row of dead briars since Tuesday," Brennan said, cornering Casian by the water-trough before the sun was over the stables. "Mother says you're to be humoured, Da says nothing at all, which from Da is a thunderclap, and Gareth looks like he wants to ride through the gate and hang the first drover he meets. You are coming with me to the river."

"The masons are laying the new foundation for Mirra's cottage today," Casian said, rinsing an old leather halter in the stone basin.

"The masons have four arms and two trowels," Brennan replied, catching the halter by the brass cheek-ring and hanging it on a peg. "You are standing twenty paces away with your chin tucked into your collar, looking like an owl in a hay-loft. Two days on the road, Casian. There is an ale-wife at the butter-cross who bakes an apple turnover with lard and honey that will reform your entire understanding of human virtue."

Casian looked at his brother. Brennan was eighteen years of age, broad in the chest, wearing his second-best surcoat of forest-green wool and calfskin riding boots he had greased with mutton fat until they mirrored the morning clouds. He had his dark hair curled with hot iron, a feather tucked behind his cap, and that effortless, reckless charm that made every tenant in the valley want to give him a cup of cider.

The truth was that Casian had needed an excuse to ride to Harrowmere for eight days. He needed to walk the wool-wharves and the horse-lines without his father asking why the fourth son had developed an appetite for the trade routes.

"Buy me the turnover," Casian said, "and I'll carry your saddle-bags."

---

They rode out together along the river road, where the morning mist hung in long, silver shrouds over the water-meadows.

The road to Harrowmere was twelve miles of packed chalk and river gravel, following the winding course of the Veyr as it broadened toward the lowland shipping cut. In normal years, the road would have been choked with sheep-flocks and harvest wains, the air thick with the shouting of drovers and the dry clatter of ungreased cart-axles. Today, the traffic was thin and cautious. The carters traveled in small convoys of three and four, their drivers carrying iron-shod bills across their knees and looking into every hazel copse as though a troop of upland lances were waiting behind the leaves.

"They're skittish," Brennan observed, reining his bay gelding around a loaded peat-cart.

"Two people were burned in their beds nine miles from here," Casian said from the saddle of his dun pony.

"Aye," Brennan said, his smile fading into a grim line. "And Da writes letters to a Count who won't read them until Michaelmas. If Gareth had his way, we’d have twenty lances burning the gorse off the high ridges by sundown."

"And if Gareth did that," Casian said mildly, "the high valley would send forty lances down the drove to burn the mill. And then we would have no bread for the winter."

Brennan glanced sideways at him, his dark brow arching. "You have an uncomfortably tidy way of looking at a slaughter, little brother."

"I have an uncomfortably tidy way of looking at flour," Casian answered. "A dead raider doesn't grind corn."

Brennan laughed, the tension breaking, and spurred his gelding into a canter along the grassy shoulder.

---

The town of Harrowmere was two thousand people, four sheep-pens to every tavern, a great deal of sour ale, and every debt in the eastern marches walking the turf without its cloak.

It sat at the tidal head of the river, where the inland barges met the sea-cut: a crowded, noisy sprawl of timber warehouses, slate-roofed guildhalls, and muddy alleys reeking of fish-heads, damp wool, and stale beer. The fair had swallowed the open common north of the town ditch, turning three square miles of pasture into an ocean of canvas stalls, livestock hurdles, and shouting merchants.

Casian spent the morning in his brother's shadow.

It was an easy performance, one he had practiced since he was ten: being whatever the room required. In the horse-lines, beside a dealer trying to pass off a wind-broken mare as a sound hackney, Casian was a slow, agreeable boy who asked foolish questions about teeth and looked suitably amazed at the answers. At the pie-seller’s booth, he was a hungry lad who praised the crust and dropped three copper pennies into the flour-bin.

Behind him, Brennan worked the crowds with the skill of a seasoned factor. He clapped sheep-farmers on the back, admired a grazier's wethers, and haggled thirty minutes over a set of silver-plated spurs before walking away with a laugh and a promise to drink with the smith at sunset.

When Brennan drifted toward the falconer’s benches to look at a pair of goshawks, Casian turned toward the wool-wharf.

The wharves were crowded with high-sided flatboats tied to the oak pilings, loading bales of raw fleece wrapped in coarse hemp sacking. At the center of the timber pier, beside a pair of towering iron weighing-beams, a stout merchant in a beaver-fur cap was arguing with a clerk over a pile of notched tally-sticks.

Casian leaned against a stack of empty crates, watching the brass balance swing.

"Fair clip from the upper borders?" Casian asked, his voice casual.

The factor looked up, his brow shiny with sweat, squinting at Casian’s plain grey tunic. "Clip's fair enough. The trade is rotten."

"A high price is generally considered good trade," Casian noted.

"A high price from five buyers is good trade," the factor spat, tucking a wooden tally into his belt. "A high price from one buyer is a noose around your neck. Four summers back, I had clothiers from Redmere bidding three silver groats a stone for Vale fleece. Then a man comes down out of the high cut twice a season with an iron-bound chest, buys every bale on the pier, and pays on the nail in southern coin."

"Where does the coin go?"

"Into my purse, God be praised," the factor said. "And the year after that, two of the Redmere clothiers went elsewhere. This season, not a single southern merchant showed his face at the wharf. So who do I sell to? The man from the hills, or nobody at all. He sets the tally, he names the price, and if I complain, his carters shrug and ride east."

"Who is the buyer?"

"Not the master himself," the factor muttered, leaning over the timber rail and lowering his voice. "A captain for him. Broad-shouldered man on an iron-grey horse. Very quiet. Wears a copper ring in his ear and counts his silver twice. Never shouts, never haggles, and never drinks in the taverns."

*Tolan,* Casian thought. The name settled into the cold recesses of his mind.

"He takes the wool straight east," the factor continued, spitting into the scummy river water. "Up over the high cut and into the Veyr valley. Ten thousand fleeces a year going into a country that produces nothing but sheep-thieves and granite."

"Ten thousand fleeces makes a great deal of cloth," Casian said.

"Aye," the factor grunted, turning back to his scales. "If you have looms. And there isn't a loom between here and the high passes that isn't made of hazel sticks."

---

At the cheese-stalls on the high common, Casian bought a wedge of hard ewe's-milk cheese and lingered in the shade of a brewer's wain.

Three drovers were sitting on empty casks, sharing a skin of cider and listening to a farmer's boy with red hair and mud to his knees.

"—they have thirty men sleeping in a stone barn," the boy was saying, gesturing with a tallow candle. "Not timber and thatch. Squared ashlar with a slate roof, like a castle. And the master pays on the day. My cousin works in his stables. Says he’s never heard the man curse a groom in two years."

"A gentle lord in the high valley?" an older drover scoffed. "There’s no gentle blood north of the Black Crag."

"I didn't say he was gentle," the boy retorted. "I said he was polite. He asks you to sell your pasture once, polite. Then he asks you twice, polite. If you say no the third time, your haystack catches fire while you’re at church, and he buys the ash off your widow for three silver groats."

Casian took a small bite of the cheese.

*He remembers his stable boys' names,* Casian thought. *And he burns the hay when the answer is no.*

"He is establishing an administrative apparatus," Argius noted in his skull. "Disciplined personnel, fixed compensation, systematic supply. He is not behaving like an upland raider. He is behaving like an aspiring territorial sovereign."

*And he knows where our fences are thin.*

---

In the late afternoon, a scuffle broke out by the beer-tents.

Two upland horse-drovers had come to blows over the ownership of a wire-haired lurcher. The crowd surged in a shouting circle, men standing on beer-kegs to watch the two brawny carters roll in the sawdust, trading heavy, clumsy blows with their fists.

Casian stood twenty paces back, leaning against the timber pole of a weaver's booth.

Through the Current, the entire fight was laid out in advance like the movements of a water-wheel: the tensing of the taller man’s shoulder muscles, the shift of weight on his left heel, the wide, telegraphed swing of a clubbed fist aiming for the jaw.

*Four seconds,* Casian thought. *If I tilt the tall man’s heel an inch on the wet grass, his strike goes wide. If I numb the nerve in his forearm, his hand drops. Neither of them would ever know.*

"Intervention is statistically meaningless," Argius murmured. "The combatants possess no strategic relevance. Local authorities are already approaching."

Two town watchmen armed with heavy ash cudgels forced their way through the throng, cracking the brawlers across their shoulders until they rolled apart, cursing and spitting bloody sawdust into the mud.

Casian watched the crowd disperse with a feeling of deep, quiet weight.

*I could stop every fist in this fair,* he thought. *And while I held the fist of a drunken carter, nine riders could burn another byre five miles away.*

"You are perceiving the limitation of local presence," Argius observed. "Omnipotence without comprehensive perception is indistinguishable from absence."

*Then we must build the perception,* Casian thought.

---

He walked to the horse-troughs to wash the cheese from his fingers.

Beneath the noise of the fair, his mind fitted the pieces together.

The factor had said the wool went straight east over the cut in wagons. But there was no highway east of the Veyr valley; the official road ran south-west to Harrowmere, and the mountain tracks were impassable to two-wheeled wains.

*The wool moves in heavy carts,* Casian realized. *Which means there is a road wide enough for two axles that doesn't show on my father’s maps.*

"Analyzing topography," Argius said. "The limestone shelf along the northern Common provides a natural hard-bottom grade. It bypasses the peat bogs, skirts the Fenwick beacons three miles to the north, and connects the upper Veyr valley directly to the Thornwall highway."

*The fifth road,* Casian thought. *A chalk drove that carries wool, slate, and raiders across our border without passing a single watchtower.*

"A blind spot forty leagues wide," Argius confirmed. "Maintained through official neglect."

"Master Vale."

The voice was cheerful, smooth, and entirely unhurried.

Casian looked up.

Rennick stood before the trough, wearing an expensive wool coat dyed saffron-yellow, with polished silver buckles on his turnshoes. He was holding a pair of newly carved ash bowl-troughs under his arm, smiling down at Casian with the pleasant, predatory ease of a merchant who owned half the debts in the valley.

"Master Rennick," Casian said, not standing up from the coping.

"I didn't expect to see the master of the Vale mill eating sheep-pies at a fair," Rennick said, stepping closer. His eyes were small, pale, and thoroughly amused. "My clerk tells me you walked into my Thornwall shop with four hundred and twelve marks in coin and didn't even ask for a discount on the interest."

"You had the paper," Casian said mildly. "I had the silver."

Rennick let out a short, comfortable chuckle. "Aye. You had the silver. An inheritance from your grandmother, they tell me. An expensive hobby, Master Casian. Buying broken water-wheels for widows who can't pay two groats on the quarter."

"The wheel turns."

"For now," Rennick said. His smile remained genial, but his gaze sharpened, pinning Casian like a specimen on an apothecary’s board. "You’re a clever lad. The quiet one, they call you down at Vale. The boy who gardens."

He leaned down slightly, lowering his voice beneath the clamour of the horse-dealers.

"There is a great deal of money moving out of the hills, Master Vale," Rennick murmured. "Much more than four hundred marks. And the men who hold the paper for it are very careful people. They don't mind an eager boy spending his legacy to feel like a lord. But they remember faces. And they do not like young men who build fences across their roads."

Casian took a slow bite of his apple turnover.

"Was that a threat, Master Rennick?"

"Bless you, no," Rennick laughed, straightening his coat. "An observation. From one careful man to another. Enjoy your fair, lad. Give my respects to your father."

He turned and melted into the crowd of weavers and sheep-factors, his yellow surcoat visible for fifty paces before vanishing behind the beer-tents.

Casian chewed slowly.

*He thinks I'm a boy playing at commerce,* Casian thought.

"A reasonable deduction based on available evidence," Argius stated. "You are sixteen, you expended your liquid capital on a single non-performing asset, and you offered no resistance to his usury."

*Let him think it,* Casian said, dusting the pastry crumbs from his fingers. *It will keep him in Thornwall while we close the road.*

---

The ride home was quiet.

Brennan had sold his gelding for two silver marks above market value and spent the first three miles bragging about his trade. But as dusk settled over the Common, the wind turned sharp, smelling of autumn frost and drying heather.

A mile short of the Vale postern, Casian reined his pony in at the crest of the chalk rise.

To the south, the Vale chimneys showed dark against the purple twilight, two pale tallow lights glowing in the solar windows. Five miles to the east, the black scar of the Ashgrove holding was invisible in the dark, but Casian could feel the empty hollow where the hearth had stood.

Brennan halted beside him, glancing sideways at his brother's still face.

"You've been strange all afternoon," Brennan said, his usual mocking banter dropping away into something closer to real concern. "You were pleasant to the factors, you ate your turnover, and you looked at the horses. But you look like a man counting graves."

"I am counting roads," Casian said quietly.

Brennan frowned, staring out across the empty heather. "There are only four roads out of this county, Casian. Gareth patrols three of them, and Da pays tolls on the fourth."

"There is a fifth," Casian said, turning his pony down the slope toward the gate. "And it runs right past our beds."

---

That night, after the manor had surrendered to the heavy breathing of sleeping men and the twitching of hounds, Casian went to the walled garden.

He carried a small horn-lantern and a narrow, leather-bound blank book he had bought from a peddler at the fair. He knelt by the corner of the east terrace, lifted a loose coping stone from the wall, and pulled out an ink-horn and a goose quill.

By the yellow light of the tallow candle, he opened the book to its first blank leaf and began to write:

*Four roads known to the King’s sheriff. One drove over the chalk Common, known only to the hills.*

*Wool, slate, and plunder move east along the drove. Raiders strike west along the highway in daylight.*

*The Ashgrove road is unwatched because Harven writes letters and Edric waits for law.*

*Conclusion: I cannot rely on thirty lances to guard twenty leagues of border.*

He paused, the quill hovering above the rough paper.

He remembered Mirra lying on the salting table, her scorched grey hair and her blackened hands. He remembered Tolan notching thirty-one into his tally tablet. He remembered Rennick’s yellow surcoat and his smug, condescending smile by the horse-trough.

He could act through the Current from any distance. He could close a road, crush a heart, or wipe an army from a hillside without rising from his bench.

But power was blind without knowledge. If he did not know the raiders were riding, his godhood was worth no more than a dry leaf in a gale.

*Anything I know about, I can do,* he thought, the truth settling into his bones like cold iron. *The entire difficulty is the knowing.*

"Record that statement," Argius murmured in the dark of his skull. "It is the foundational axiom of your operational doctrine."

Casian dipped the quill and wrote the words across the page in clean, bold strokes:

*Anything I know about, I can do. The difficulty is the knowing.*

He looked at the single line on the white paper.

*We need eyes,* he thought. *On the roads. In the markets. At the river-crossings.*

"That requires personnel," Argius observed. "Alliances of men require coin, loyalty, and vigilance. They are vulnerable to fear, greed, and error."

Casian blew on the wet ink and closed the book.

*Then we will choose people who have already lost everything,* he thought, slipping the ledger into the hollow beneath the stone coping. *Starting with the fence at Harrowmere.*
