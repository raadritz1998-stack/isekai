The Harrowmere fair was nine days after the burning, and Brennan wanted to go, and Brennan wanted Casian to come, and Brennan did not explain either of these things because Brennan never explained anything and got away with it more often than was good for him.

"You've not been off this land in a month," he said, in the yard. "You've been sitting in that garden looking at a wall. Mother says you're to be humoured and Da says nothing at all, which from Da means a great deal."

"It means the holding gets rebuilt and I'm building it."

"The masons are building it. You are standing near it." Brennan put a hand on the cart. "Two days. There's a woman at Harrowmere who makes a thing with apples that will change your understanding of the world."

The truth was that Casian had needed a reason to go to Harrowmere for eight days and had not been able to invent one that would survive his father's attention, and his brother had handed him one for free.

"I'll buy you a pie," he said.

---

The fair was two hundred people, four sheep contests, a great deal of beer, and every debt in the eastern marches walking around without its coat on.

Casian had known this about fairs since he was twelve and had never once been able to make anyone else see it. A fair was where the counties settled their accounts — not with money, or not only, but with information. Who looked well. Who was selling a horse and pretending not to. Whose daughter was being introduced and to what, exactly. Whose son had come home from the east with a limp and no story.

He spent the morning being Brennan.

That was the phrase he used for it and had used since he was a boy: being whatever the room needed, in the way that a good coat was whatever the weather needed. It was not lying, is what he would have told you, if anyone had asked. It was more like being usefully shaped.

At the apple woman's stall he was a boy who could not tell a good apple from a bad one and was pleased to be told. At the wool factor's he was the fourth son of a small barony who knew nothing about wool and asked a great many questions and laughed at his own ignorance, and the wool factor, who was a bored man with a long afternoon, told him all of it: where the best clip went, who was buying, and how the trade had changed in the last four years.

"And the price?" said Casian.

"The price is good and it's been good four years and I'll tell you what nobody in this fair will tell you, which is that a good price is worse than a bad one for a man like me." The factor drank. "In a bad year I sell to four buyers and I know all four. In a good year a man comes down out of the hills with coin and buys the lot in one morning and I don't ask where the coin came from because I'd be a fool, and the next year there's three buyers and not four, and the year after that there's one."

"So you're being bought."

"I'm being *eased*," said the factor, with the bitterness of a man who has thought about it a great deal and had nobody to say it to. "There's a difference and it's the difference between being robbed and being told to go home. And the worst of it is the coin is good coin. I've had it assayed. It's honest silver and I've no complaint I could take to a magistrate and a magistrate would laugh at me if I did."

Casian turned his cup around.

"Who's the man who comes down out of the hills?"

"Not him. A man for him." The factor shrugged. "Big fellow on a grey, very quiet, polite. Pays, counts, doesn't drink. If you find a man in this county who's spoken to him twice I'll give you a fleece."

*Tolan,* said Casian, and did not say it out loud.

"Noted," said Argius.

"He's on the road out of the hills every fortnight," said the factor, "and I've watched him do it four years and it's the only reason I still have a trade, and I'd not like him to be arrested, if it's all the same to you."

"It's all the same to me," said Casian.

"Twenty years ago it all went south," the factor said. "Now it goes east. Straight over the cut and up into the hills and I could not tell you who has it when it gets there and I have asked twice and been told to mind my business twice."

"Which hills?"

"The upper Veyr. That'll be the buyer's buyer's buyer, mind. It's four hands gone by the time it stops moving."

At the cheese stall he was nobody at all, which was the best thing to be, and he spent twenty minutes there because the cheese woman's son had come back from the east and was telling three men about it.

"— and they're building," the son said. "Not a farm. A hall. There's a wall round it and there's a gatehouse and there's thirty men sleeping in a barn that's got a stone floor, which I have never in my life seen for a barn."

"Whose is it?"

"Nobody's," said the son, "and that's the thing I can't get anyone to hear. There's no lord up that valley. There's Harven, and Harven's a hundred miles off, and there's a man in a hall with thirty men and a stone floor in his barn, and if you ask who he is they say he's the man that bought the land and if you ask what he is they say he's the man that bought the land."

"And what's he like?"

"He's polite," said the son, in a way that made two of the three men laugh and one of them stop laughing. "I mean it. He's polite and he pays on the day and he remembers your name. My cousin's boy works in his stable and says he's never been shouted at in two years. Said it like it was a good thing."

"It is a good thing."

"It's a good thing if you're a stable boy," said the son. "It's not a good thing if you're a man with a farm up that valley who's been asked, polite, three times, whether he'd like to sell."

Casian bought a cheese he did not want and moved on, and thought about the difference between a man who shouts at his stable boys and a man who remembers their names, and found that he did not care for the answer he arrived at.

*He's careful,* he thought.

"He is careful," said Argius. "You are careful. You will meet men who mistake the two, and one of them will be you."

At the horse lines he was a boy who liked horses, which was not a lie. He walked the whole line twice. He noted which beasts had been bred where, which are cheap and which are dear and which have been standing there all day and are being sold by a man who does not own them.

*That grey there.*

"East-bred," said Argius. "Very. Same country as the steward's horse in Thornwall."

*Mark it.*

"Marked."

---

By the afternoon the sun had gone off the square and Casian was sitting on a wall eating a pie that was, as promised, a genuine event in the history of apples, when he found the road.

It was not a road. That was the thing he almost missed and would have missed if he had not spent a fortnight looking at his own thoughts sideways instead of at them.

There were four ways out of the eastern hills and everybody knew them: the Ashgrove road going west, the upper track going north to Harven, the drovers' route to the Common, and the river ford where the Veyr ran shallow in summer. Any man who looked at a map would see those four and would set a watch on them, and Casian had already, three nights before, worked out where he would set the watches.

But the wool did not go out of the hills. That was what the factor had said without knowing he had said it. Twenty years ago it went south and now it went east, straight over the cut and up into the hills —

*East,* said Casian. *There's no road east out of the Veyr valley. There's a pass.*

He looked out at the Common, at the way it rolled, at the smoke of a village four miles off, and he understood something so simple it was almost embarrassing.

*There's a road that isn't a road. It's a drove over the top and it comes out into the Common three miles north of Fenwick, where there is no village and no toll and nobody at all.*

"Confirmed," said Argius. "My estimate agrees with yours. It has the advantage of being a route that carries goods and not men, so no one counts it."

*They can leave the hills in secret. They can be at Fenwick in a night.*

"Yes."

*And then they can be anywhere in this county by dawn.*

"Yes." A pause. "That has always been true. What is new is that you now know it, and you are the first man in a generation to write it down."

Casian sat on the wall in the sun with a half-eaten pie and was very still for a moment, the way a man is still when he has just found out that the house he lives in has no floor.

---

The thing he had not expected, at the fair, was to be recognised.

He was at the joiners' stalls in the late afternoon, being a boy who was interested in wood and knew nothing about it, when a man in a good coat stopped in front of him and said, "You'll be Vale."

"Casian Vale."

"Rennick." The man put out his hand. He was about forty-five, well fed, with the specific careful cheerfulness of a man who has decided in advance to be liked. "I had a paper off your father's mill. It was bought out from under me in the spring, and I've been wondering ever since who had the money for it and why they'd want a mill that can't pay."

"Was it a good paper?"

"It was an excellent paper," said Rennick. "It was the best paper I've had in three years and I'd have had the mill by spring and the race with it."

"Then you'll have to find another mill."

Rennick laughed, which Casian noted, because nothing funny had been said.

"You're a cool one," he said. "I've asked about you. They say you're the quiet one and I've been in this county eleven years and I know what the quiet one usually means." He leaned in a fraction, still cheerful. "I'll say a thing and then I'll leave you to your wood. There's a great deal of money coming into these parts from the east, and the men who hold the paper for it are careful men and they don't forget a face. Not a threat. Just an observation, from one careful man to another."

He went off into the crowd.

Casian stood at the joiners' stall holding a piece of oak he had no use for and looked at the man's back.

*He's not afraid of me.*

"He has no reason to be," said Argius. "You are sixteen and you have bought one mill."

*He thinks he's just met a clever boy who cost him some money.*

"Yes."

*And in a month he'll be telling people he met me and I was charming.*

"Almost certainly."

*Good,* said Casian, and put the oak down, and found that the whole of his afternoon had changed shape around that single conversation, in a way he did not entirely like.

---

The other thing at the fair was a fight.

It happened outside the beer tent in the late afternoon and it was over in a minute, and it was between two drovers over a dog, and it was not a serious fight and nobody was hurt past a split lip. Half the fair watched it and half the fair pretended not to and the two men were drinking together by evening.

Casian watched it from twenty feet away and saw the whole of it happen in a way that made him feel very old.

*Nine seconds,* he thought. *He was going to hit him at the fourth and then he didn't because his friend put a hand out. If the friend had been a second later I could have stopped it and nobody would have seen anything and they'd both have thought they'd changed their minds.*

"That is true of every fight in the eastern marches," said Argius.

*I know.*

"You are discovering the difference between being able to stop a thing and being there to stop it. It is a difference that will occupy the rest of your life."

*And if the friend had been a second earlier?*

"Then nothing happened, and you would never have known, and it would not have mattered." A pause. "That is the arithmetic of a man who believes he is responsible for everything he can see. It is a bad arithmetic. You are going to do it anyway."

---

"You've gone strange," said Brennan, later, on the road home. "You've been strange all day. You were pleasant to everybody and then you were strange."

"That's the most accurate description of me anyone's ever made."

"Don't be clever at me, I'm your brother."

Casian laughed, properly, and looked at Brennan, who was eighteen and rode like a man who had never once worried about a horse and had the gift of making people want to tell him things.

"Brennan. Who do you know on the Ashgrove road?"

"Everybody."

"Who do you trust?"

"Nobody," said Brennan, with enormous cheerfulness. "But that's not the same as not liking them. I trust you, if it helps. I lent you money and didn't ask why, and I've been wondering about it ever since, mostly about how you paid it back in a week."

"I sold two horses."

"You have three horses and one of them is the horse you're riding."

"Two of them were mine and one was Da's."

"I'm not asking," said Brennan. "I'm saying I'd like to be the kind of brother who gets told things before they happen, and I want that noted, and I want you to know I'll bring it up again at a bad moment."

"You always do."

"It's the only way to get anything from you," Brennan said, and swung up onto his horse, and they went home in warm weather past fields that had been cut and fields that had not been, and Casian, who had spent the whole day asking questions, spent the whole ride back not answering any.

---

They came home in the dark, and there was a moment on the road, a mile short of the Vale wall, when Casian stopped his horse.

Brennan stopped too, because he always did.

"What."

"Nothing. A thing I'm doing."

"You do it three times a week now," said Brennan. "You stop on the road and look at nothing and then you go on. You want to know what it looks like? It looks like a man checking that his house is still there."

"That's exactly what it is."

"Your house is a mile away and you can see the light in the window."

"I know," said Casian, and they went on.

He did not say the thing he was thinking, which was that the light in the window was all he could see and that a mile away from it a woman's yard had burned nine days ago while he was sitting on a bench with a pair of shears in his hand. He did not say that he had started checking, every night, in a way that had no name, that the country between the ridge and the wall was still the country he had left that morning.

It was, this evening. The Vale fields, the mill, the race, the people in the house and the four awake and the dogs. All of it exactly where he had put it.

He thought that if he did this every night for a year he might begin to believe it, and was honest enough with himself to know that he would not.

That night he wrote the first of the things he would eventually have ten years' worth of, in a book that lived behind a stone in the east wall.

*Four ways out of the hills. Five, if you count the drove over the Common, which nobody counts.*

*Men who trade leave by the drove. Men who raid come by the Ashgrove road, in daylight, in August.*

*The Ashgrove road is watched by nobody because there is nobody to watch it. Harven is a hundred miles away and answers letters in eleven weeks.*

*So: a man on the Ashgrove road. Not a soldier. A man who can see.*

He sat back and looked at what he had written, and it was so small. One man, on one road, in one county, in one country. A boy of sixteen who could stop a heart would spend the autumn thinking about how to place a man with a lantern.

He could, of course, do it himself.

That was the thing he made himself look at directly, in the dark, with the ink still wet, because it was the thing he was going to have to keep looking at for the rest of his life. He could sit in this garden and know every road in the eastern marches at once, and he could know a wagon was coming up the drove before it came, and he could stop the men on it, one after another, the way you put out candles.

And he would still not know that a woman was going to be burned in her own yard at noon on a Tuesday, because he had not been looking at that yard, because there were four hundred yards and one of him.

*Anything I know about, I can do,* he thought. *The whole of the difficulty is the knowing. It has always been the knowing.*

"Say that again."

*Anything I know about, I can do.*

"That is the most useful sentence you have thought all summer," said Argius. "Write it down."

And he did, underneath, in his own hand, and it looked like a boy's maxim in a boy's book, and it was the true root of everything that came after.

*This is not a great deal,* he thought.

"It is the first thing you have done that could be taken away from you by weather," said Argius.

*What?*

"It could fail. The man could be deaf, or drunk, or fearful, or bought. You have just built something that has to be trusted, and you have never had to trust anything in your life."

Casian put the stopper in the ink.

*No,* he said. *I suppose I haven't.*
