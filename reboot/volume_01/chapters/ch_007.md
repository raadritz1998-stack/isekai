Ansel Trask kept a shop in Harrowmere that sold nothing, and had sold nothing for eleven years, and was the most prosperous man on that street.

The shop had a window with three objects in it that never changed: a brass buckle, a knife with a cracked handle, and a length of grey cloth. Men went in. Men came out. Nobody ever carried anything.

Casian had walked past it perhaps two hundred times in his life and had understood roughly nothing about it, and then in the third week of August he had sat in the fair and watched who went in, and by the end of the afternoon he had understood all of it.

"Ansel Trask," he said, in the yard, to Brennan.

"Don't."

"Don't what?"

"Don't do whatever it is you're going to do. He's been in that shop since before we were born and there's a reason he sells nothing, and the reason is that he is not a shop." Brennan was not smiling. "He does the paper in this county. All of it. If a man in Harrowmere cannot pay, he goes to Ansel, and Ansel finds the man who holds the debt, or becomes the man who holds it. He knows what every farm between here and the hills is worth down to the last rotted fence post. He has never once been cheated, and men have tried, and I do not know what happened to them and I have never wanted to."

"So he's a fence."

"He's the fence," said Brennan. "You don't walk into a man like that with a purse. He'll have your purse and your coat and the wall you're standing next to and you'll thank him."

"Then I won't bring a purse."

---

He went on the twenty-fourth of August, in the afternoon, when the light in a shop with nothing in the window is very bad and a man behind a counter cannot see the face of a boy standing in front of it.

Ansel Trask was sixty and looked sixty in a way that suggested he had looked sixty for a decade and intended to look it for another. He had small hands and clean nails and the specific stillness of a man who has spent his life listening to things being said by people who did not know they were saying them.

"Master Casian Vale," he said. "Sit down. Or don't. I'd rather you didn't, if you're going to be tiresome."

"I'm going to be tiresome."

"Then sit down, it's better for my knees." Ansel came round the counter and lowered himself into the only chair. "Your brother Brennan I like. Your brother Gareth I respect. Your father I have never done business with, which is a thing I consider to be to his credit. You I have heard of twice in a fortnight, which for a fourth son of a small barony is a remarkable rate."

"From who?"

"Now why would I tell you that?"

"Because you've already decided what to do with me," said Casian, "and the only question is the price."

Ansel's expression did not change, which Casian noted, because a man whose expression does not change when he is surprised is a man who has practised.

"Go on," said Ansel.

"You hold paper in this county. I want to know whose. Not to buy it. To know whose it is."

"That is worth a great deal more than paper."

"I know. I'm not going to pay you money for it."

"Then we have nothing to say to one another and the door is behind you."

"Sit down," said Casian. "You've been sitting for a minute and you haven't told me to leave, and the door is the same door it was, and you're a man who would have had me out of here the moment I stopped being interesting. So let me tell you what I have, and then you tell me if it's worth it."

Ansel put his hands on his knees.

"Before you speak," he said, "I'll tell you the thing you don't know, and then you'll tell me whether you still want to be in this shop.

"I started at seventeen with a paper on one man. One. He owed my father eight marks and my father was dying and I went to the man and stood in his door and was afraid and he paid me out of pity, and I have never in forty-three years taken another mark out of pity and I have never once let a debt lie past the third quarter.

"There's two hundred men in this county who owe somebody, and forty of them owe somebody who is not in this county, and I know all forty by name and I know what they'll do before they do it. That is not a small thing to know and it is not a thing I have ever given away.

"You want to know who's buying the east. If I tell you, and it goes wrong, then the men who are buying the east will know that Ansel Trask told a boy, and there is no corner of this county I can hide in from that." He straightened. "So. Why should I?"

"Because they've already bought you," said Casian.

Ansel's face did not move at all.

"You're sixty," Casian said, "and your paper in Thornwall is not the paper you had four years ago, because four years ago it was yours and now there's a man with a better purse and you're the one who finds him the borrowers. You're not the fence any more. You're the clerk. You've been the clerk for two years and you know it and it has been eating you alive and no one in this county has noticed because you're the one everyone's afraid of."

The silence went on for a while.

"You are sixteen," said Ansel, at last, "and you have just said the only true thing anyone has said to me since the year my wife died."

"Then tell me the eleven names."

"I'll tell you the eleven names," said Ansel Trask, "and then we will never speak of today again, and you will pay me twice a year for the rest of my life, and I'll be honest with you about why: because when I die I would like one man in this county to be able to explain what I was."

"Speak."

---

"I know about the eleven shops."

There was a small silence.

"On the market street at Thornwall," Casian went on, "there are eleven shops that carry paper. Six of them are carrying paper that came from a factor who doesn't live there. I know that because I spent an afternoon in the square. It's in the way they keep their books in the window and in the fact that six of them have a new lock on the same kind of door. It took me an afternoon and I'm sixteen and I know nothing about paper.

"You've known for two years," said Casian. "You knew before I did. You knew when the first one opened and you knew which of them is the best and which is money on top of money. And you have done nothing about it, and I want to know why."

Ansel looked at him for a long, unreadable moment.

"Because it is not worth my while," he said.

"Because you can't prove it's a problem," Casian said. "Because paper that comes from somewhere is not against anything. Because if you go to Harven with it, Harven says what you've said, so what, and if you go to the court you spend more than the paper is worth. It's not a crime. It's just a great many men suddenly borrowing from the same hand and nobody in a position to say the hand's name."

"That is very good," said Ansel, "for a boy who has been in this shop for four minutes."

"I've been in this county for sixteen years." Casian sat down on the counter's edge, which Ansel permitted by not objecting. "Here's the thing I have. There's a holding on the Ashgrove road that burned nine days ago and two people in it died, and there is a man in the upper Veyr valley with a hundred men and no title, and there is a steward at Thornwall on a horse bred in that valley, and there is a wool clip going east over the Common by a drove road that isn't a road, and there is a miller in this barony who will show me her book twice a quarter."

Ansel said nothing at all.

"That's not paper," said Casian. "That's the whole of the east in one sentence, and it's in my head and nowhere else in the world, and you're the only man in this county who would understand the value of a thing that isn't written down."

"You want a partner," said Ansel.

"No. I want a fence." Casian met his eye. "You'd be a terrible partner. You'd want a share and I'd have to argue with you and you'd win because you've been doing this for forty years and I haven't. I don't want to argue with you. I want to buy something from you and I want to do it once."

"And what is this one thing?"

"A name. The name of the man Hask Veyr buys his friends with, and the name of the friend, and the name of the friend's man at Thornwall."

Ansel Trask sat back in his chair.

"You've just asked me for the most dangerous sentence in the eastern marches," he said.

"Yes."

"And what will you pay?"

"Nothing," said Casian.

Ansel looked at him for a while, and then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a dry, quiet, genuinely amused sound, and his whole face changed with it.

"Nothing," he repeated.

"Nothing tonight. I'll pay you twice a year for the rest of your life for what I'm going to ask you after this. But for the name I'm going to pay you nothing at all and you're going to give it to me anyway, because you've had eleven years of a shop that sells nothing and a fortnight ago a woman was burned in her own yard, and because you're sixty and you have never once in your life been on the same side as the ones who pay with paper, and it's starting to show."

Ansel sat very still.

Then he got up, went to the back of the shop, and came back with a book.

"Eleven names," he said. "That's what you asked for before you came in, isn't it. That's what you actually walked in here for." He opened it. "You'll want to write them yourself. I don't do copies and I'm not writing it twice and if you lose the paper I'll deny the whole of this conversation and I'll do it with a straight face."

He read them out slowly, so that a boy could write them down, and he did not editorialise, and once or twice he spelt a name.

Casian wrote them down.

Eleven names. Nine of them meant nothing to him. One of them was Osric, Baron of Thornwall, and under it Ansel had written a date in a careful hand, four years old.

And the last was a man called Rennick, who held a mill.

---

It took an hour and by the end of it Casian's hand was cramped and Ansel had talked himself into a mood that was almost cheerful.

"Read it back to me," said Ansel.

Casian read it back.

"Wrong," said Ansel, at the fourth name. "You've written 'Corbel' and it's 'Corble'. There's a Corbel in Harrowmere and he's a wheelwright and he's never owed anybody a mark in his life, and if you go asking after Corbel you'll be laughed out of the town and the man you want will hear about it inside a day." He sat back. "That's the whole trade, boy. Not the names. The half of a name you got wrong."

Casian corrected it.

"Now say the names to me in a different order."

"Why?"

"Because a list is a thing a man can be shown and a sentence is a thing a man has to remember, and one of them you can lose in a fire and the other one you can't." Ansel watched him. "In order. By who they answer to."

So Casian said them, out loud, in the order of who they answered to, twice, and on the second time Ansel nodded and said, "Now forget my shop."

"I'm not going to forget your shop."

"Forget it," said Ansel, "because if a man comes in here in a year and says the Vale boy sent me, I'll say I've never heard of him, and I'd like that to be true in a way that would satisfy a man who's holding a knife."

---

He walked out into the light and stood in the street

"Now the other thing," said Ansel, and closed the book. "You said you'd pay me twice a year for what you're going to ask. So ask."

"You know every road in this county."

"I know every debt on every road in this county, which is better."

"I want a man on the Ashgrove road," said Casian. "Not a soldier. A man with a lantern and a good pair of eyes and no reason for anyone to suspect him, and I want him there every night for a year."

"With a lantern."

"With a lantern, a dog, and a story that makes sense to his neighbours."

Ansel's brows went up a fraction.

"You understand what you're asking for," he said. "A man on that road is a man who will see things, and a man who sees things will want to be told what to do about them, and you will not be there. You will be in a garden eleven miles off, and the road will go by, and the wagon will come, and your man will have an hour to decide something and you will find out about it a day late."

"I know."

"Then say it properly, because I've sold a good many men into a good many arrangements and the road is paved with the ones who weren't told the truth about what they'd be by themselves."

"He will be by himself," said Casian. "He'll see a thing and not be able to ask me. And the first time that happens he'll either do the right thing and find out he's better than he thought, or he'll do nothing and be ashamed, and either way he'll be a different man by spring than the one I hired in the autumn."

Ansel looked at him with something that was almost respect and not quite comfortable.

"And you're sixteen," he said again, mostly to himself, and reached for his book.

"That'll be an innkeeper," he said. "Or it'll be a man who looks like one."

"Can you find me one?"

"Can I find you one." Ansel put the book back under the counter. "Boy, I have paper on forty men in this county and half of them are worth less than a man who owns a bed on a road. Finding you one is not the difficulty. The difficulty is that you're sixteen and you have no name and no title and no men, and the sort of man you're describing is not the sort of man who takes an order from a boy."

"He wouldn't be taking an order from a boy. He'd be taking something else."

"What?"

"I'd pay him," said Casian, "and I'd be straight with him, and there'd be one of me and not four, and in ten years he'd be telling people about the year the Vale boy came and put a light on the Ashgrove road." He shrugged. "That's worth more than a soldier's wage to the right man. It's worth most of a life."

Ansel Trask looked at him for a long moment with the flat professional attention of a man weighing an object he is certain he can sell.

"Come back in a week," he said. "Bring money, that time."

---

---

He walked out into the light and stood in the street for a moment with his hand on his horse's neck.

Behind him, in the shop, Ansel Trask sat down again in the only chair and did not move for some minutes.

He was sixty years old and he had spent forty-three of them arranging other men's affairs so that nobody could ever say he had arranged them. He had a book under the counter with eleven names in it and a second book with two hundred, and he had just read out nine of the first eleven to a boy of sixteen with ink on his fingers, and the boy had written them down without asking a single question about what they would cost.

That was the part Ansel could not get past. A man of his own age would have asked what it would cost.

The boy had asked what it would *take*.

"Morag," said Ansel, to nobody. "You'd have liked him and he'd have ruined us both."

Then he got up and put the book back under the counter and put the kettle on, because it was the afternoon and there was nothing in the window and no reason for anyone to come in.

*That was the first time I have ever been in a room with a man who could beat me at something.*

"He did not beat you."

*He let me win.* Casian swung up. *He could have had me out of that shop in a minute and he wanted to see what I'd do instead. He's spent forty years learning how to make a boy say more than he meant to and I walked in there with my father's name and a piece of gossip about locks.*

"He gave you the names."

*He gave me the names because I was the first person in two years who'd spoken to him as though he might be something other than a fence.*

"That is how people are bought," said Argius. "You will remember this one, because one day you will do it to somebody who is very old and very tired, and you will not notice yourself doing it, and it will be the first thing you do that you would have called monstrous in someone else."

Casian rode three streets before he stopped shaking.

*That,* said Argius, *was the most dangerous thing you have done.*

*He's an old bookseller.*

"He is an old bookseller the way a mill is a shed. He gave you eleven names in an hour and he is going to think about it tonight and he is going to realise that he has just handed a boy a weapon, and there are two ways that goes."

*He'll want more money.*

"Or he sells the fact that the Vale boy is buying. Which of those is worse?"

Casian sat on his horse in the middle of a street in Harrowmere and considered, for the first time since the burning, that he might have done something stupid.

*Both,* he said. *Both are worse. And I'd do it again.*

"I know," said Argius. "That is the difficulty and it is not going to get easier. You have just discovered what it is like to need a person you cannot compel."

*He's not my friend.*

"No," said Argius. "He is worse. He is a man who has decided you are interesting, and men who decide that about you will always be the ones worth being afraid of."

*I'm not afraid of him.*

"You are afraid of being large in a room," said Argius, "and that is a much better fear than the other kind. Keep it."
