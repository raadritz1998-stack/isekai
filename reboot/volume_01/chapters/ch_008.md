Corin Ahl had been an armsman at Vale for eighteen years, which was two years longer than the boy who came to find him had been alive.

He had come to the gate at seventeen, in the autumn of the bad frost, with a bundle of spare linen, three silver pennies from his uncle, and a spear with an ash shaft he had peeled himself. He had stood sentry through eleven months of sleet without a word of complaint. By twenty-four he was the man Captain Jory sent when a tenant refused his tithe or a steer had to be fetched out of the marsh without disturbing the border ditch. By thirty he had turned down three offers from larger houses in the North Riding and the rich river pastures near Harven.

When people asked why, Corin told them he liked the country. It was true, but it was not the whole reason.

The reason was that Corin Ahl was a very good soldier in a very small barony, which meant he had spent eighteen years being given tasks an alert boy could do with a stick.

He did them without flaw. In eighteen years he had never left a latch unfastened, never let oats rot under a leaky gutter, and never lost an argument with a carter. He had never once been handed a duty that demanded every scrap of his mind and blood, and after thirty he had stopped expecting one. He had settled into the routine as a heavy hound settles into a clean kennel: turning around three times, lying down with his muzzle on his paws, and keeping his ears toward the gate.

He was thirty-five years old. His hair had turned the color of dry salt, but his shoulders were thick, his knees sound, and he had a wife, two daughters, and four rooms under cedar shingles behind the lower brew-house.

His wife was Ivet, and she was the sharper half of their marriage. She had told him, eight summers back while mending a surcoat: "You are a large man in a small place, Corin. The place will not widen because you wish it, and you will not shrink to match it. Decide what to do with your hands before you are fifty, or you will turn into an old bailiff who hates every young man with a sword, and I will not live with a bitter man."

Corin had looked at his scarred hands, said, "Aye," and gone down to lock the outer stables.

Their daughters were fourteen and eleven. Miri, the elder, had her mother's level stare. Lyra, the younger, had decided three seasons ago that her father was the most remarkable creature in the county. Every evening when Corin set his leather jerkin over the stool, Lyra rested her chin upon her knuckles and asked what had walked through the gate. He answered every question, in order, without shortening the tale, because he knew that the day a man grew too tired to tell his child about an iron shoe was the day he became an old man at a table.

---

The boy came to him on the last evening of August, by the wicket in the east wall, just as the swallows dropped toward the reed beds.

Corin was rubbing tallow into a stiff stirrup leather when the shadow fell across his bench—light, even, deliberate, the gait of someone who never hurried.

"Corin," said Casian. "Walk along the wall with me."

Corin set the tallow pot down. In the Vale yard you did not snap at the baron's sons, though with Aldric you kept your chin up, with Brennan you kept your purse tucked, and with Gareth you kept your sword arm ready. With Casian you simply waited. The fourth son had an unsettling habit of arriving without noise and leaving without leaving an impression, like water seeping under a sill.

They walked thirty paces along the curtain wall, past the wood stacks, stopping where the wall turned north toward the orchard gate. Below them the valley opened into dusk, purple and grey, the drovers' path winding toward the High Crag.

Casian rested both hands on the cool lime-wash of the parapet and looked into the gathering dark. Corin watched his profile. It was a young face, clean and smooth, but the mouth was shut with a steady weight that did not belong to sixteen summers. There was no fidgeting in the boy.

"I want a wagon driven down the Ashgrove road on Thursday," Casian said lightly. "Empty. Broad daylight. Two horses, an ordinary hay-wain. It should stop at Mirra Cott's burned holding for an hour, then turn around and come home at a walk."

Corin tilted his head. "To show the Vale banner on the road?"

"No," Casian said. "For the men who burned the holding."

Corin went still. "You'd better tell Captain Jory that."

"I don't want Jory told," the boy said softly. "Not first."

Corin turned toward him. "Now look here, Master Casian. I've taken bread from your father's board for eighteen years. I came into this yard when Aldric was in swaddling clothes. I've never taken a duty behind Jory's back."

"It isn't a game," Casian said, as plain as salt. "There were nine riders on the Ashgrove road twelve days ago. They came down off the Common by the old turf drove, which nobody watches. If those men are watching the road—and they are—they will see an empty wagon trundle down to Mirra's yard and turn around. They will think the bailiff is taking inventory of charred beams. They will think Vale is slow, blind, and habit-bound."

Corin's jaw tightened. "And what does that buy us?"

"Patience," Casian said. "A wagon on Thursday. Another wagon next Thursday. An empty wain rolling down the lane every seven days until it becomes part of the landscape, like the rookery or the ditch. Men who watch an empty wagon week after week get careless. They leave their horses tied lower down the gully. And when the wagon finally carries something they want, we will know where their camp sits before they reach for their girths."

Corin stared at him. The tactical shape of it hit him between the ribs like the butt of a lance. It was not a squire's notion, nor Gareth's noisy bravado. It was patient, methodical, and bloodless—how an old garrison captain in the border marches set a snare when he had five men to cover twelve miles of heather.

"It's bait," Corin murmured.

"A very small bait," corrected Casian. "And I cannot drive a wagon down that road without an armsman on the bench, or the men in the hills will know it is a trick."

Corin looked down at his broad palms. "We go to Jory. And we go to your father. I will not drive an inch of that lane without the baron's word."

Casian gave him a small, faint smile, entirely warm. "Fair enough. Let us go to the study."

---

The study was warm and smelled of beeswax, dried parchment, and old wool. A tallow candle guttered in an iron sconce over the desk where Baron Edric sat with his linen shirt unlaced.

Edric looked fifty, though he was forty-nine. The silver in his beard had deepened since the smoke had risen over Ashgrove, his eyes clouded with the dull ache of a lord whose border has been scratched and who cannot afford a war.

Jory was already there, leaning against the wainscoting with his thumbs tucked into his sword-belt. When Corin walked in with Casian half a pace behind, Jory's eyebrows lifted.

"Corin?" Edric said, laying aside an iron seal. "Has something broken?"

"No, my lord," Corin said. "The boy has an errand he wants run on Thursday. Down the Ashgrove lane. An empty wain to the Cott place."

Jory frowned. "An empty wain? The timber there is charred charcoal."

"Tell him, Casian," Edric said, his voice flat and weary.

Casian stepped forward. "Mirra and Jon were killed twelve days ago, Father. The men who did it were not common bandits looking for meal-sacks. Bandits do not ride sixteen horses down a marsh lane and leave eight fat steers in the ditch. They came to test how fast Vale answers."

Edric's hand twitched. "Go on."

"If we send three lances galloping down the lane, they pull back into the High Crag and wait until frost. If we do nothing, they burn the lower granary next month. But if we send an empty hay-wain on Thursday—just Corin and two boys, moving like fools who think danger has passed—they will stay on the ridge to watch. They will think we are stupid. And a man who thinks his neighbor is stupid will always show his hand."

The study went quiet. Jory looked from the boy to Edric. "My lord... that's an upland trick. That's how borderers drew cattle-thieves out of the Moss."

Edric looked straight at Corin Ahl, his eyes narrowing. "Corin."

"Aye, my lord."

"He is sixteen years old," said the baron. "He has spent his summer pruning damask roses and hoeing leeks in the walled garden. Corin. Is he right?"

Corin took a breath. "I don't know if he's right about who they are, my lord. But he's right about the ground. If we leave that lane alone, the tenants will say Vale is hiding behind stone. If we ride three horsemen, we catch nothing but wind. An empty wain gives us an eye on the road. And I'd rather hold the reins of a wagon on that lane than sit in the guardroom waiting for another messenger with his hat in his hands."

Edric stared at him for four slow heartbeats. A heavy shadow crossed the baron's face.

"You'll drive the wagon," Edric said at last. "Two lads from the village, not household men. No mail, no lances, nothing but hunting knives and a billhook under the straw. You'll drive to the holding, look at the charred beams for one hour by the glass, and drive straight back. And you report to me. Directly to me. Not to Jory, and not to this boy."

Edric turned his gaze upon Casian. "If I hear you've been giving orders to my armsmen again, Casian, I will set you to hauling gravel for the north ditch from dawn to dusk until Michaelmas. You are a fourth son. Remember that."

"Yes, Father," Casian said, bowing his head with immaculate deference. "Thank you."

---

The first Thursday was dry and dusty.

Corin sat on the wain's ash seat with young Hob whistling beside him. The Ashgrove road was quiet; ditch reeds stood yellow in the heat. At the burned holding, the stench of wet soot hung over the blackened foundations. The roof had fallen inward in a heap of shattered thatch and charred rafters; the stone chimney stood alone like an unwashed tooth.

Corin spent an hour walking the perimeter, poking at cinders. Nothing moved on the ridge. Two ravens circled over the Common, but there was no gleam of steel. He drove home at three, reported an empty road to Baron Edric, and went home to Ivet's pottage.

The second Thursday brought driving rain.

Upland rain turned the road into thick red grease. The farm boys huddled under an oiled blanket, shivering and silent. Corin's cloak soaked through, but he sat upright, watching every willow clump and gully.

Again, nothing. When he brought the dripping team into the Vale yard, Gareth Vale was standing under the archway with an unstrung bow.

"Making a habit of that mud-hole, Corin?" Gareth called out with a grin. "Has my father decided to fold sheep among the ashes?"

"Checking the culvert drainage before winter, Master Gareth," Corin answered politely.

Gareth snorted. "Send tenants to dig ditches. If those border scoundrels show their faces again, tell me. I've three horses ready to run them down."

"Aye, Master Gareth," Corin said. He climbed down from the seat, not looking toward the walled garden where Casian was trimming vines.

---

The third Thursday brought a cold wind out of the northeast, the first breath of autumn biting through the heather.

Corin drove four miles down the lane to the low sheep bridge where the turf drove crossed from the Common. He pulled the horses beneath a stand of alder and turned to Hob and young Wil.

"Stay with the horses," Corin ordered. "Keep your mouths shut. If anyone comes from the south, tell them the axle is hot and I've gone to cut an ash wedge. If anyone comes from the hills, run through the reeds toward High Crag church."

Corin slipped down, hopped the boundary ditch, and climbed up through the gorse.

He did not walk the turf track; he kept ten paces down the lee slope in deep heather where his grey cloak blended into granite outcrops. He walked as he had been taught during the border campaigns nineteen years ago: eyes lifted toward the skyline, feet feeling the ground before weight fell, breathing through his nose.

Half a mile from the crest, where the drove flattened onto a shoulder of peat before plunging east toward the Veyr valley, he found it.

Tucked behind a shelf of exposed limestone that completely hid it from below, sat a cold camp.

Grass in the hollow was beaten flat in a fifteen-pace square. In the center, half-sunk into black peat, sat a hearth ring of five grey fieldstones.

Corin dropped to one knee and drove his fingers into the ash.

It was dead cold—damp ash that had taken two nights of heavy dew and morning mist. A week old, at least.

He stayed on his haunches, reading the ground. Heather was crushed in three neat swathes where horses had been picketed. No dung—they had shoveled droppings into a badger hole and covered it with turf. Sentry scrapes had been dug on the north and west corners: shallow trenches, six inches deep, just enough for a man lying on his belly to rest an elbow and keep his head below the ridge.

Corin stood up slowly, the wind whipping his cloak.

"Nine men," he murmured. "Ten horses. Here for eight or ten nights."

And then the cold reality settled into his gut.

Bandits did not dig sentry scrapes. Border reivers did not shovel horse dung into badger holes to keep crows away. Raiders lit fires in gullies, drank stolen cider, and left broken halter straps behind.

Whoever had laid out this hearth had sited it with an engineer's eye. The limestone ridge masked ember glow from the south; the peat bank swallowed hoof-fall; pickets were placed so every mount could be reached in four strides without crossing the sentry's view.

*That is an armsman's camp,* Corin thought. *A real soldier, trained in a garrison, commanding men who listen to orders in the dark.*

And he was working for a warlord who had never held a commission in his life.

Corin backed out of the hollow, stepping in his own tracks until he reached the gorse, then trotted down toward the wagon.

---

The study was darker that evening. Edric sat alone with a pewter flagon at his elbow. When Corin closed the door, the baron looked up.

"Nothing?" Edric asked.

"Something, my lord," Corin said.

He laid it out plainly: the hearth ring behind the limestone shelf, the concealed dung, the sentry trenches, the disciplined watch kept over the road for ten days before the burn at Ashgrove.

"Whoever is directing them isn't a sheep-stealer, my lord," Corin said quietly. "He's an officer. He's served in a troop, or held a gate for someone with money. He sited that camp so forty horsemen could pass below and never know he was ten feet above their heads."

Edric took a slow breath and pushed a cup of wine across the timber. "Drink that."

Corin drained it. The wine was sour and hot.

Edric rubbed his temples. "A soldier. In the Veyr hills. And Casian told you to look at that drove before you ever set foot on the wain."

"He told me nine men had come down off the Common, my lord. He didn't say how he knew."

Edric looked down at his blotter. The ivy rustled against the leaded pane.

"He was four years old when sickness took his mother's sisters," Edric murmured. "He never cried. He sat on the courtyard bench watching them carry the coffins out, and when I took his hand, he asked if the joiner had enough oak left for winter. Not tears. Arithmetic."

Edric looked up, his grey eyes fixed on his armsman. "Keep the wagon running, Corin. Every second Thursday until frost hardens the lane. Two lads, no steel where it can be seen."

"And the boy, my lord?"

"The boy is sixteen," Edric said, his voice dropping like dry leaves. "God help us if he is right about the rest of it."

---

Corin left the hall by the postern door into black, starless mist.

As he turned the corner of the brew-house, a figure stepped from the shadow of the woodstack—Casian, alone in the dark, his plain wool coat buttoned to the chin.

"Did you find the stones?" Casian asked.

"I found them," Corin said. "Nine men. A cold hearth behind limestone. And whoever put them there knows how to fight a campaign."

Casian nodded once, the nod of a carpenter confirming a mortise fit. "His name is Tolan. He is Hask Veyr's third captain. He is the only one among them who knows how to count."

Corin felt the hair on his neck prickle. "Where did you get that name?"

"Ansel Trask," Casian replied evenly. "In Harrowmere. I bought his debts. He is very old, has many enemies, and prefers a creditor who does not live in Thornwall. Corin. What do you want?"

The sudden shift knocked the breath out of Corin. "What?"

"What do you want?" Casian repeated, stepping closer. "Not now. Not this winter. When you are fifty, and Miri is married, and Lyra has children, and you sit on a bench by the west gate watching lads drop pikes. What do you actually want?"

Corin pressed his shoulders against the brew-house stone. Nobody had ever asked him that.

"You want the truth," Corin said, his voice raspy, "or the polite tale an armsman tells his master's son?"

"The truth," said Casian. "I have no use for polite tales."

Corin swallowed against the damp mist. "I want everything. I want every road in this county. Every bridge, ford, and drove-path from here to the sea. I want a duty that falls to pieces if I am an hour late. I've spent eighteen years being reliable. I've been safe, had good meat and sound boots, and never once had a task that would have gone wrong if I had died of a fever in the night. If I had dropped dead ten years ago, Jory would have hired another carter's son and the gate would still have shut at eight. I want to matter."

Casian listened with profound stillness.

"I cannot give you every road yet," Casian said softly. "I only have one wagon, a miller's ledger, and an old fence who cannot leave his shop. But if this holds—if we build what must be built—there will come a day when you are late by an hour, Corin. And when that happens, someone will pay for it. Not a stranger. Someone whose name you know."

Corin stood rigid. "I know the price of a late horse."

"Then be at the wagon on Thursday," Casian said.

He turned and walked away into the grey fog, his solitary footsteps swallowed instantly by the damp grass.

---

Corin went to the guardroom.

Captain Jory sat at the oak table with an unrolled sheepskin map pinned by four iron candlesticks—an old border survey from thirty years gone.

Jory looked up as Corin barred the door. "The baron told me."

Corin sat opposite him. "Aye."

"A soldier's hearth." Jory leaned back against the plaster wall. "You're sure?"

"Five stones in a reverse slope. Badger hole for the muck. Sentry trenches two inches deep. You couldn't have laid it out cleaner yourself."

Jory whistled softly. "Then the hills aren't raiding for bacon. They're probing."

"They're probing," Corin agreed.

Jory's thumb traced the red line of the Ashgrove road on the sheepskin. "You'll be a fool if you keep running that wagon, Corin. And if it goes right, you'll be the kind of fool that ends up with forty men looking to him in the dark when lances start snapping. I've been captain twenty years. I know twelve things. How to mend a shoe, salt pork, keep six lads from stabbing each other over dice, and shut the gate before the moon comes up. Those twelve things have kept Vale whole while three other houses went up in smoke."

"I know," Corin said.

"And if a boy of sixteen wants to teach this yard a thirteenth thing," Jory said, his voice low, "I'm not going to be the man who breaks his legs to stop him. Because the world outside this valley isn't doing the twelve things any more."

Corin pulled his small leather-bound notebook from his breast pocket. He dipped an iron pen into Jory's inkpot and wrote in his slow, awkward hand:

*Third Thursday. Wagon went and returned. Road clear. Found cold camp on turf drove half-mile from crest. Nine horses, ten nights. Sited behind limestone shelf, reverse slope, sentry scrapes. That is an armsman's hearth, not a reiver's.*

*Tolan of Veyr.*

He paused, the nib hovering over the coarse paper, then pressed hard enough to scratch the oak beneath:

*A boy of sixteen should not be able to see from a garden bench what I cannot see from a wagon. I will ask him about it one day when there is time, and when I am not afraid of what he will say.*

He blew upon the wet ink, closed the book, and tucked it beneath his coat.

"Thursday?" Jory asked quietly.

"Thursday," Corin Ahl said.

He walked home through the mist to his four rooms behind the brew-house, kissed Ivet on the forehead while she slept, checked the blankets over his two girls, and lay down on his straw tick. For the first time in ten years, he slept without waking once before dawn.
