Corin Ahl had been an armsman at Vale for thirty-five years, which was longer than the man who paid him had been alive.

He had come at seventeen, in the year of the bad winter, and had stood at a gate with a spear for eleven months and had not complained about it, and by the time he was twenty-four he was the man the guard captain sent when something needed doing properly and quietly. By thirty he had been offered three better positions in other houses and had turned all of them down. When people asked why, he said he liked the country. This was true and it was not the reason.

The reason was that Corin Ahl was a very good soldier in a very small barony, and being a very good soldier in a very small barony meant that he had spent thirty-five years being given tasks that a competent boy could do.

He did them perfectly. That was the thing about him. He had never once, in thirty-five years, left a gate unlatched or a man unpaid or a road unwatched when he had been told to watch it. He had never once been given a task that used up the whole of him, and he had stopped expecting one some years ago, and had made his peace with it in the way that a big dog makes its peace with a small yard: by walking it very thoroughly.

He was thirty-five and his hair was going and he had a wife and two daughters and a house with a good roof, and none of that is the sort of thing a man complains about.

His wife's name was Ivet and she had been the clever one in the marriage since the day they met, and she had said to him, once, in the second year, something he had never forgotten: "You are not a small man in a small place. You are a big man in a small place, and the place is not going to get bigger, and you must decide what to do about that before you are fifty, because if you don't decide it'll decide for you and you'll be bitter and I will not live with a bitter man."

He had said, "Aye," and had gone out to the gate.

Their daughters were fourteen and eleven, and the fourteen-year-old had the same expression Ivet had, which was unnerving, and the eleven-year-old had decided some years back that her father's life was the most interesting thing in the world and asked him a question about the yard every single evening with her chin on her hands, and he answered every one of them, because he had worked out early that the day a man stopped answering the eleven-year-old's questions was the day he became an old man at a table.

---

The boy came to him on the last day of August, at the yard gate, which was the second time in a month that the boy had come to the yard gate and the first time he had come at night.

"Corin. I want to ask you a thing."

"Ask it, then."

"Not here."

They walked out along the east wall to where it turned north, and the boy stopped at the corner and looked out at the dark the way he had been doing all summer, and Corin thought — not for the first time and not for the first time without discomfort — that a boy of sixteen did not usually look at dark in that particular way. Not fearfully. Appraising it. The way a man looks at a horse he is thinking of buying.

"I want to send a wagon down the Ashgrove road on Thursday," said Casian. "There and back, nothing on it, in daylight, and I want it to stop at the burned holding for an hour and come home."

"That'll be to let the county see Vale on the road."

"Yes."

"Then send one of the young ones and tell Jory."

"I don't want Jory told."

Corin turned his head.

That was the moment, though he did not know it yet, and would look back at it for years.

"Then I'd like to know why," he said, "because I've been in this yard since before you were born, and I've never once taken an order that came to me round the back of the captain."

"Because Jory would tell my father."

"Aye. He would."

"And my father would stop it."

Corin considered this, and the shape of it, and the whole warm reasonable world of the Vale yard where a man did what the baron said and the baron did what the law said and the law was slow and the law was what we have.

"Go on," he said.

"The wagon is bait," said Casian. "There were nine men on the Ashgrove road nine days ago and they came from a drove over the Common that nobody watches. If they're watching the road — and they'll be watching the road — they'll see a wagon go down it with nothing on it and come back the same day and stop at the same place, and they'll decide that Vale has got careless, and then they'll decide something else."

"Such as?"

"Such as that there's a habit in it. A wagon Thursday, a wagon next Thursday. Then one Thursday there's a wagon with grain on it, and if I were them I would take that wagon, and if they take it, I'll know who they are."

Corin said nothing for a while.

"It's a good plan," he said, at last, and was surprised at how much he meant it.

"It's a small plan."

"Aye." Corin looked out at the dark on the hill. "And it's the first plan that's come through that gate in thirty-five years that I couldn't have written in an afternoon with one hand."

---

The eleven-year-old asked him that evening what he was going to do on Thursday and he said he was going to drive a wagon down the Ashgrove road and back.

"Alone?"

"With two of the boys."

"Can I come?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a wagon and you're eleven," said Corin, and she considered this and said, "That's not a reason, that's a description of the two of us," and he laughed, and then did not sleep well.

He took the wagon himself.

That was not the plan and it was not wise and he did it anyway, because he was thirty-five and had been walking a small yard for a long time, and because a man who sends another man into a thing does not learn what is in it.

The wagon went down the Ashgrove road on the Thursday in warm weather. It stopped an hour at the burned holding, which was a black floor and four walls and a great deal of ash and one corner of the byre still standing, and Corin got down and walked it and stood in the yard for a while and thought about a boy of nineteen who had bought a knife and been proud of his horses.

He had known Mirra Cott. Of course he had. Everyone had. She had washed the guardroom shirts for eleven years and had never once in that time been given a decent wringing-out board, which she had mentioned every second month, and which Corin had now decided, standing in the ash of her house, to fix.

He rode back in the evening. Nobody took the wagon. Nobody did anything at all.

He wrote it down that night, in the guardroom, in a hand that had never been good, because a soldier who works alone in a small place learns to leave a record or be called a liar.

*Third Thursday. Wagon went and come back. Nothing. Walked the drove a quarter mile from the top and found a fire square with three stones and the ash cold a week. Nine men ten nights, most like. They sit below the ridge on the east side.*

*Whoever set it knew what he was doing. That is a soldier's fire.*

*The baron is to be told.*

He read it back and then added, at the bottom, because he had been turning it over all afternoon and had nowhere else to put it:

*A boy of sixteen should not be able to see a thing that I cannot see from a wagon on the same road. I will ask him about it one day when there is time and I am not frightened of the answer.*

Then he put the book in the chest with the map, and shut the lid, and sat with his hands on it for a while.

He reported it, in the yard, in front of Jory, because he had decided that whatever happened next he was not going to be a man who did a thing quietly.

"It went to plan," he said. "Nothing to report."

"The boy had you at the burned holding," said Jory, who was not a fool.

"Aye."

"You'd better come to the baron with me."

"I'd rather not," said Corin. "I'd rather you told him and I'd rather I stood there while you did it."

Edric, when he heard, was quiet for a long moment and then said the thing that Corin would remember longer than any of it.

"He is sixteen years old," said the baron. "Corin. Is he right?"

And Corin Ahl, who had been an armsman for thirty-five years and had never once been asked a question like that by that man, said: "I don't know yet. But it's the best plan I've heard in this yard, and the ash on that road is still warm, and I'd rather be doing something than watching a man write letters to Harven."

Edric looked at him and then at his son, who was standing at the back of the room saying nothing with his hands behind his back, and then back at Corin.

"You'll do it," he said. "You'll report to me every time and not to him, and if I hear you've taken an order from a boy of sixteen again without my knowledge you'll be a guard at the gate on the west side by Friday and you'll be there until you're sixty." He picked his pen back up. "That's not a punishment. That's a promise about what happens next time."

---

The second Thursday it rained, and Corin drove eleven miles in it with his hat pulled down and the two boys miserable behind him, and nothing happened, and they came home soaked and laughing at nothing in particular.

The third Thursday he took a boy with him because the first boy had asked to come and had asked in a way that suggested he had been thinking about it for a week.

"You'll not speak," said Corin, on the road. "You'll not look at the trees. You'll look at the road ahead of the horse and you'll be a boy on a wagon and nothing else, and if I say get down you get down and lie flat in the ditch and you don't ask why until I tell you."

"What if something happens and you don't say anything?"

"Then nothing's happening."

"What if something's happening and you're dead?"

Corin thought about that for a mile.

"Then you put your head down and you drive this wagon home at a walk and you don't stop for anybody," he said, "and you go to my house before you go to the baron's, and you tell my wife before you tell anyone."

They went on.

"You've thought about this a lot," said the boy.

"Thirty-five years," said Corin Ahl. "It's the only thing I've thought about."

---

The next Thursday the wagon went again, and the Thursday after that.

On the third Thursday, Corin took a boy with him, and on the fourth he took two, and on the fifth he stopped the wagon himself at the top of the drove road and got down and walked twenty paces off the track and stood there.

He was a good soldier and he had done this before, in the year of the border trouble, when he was twenty-six and there had been a real war for eight months. He walked the edge of the drove in the way you walk a hay field looking for a hare: in a long line, not looking at your feet, letting the ground tell you things rather than asking it.

A quarter of a mile along there was a place where the grass was beaten down in a square and there were three stones set in a ring, and the ash in the ring was cold and had been cold for a week.

"That's a fire," said one of the boys, behind him.

"That's a fire," Corin agreed. "Sit down in it, and I'll tell you what you are."

The boy sat down.

"You're nine men on a hill at night in August, and you can't show a lantern or you'll be seen from Harrowmere, and the nights are cold. So you dig a hole." Corin crouched. "Not here. There. Down the slope where the land falls and the light can't be seen off the top. You come over at dark, you sit till four, you go down, you go over the top before first light, and you sleep wherever it is you came from."

"Eight or nine nights," said the boy.

"Ten, maybe. They'd need to be sure of the day." Corin stood up and looked down the slope at the whole dark bulk of the eastern hills. "And that means there's a man in the Veyr valley who wants a wagon on the Ashgrove road enough to put nine men on a hill for ten nights for it."

He walked the ring once more before he left it, and he was careful, and he was not sure why he was careful until he was already doing it, and then he understood.

He was looking at it the way he would look at a position. Whoever had built that fire had picked it in the dark, on ground they did not own, in a season when nine men on a hill is nine men that nobody is expecting. That took a kind of thinking that Corin recognised, because he did it himself and had done it for thirty-five years in a yard with nothing to think about.

Whoever was up that valley was not a raider. Raiders did not site their fires.

*That's a soldier,* he thought, and the whole shape of the autumn rearranged itself quietly in his head. *That's a soldier working for a man who isn't one.*

He got back on the wagon.

"Anything?" said the boy.

"Nothing," said Corin, and drove on, and did not say what he had worked out, because a boy who has just been told to look at the road ahead of the horse should not be given that to carry home.

---

He reported it in the yard, in front of the baron, because that was the arrangement and he had decided to keep it, and then afterward he stayed behind.

*This is a good day's work,* he thought. *This is the first good day's work I've done since the border.*

---

He reported it in the yard, in front of the baron, because that was the arrangement and he had decided to keep it, and then afterward he stayed behind.

"My lord. A word."

"Go on."

"The boy found the fire," said Corin, "but he didn't need to. He knew it was there before the wagon went out the first time, and he never said it, and I've been chewing on that all afternoon." He turned his hat over. "My lord, I've been in this yard thirty-five years. I know every road in this county to the length of a horse. And I have never once — not once — seen a thing on that road that a boy could see from a garden."

"It's the marches," said Edric. "It's a strange place. His grandmother will tell you."

"Aye," said Corin, and did not believe a word of it, and did not say so another way, because he was a soldier and a soldier knows how much a room will hold.

Later, walking back down to the guardroom with his hat in his hand, he found the boy waiting at the corner of the wall.

"Did he take it badly?"

"No."

"That's not what I asked."

"He took it the way your father takes everything," said Corin, "which is to say he took it in and didn't say the whole of it."

Casian's face did something at that, quickly, and was ordinary again.

"Corin. What do you want?"

"Master?"

"Out of all of this." Casian nodded back at the yard, the house, the wall, the dark. "What do you actually want? Not now. When you're fifty and your daughters are married and you've got nothing better to do than watch a gate on a Tuesday."

Nobody had asked Corin Ahl that in his life.

"You want the honest answer," he said slowly, "or the one a man gives a boy of sixteen?"

"The honest one."

"Everything," said Corin. "Every road in this county and every man on them. I'd like to have a thing that falls apart in my hands if I'm late by an hour, and I've never once had that, and I've had a good life and a good wife and a roof and I'm not complaining, so don't you go thinking that I am." He swallowed. "Three times, in bad years, men have come up to the Vale wall, and each time there was plenty of time and I had eleven men and it ended with a word. That's the whole of my career. Three words."

Casian looked at him a long moment.

"I can't make it fall apart if you're late," he said. "Not for a while. I haven't got anything to fall apart yet."

"No. You've got a wagon."

"I've got a wagon," Casian agreed.

"Then I'll drive the wagon."

"Corin." The boy's voice was quiet. "If it works — if this is the small end of a thing — then one day you will be late by an hour and someone will pay for it."

"I know it."

"Not a stranger. Someone I'll know."

"Then I'll mind it," said Corin Ahl, "and I'll be at the wagon on Thursday."

He went down to the guardroom and did not go home, and Jory found him there an hour later with the lamp lit and a map that had been in the chest since the border and had not been opened since.

"You'll be a fool if this goes wrong," said Jory.

"Aye."

"And you'll be a fool if it goes right, and a different one." Jory sat down on the bench opposite him. "Twenty years I've been captain and I've watched this yard do the same twelve things in the same order every year, and I know the twelve things, and I could do them blind. That's what a captain is, in a place like this. A man who remembers."

"And?"

"And if there's a boy wants to do a thirteenth thing," said Jory, "I'd rather be the man who didn't stop him, because I've seen what happens to a yard where nobody's allowed to do the thirteenth thing. My father was in a yard like that and it was perfectly tidied and it went out like a candle in the year of the bad winter and no one lifted a hand."

Corin looked at the map.

"There's a thing I've never said out loud," he said, "and I'll say it now because tomorrow I'm driving a wagon and I've a notion it'll be a different road after."

"Say it."

"I've been a good soldier in a small place for thirty-five years and I've never once been frightened. Not in the war, not on the border, not at the wall in the bad year." He turned the map round. "I'm frightened now, and the strange thing is it's not about the hills. It's that it's the first time in my life I've had a thing that'll be *worse* if I'm not there. It was never worse before. If I'd been ill in the border year the yard would have got on. If I'm not on that road on Thursday, a boy of sixteen has nothing."

Jory considered this.

"Then be on the road," he said.

"Aye," said Corin. "That's the whole of it, isn't it."

"You'll not tell the baron that."

"God no," said Jory. "The baron's a good man and a good man needs his twelve things. That's what good men are for."

He went to the door.

"Corin. One thing."

"Go on."

"Thursday." Jory's hand was on the latch. "If it's not a wagon that comes up that road, it'll be a thing that's been waiting a long time to happen, and there's eleven men in this yard and I've been trying to work out for six years which of them I'd want beside me in the dark." He opened the door. "I'm glad it's you and I'd rather it weren't."

---

Corin Ahl had the best night's sleep he had had in ten years.
