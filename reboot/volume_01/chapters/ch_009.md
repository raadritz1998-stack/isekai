Osric, Baron of Thornwall, was fifty-five years old and had been careful for thirty of them, and he did not think of himself as a cruel man.

He had been a second son and had never expected the barony. He had spent his twenties as his father's steward, in the yard and the barn and the money-house, learning what a hide of land would carry and what an honest tenant would lie about when the frost came early. His father had been fond of repeating that a baron who relies on his bailiff's memory will wake up owning nothing but his bolster. Osric had taken that lesson to heart. He had spent ten winters with grease on his sleeves, tallying the weight of fleeces and smelling the damp wool in the cellars.

His elder brother had broken his neck in a hunting ditch at twenty-eight, and Osric had inherited the stone hall at twenty-five with eleven years of arithmetic in his head and no idea what to do with a banquet.

He had kept the arithmetic. It had never failed him.

He thought of himself, when he turned the matter over in the evening, as a man who understood what things cost. That was his entire philosophy and he had never found it wanting. Land cost money. Loyalty cost money. A road cost money to keep and yielded money to hold, and the gentlemen who claimed otherwise had never been forced to find eighty marks of good coin in a wet spring. He had a fat, prosperous barony on a paved highway, with four money-changers in a market town that should have supported one, and he had built every stone of it by knowing the weight of every penny.

He was sitting in the small solar with the mullioned casement unlatched, checking the harvest receipts, when his steward entered.

"Rennick is back from Harrowmere," said the steward.

"And?"

"And he lost the mill paper."

Osric set his goose quill into the pewter stand. "Lost it."

"Bought out. Paid in coin before the clerk. Rennick went to collect the overdue interest and found the miller's widow holding a sealed satisfaction." The steward was Weyl, who had served Thornwall for nineteen years and had learned that the quickest way to remain useful was to lay out the plain truth and let his lord chew on it. "The buyer was the fourth Vale boy. Sixteen years old. He counted out four hundred and twelve marks on the counter in front of two witnesses."

Osric looked past the stone frame of the window into the sunlit courtyard below.

"Four hundred and twelve," Osric said.

"Every penny. Including the interest Rennick added for the delay."

"That is not a foolish boy buying a toy," Osric murmured. "That is someone who wanted the parchment destroyed before nightfall. And he paid the false sum as well, without a word of protest. That means he knew it was false. That means he read the ledger."

"A miller's book in a backwater village," said Weyl.

"Mm." Osric touched the tip of his quill with his forefinger. "Send word to Harven again regarding our petition for the eastern strip. And tell Rennick to come up here. Tell him to leave his grin at the foot of the stairs."

---

Rennick came up the stairs with his grin firmly in place.

"There is no harm done, my lord," Rennick said, taking the stool unasked and spreading his knees. "Four hundred and twelve marks of good silver in a leather sack is four hundred and twelve marks. I would have spent two years dragging the widow through the county court to take the wheel, and even then the millrace needs fifty marks of oak piling. Instead I have the silver, I have met the lad, and I would say we came away with the fat of the pig."

"You met him," Osric said, his voice flat.

"At the sheep fair. A civil lad. The quiet one of the four brothers. He was walking the pens with Brennan, looking at fleeces like any squire's son." Rennick tapped his knuckles against the table. "I dropped a polite word in his ear about how careful men remember faces. He took it like a lamb."

"I am sure he did."

"He is only a boy, my lord."

"Rennick." Osric placed both palms flat upon the oak desktop. "Over the past four years I have bought the mortgages on eleven estates in this county. Not one of those debtors has ever paid me back. In August, a boy of sixteen produced four hundred marks from under his sleeve, bought back the paper on a ruined mill, and paid a fraudulent penalty of a hundred and thirty-one marks without blinking. That is the action of someone who has calculated that the cheapest way to end a dispute is to overpay it. And your report is that he is civil and sixteen."

Rennick's smile faded around the edges. "He is also a fourth son with three horses, no inheritance, and his grandmother's purse."

"Which is now completely empty," Osric said.

"Completely empty," Rennick conceded.

"Then he has emptied his pockets to buy a mill that cannot pay a dividend, solely to make an adversary of me." Osric leaned back into his carved high-backed chair. "That is not the conduct of a reckless youth. That is the conduct of someone who has decided I am not dangerous enough to worry about."

Osric spoke without anger. He never spoke with anger. A cold sentence left room for thought; an angry sentence merely gave the listener an excuse to hide.

---

Before noon, Osric performed the duty he attended to every quarter: he visited the money-changers.

There were four stalls huddled beneath the timber arcade near Thornwall's market cross. Three were managed by honest men and one by a rogue, and Osric knew the ledgers of all four better than the bailiffs did. He spent three-quarters of an hour leaning over the second counter, running his thumb down the parchment roll of small loans and wool bonds recorded since midsummer. It was tedious work, but it was the discipline that had warned him the Vale watermill was failing two full years before Baron Edric ever noticed the roof thatch rotting.

"Anything on the border?" Osric asked, closing the ledger.

"Quiet on the paved road, my lord," said the money-changer, dipping his pen. "One curiosity from Fenwick. Baroness Calla took ninety marks in coin against her clip."

"Against her own sheep?"

"Her own sheep, and she has had three excellent seasons, so she is not pressed for bread. She demanded the coin in hand ten days before the fair." The changer turned the leaf. "She expects the price of fleece to shift."

Osric pulled on his riding gloves, smoothing the kidskin over his knuckles. "If she requests another advance, send a runner to the gate."

"At once, my lord."

A border baroness who borrowed before a fair was preparing to buy before a fair. Osric placed that small fact into the corner of his mind where he stored matters that would mature in two or three seasons.

He mounted his gelding and rode out to inspect the eastern parcels.

The eastern strip was a tongue of rough pasture four miles long and a mile wide, squeezed between the Harrow road and the Fenwick ditch. Like many tracts along the marches, its legal title was a maze of dead claims. There were eleven parcels in total. Four were held by tenant families whose male lines had failed during the border troubles. Three were claimed by Baron Edric through an unwritten grant dating back four generations. Two belonged to the Vale mill, flooded half the autumn by backwater. One was scrub oak. 

The last was an ancient, sloping pasture with a moss-encrusted Keeping stone that no tenant would touch with an iron coulter. Osric halted his horse by that stone and nudged it with his boot-toe. To his practical eye, the Keeping was peasant foolishness that wasted four good bushels of rye every planting, but he knew better than to force a plowman past it. Superstitions cost nothing so long as rents arrived on Michaelmas, and a baron who respected his tenants' fears could charge two groats more for his timber.

He rode the rest of the perimeter with Weyl and a groom. He did not merely look at the grass from the saddle. When they reached the lower boundary, he dismounted and walked two hundred paces through the gorse, testing the drainage with the tip of his ash cane.

That was the discipline men never gave Osric credit for. He was not a courtier drawing boundaries on sheepskin in an ivory tower. He had walked every hide of land he had ever purchased, stepping with the heavy, patient gait of a former steward. He knew which hollows held standing water after Michaelmas and which slopes would take barley.

"The culvert in the second parcel has collapsed," Osric remarked, pointing with his cane.

"Caved in two winters ago, my lord," said Weyl.

"Note it, but do not clear it," Osric instructed. "If Thornwall men repair the culvert, we improve ground we do not yet possess. An improvement implies an acknowledgment of title. Leave the ditch choked. It will justify a lower price when the probate comes to court."

"Nobody is selling the second parcel, my lord."

"Nobody is selling today," Osric said calmly, remounting his horse. "The purpose of walking the ditch is that when someone is finally forced to sell, I will be the only man in this valley who knows what lies beneath the sod."

---

In the eyes of the folk who lived beneath his walls, Osric was an equitable lord.

That was another truth his detractors overlooked. Rents in Thornwall had not been raised in nine years. He had sunk the stone well in the lower ward at his own expense and merely grumbled about the mason's bill. He had roofed the market stalls with blue slate brought down from the north crags, replacing rotting thatch that had burned twice in his father's day. He sat in judgment every Tuesday morning, heard complaints without taking bribes, and told petitioners plainly what the law allowed, which ordinary folk valued far more than sentimental generosity.

His tenants did not love him; love was an expensive luxury for a border house. But they trusted him to remain consistent, and a consistent lord was a rare shield against ruin.

As they rode back through the town gate, an elderly weaver's widow curtsied in the lane to dispute an encroaching garden wall. Osric did not brush past her. He swung down from the gelding, listened to her grievance for ten minutes, walked the garden line, and gave her a fair boundary on the spot.

"You have the patience of an abbot, my lord," Weyl observed as they entered the inner courtyard.

"Her father hauled stone for my father for twelve years," Osric said, handing his reins to the groom. "The neighbor's wall was three feet over the survey, and setting it right costs me twenty groats of timber. Fix it."

"Aye, my lord."

"And that, Weyl, is the exact failing of House Vale."

"My lord?"

"I govern the small things with care year after year, and nobody writes a ballad about it. Edric of Vale writes a weeping letter to the Duke about a burned outbuilding, does nothing to secure his lane, and the county praises him for a gentle soul while calling me a cold usurer." Osric climbed the stone steps. "Sentimentality is always popular among folk who have never had to balance a ledger."

---

The fundamental trouble with the eastern marches was that the ground was worth more than it cost to defend, yet nobody had ever possessed the resolve to claim the whole of it.

The Vale barony sat astride the Harrow highway where it came off the moor and bent north toward the coast. Whoever held that strip controlled the wool caravans, the grain wagons, and the royal road tolls. For four generations the Vales had sat upon that artery by default, simply because no neighbor had pushed them aside.

Osric had been pushing them for eleven years. He pushed through quiet paper, through foreclosed debts, and through grain factors. Three years ago he had reached an inescapable conclusion: House Vale could never be unseated through legal parchment alone, because they were too careless to respect paper. They were a sentimental family. They pruned climbing roses. They wrote letters to the Duke in Harven, a hundred miles away, and waited for replies that never arrived.

And then there was the wolf in the hills: Hask Veyr.

Four years back, Osric had ridden into the high Veyr valley in foul weather. He had met Hask in a hall of unmortared slate: a man with forty riders, no ancestors of note, and a quiet, exhausted civility. Hask had asked three pertinent questions about grain transport and had asked for no favors.

Osric had recognized the man immediately. Hask was a warlord with a hundred spears and no patent of nobility. A man in that precarious station had only two choices: he must either conquer a legitimate title or be crushed by the Crown. No court in the realm would grant him a patent. Therefore, Hask would eventually cause immense destruction along the border, or be destroyed trying.

To a calculating baron who held mortgages on eleven estates, a season of controlled destruction in the eastern hills was an asset of extraordinary value.

"Weyl."

"My lord."

"Prepare a dispatch to the valley," Osric directed. "Not to their hall—send it to the factor, the quiet fellow on the grey mare who arranges their salt purchases. Inquire after their winter supplies, and mention that certain disputed parcels along the eastern strip may become available after harvest."

"Yes, my lord."

"And Weyl—when the reply arrives, do not leave it on the table. Bring it directly to me."

---

Two days later, an envoy arrived from the ducal seat at Harven.

Osric received him in the solar, poured a vintage Rhenish, and listened patiently while the courtier discussed the court's anxieties over border security.

"Duke Varyn is uneasy," the envoy said, setting down his silver goblet. "Two petitions have reached Harven from Baron Edric complaining of hill outlaws. The Duke wishes it understood that the Crown's peace must be maintained, but letters are poor substitutes for local vigilance."

"What does the Duke intend?" Osric asked.

"He is sending an inspector."

Osric lifted an eyebrow. "An armsman?"

"His daughter," said the envoy. "Lady Seraine."

Osric paused with his wine halfway to his lips. "A girl?"

"A young woman of nineteen, my lord, who has audited the Duke's revenue rolls since she was fifteen and who publicly corrected the marshal's levy returns last Michaelmas. Duke Varyn spoke of her with great affection—and considerable caution. She travels east next month to examine the border holdings."

---

When the courtier had departed, Osric walked the battlements with Weyl.

"Lady Seraine," Weyl said. "That will complicate matters."

"It simplifies them," Osric replied, gazing out over the town rooftops. "A young noblewoman comes east expecting border chaos. She will find Thornwall prosperous, quiet, and governed with perfect regularity. She will see an incapable neighbour to the east who cannot protect his own tenants, and she will write a report reflecting that truth. She will be a duchess one day, and she will remember that Thornwall was the only reliable anchor on this road."

"And the Vale boy?"

"He is sixteen," Osric said dismissively. "He has spent his hoard on a broken mill. In the autumn he will make some boyish blunder and confirm that the Vales are slipping into ruin. Send the letter to the hills, Weyl. Inform the chieftain that the eastern strip will be settled before spring, and that a sensible man should know his allies."

---

The reply from the high valley arrived in nine days, carried by a carter who left it with the gatekeeper.

It was written on heavy parchment in a firm, precise clerk's hand. It stated four things plainly:

First, that the chieftain noted the Baron of Thornwall's good will.

Second, that the hill folk anticipated a hard winter and would welcome terms from Thornwall's grain factors at the October market.

Third, that certain necessary measures might be undertaken along the border before the first snowfall, concerning which the honoured baron would naturally prefer to receive no prior correspondence.

Fourth, that should a neighbouring estate prove visibly incapable of defending the King's highway before the ducal inspector, the chieftain would find it fitting if the administration of the eastern strip were transferred to a lord of demonstrated competence.

Osric read the parchment three times.

"The fourth paragraph is the core of it," Osric observed.

"My lord?"

"The rest is polite bargaining. The last sentence is Hask Veyr offering to do my dirty work in exchange for my patronage." Osric walked to the stone hearth and held the corner of the letter over the flame. The parchment curled, smoked, and blackened into ash. "He is attempting to make me his debtor."

"Is that not what you intended?"

"It is what I intended, but not how I intended it," Osric said, watching the embers crumble. "When a border reiver describes your own ambitions back to you in writing, you must recognize what he thinks of you. He thinks I will be intimidated by his third point, and pathetically grateful for his fourth."

Osric took the iron poker and crushed the charred cinders into powder.

"I am not intimidated by border skirmishes," Osric continued. "I have managed this frontier for thirty years. If Hask burns what needs burning and makes Edric look like a helpless bystander before Lady Seraine, I will secure the eastern road by Easter. But Hask will discover that a baron who knows arithmetic is far harder to bargain with than a frightened lord."

He turned back to the window, resting his palms upon the cool stone sill.

Eleven miles to the east lay the Vale manor, with its quiet walled garden and a boy of sixteen who had purchased a miller's debt without writing a single letter of explanation to anyone.

*You will be an amusing tale by springtime, Master Casian,* Osric thought, turning back to his ledgers. *And I will be the law on this road.*
