Osric, Baron of Thornwall, was fifty-five years old and had been careful for thirty of them, and he did not think of himself as a cruel man.

He had been a second son and had never expected the barony. He had spent his twenties as his father's steward, in the yard and the barn and the money-house, learning what a hide of land would carry and what a man would lie about. His elder brother had died of a fall from a horse at twenty-eight, and Osric had inherited at twenty-five with eleven years of arithmetic in his head and no idea at all what to do with a hall.

He had kept the arithmetic. It had never once let him down.

He thought of himself, when he thought about it at all, as a man who understood what things cost. That was the whole of his philosophy and he had never found it wanting. Land cost money. Loyalty cost money. A road cost money to keep and made money to hold, and the men who said otherwise had never had to find a hundred marks in a bad spring. He had a fat little barony on a good road with four money-changers in a town that should have had one, and he had built every bit of it by knowing exactly what everything cost.

He was sitting in the small solar with the window open, going through the quarter's accounts, when his steward came in.

"Rennick's back from Harrowmere."

"And?"

"And he's lost the mill paper."

Osric put down his pen.

"Lost it."

"Bought out. In the spring. He didn't know until he went to collect and found the fellow had a sealed satisfaction." The steward's name was Weyl and he had been with Osric nineteen years and had learned that the fastest way to be useful was to say the whole thing plainly and let his master take it at his own speed. "The man who bought it was the fourth Vale boy. Sixteen years old. Paid four hundred and twelve in coin in front of Rennick's clerk."

Osric looked out of the window at the yard.

"Four hundred and twelve," he said.

"All of it. Including the interest that wasn't owed."

"That's not a boy who understands a debt. That's a boy who wanted the paper gone." He turned it over. "And he paid the fraud as well, which means he knew about the fraud, which means he'd read the book."

"A miller's widow in a village."

"Mm." Osric picked his pen back up and did not write anything with it. "Send to Harven again about the eastern strip, would you. And tell Rennick to come and see me, and tell him not to be cheerful at me."

---

Rennick was cheerful at him.

"There's no harm in it, my lord. Four hundred marks is four hundred marks and I'd have had to spend two years in a court to get the mill, and instead I have the coin and I've met the boy, and I'd say we've had the best of it."

"You've met him."

"At the fair. Pleasant lad. The quiet one of the four." Rennick turned his cup. "I said a word to him about careful men not forgetting faces. He took it well."

"I'm sure he did."

"He's a boy, my lord."

"Rennick." Osric set his hands flat on the table. "In the last four years I have bought the paper on eleven houses in this county, and not one of them has ever been bought back from me. In the spring a boy of sixteen found the money to buy one of them from under me and paid the fraudulent part of it as well, which is the act of a man who has decided that the cheapest way to end an argument is to lose it. And your report of him is that he is pleasant and sixteen."

There was a pause.

"He is also," said Rennick, less cheerfully, "a fourth son of a small barony with no land to his name, three horses, and a grandmother's purse."

"Which is now empty."

"Which is now empty," Rennick agreed.

"Then he has spent everything he had to buy a mill that cannot pay him and make an enemy of me." Osric sat back. "That is not the act of a careful man. That is the act of a man who has decided I am not a thing to be careful about."

He said it without heat, because he was not a man who did anything with heat, and because a thing said without heat is a thing a man can think about afterwards.

---

---

Before he went out he did the other thing he did every quarter, which was to sit with the money-changers.

There were four of them in Thornwall and three were honest and Osric knew exactly which three. He spent an hour at the counter of the second one going through what had been lent in the county that quarter, and it was dull and necessary and he had done it forty times, and it was the reason he knew that the mill at Vale had been in trouble two years before the Vales knew it.

"Anything?" he said, at the end.

"Nothing on the road," said the changer. "There's a thing at Fenwick. The Baroness has taken a loan of ninety marks against the wool."

"Her own wool."

"Her own wool and she's had three good years, so she's not doing it because she's short. She's doing it because she wants the money in her hand before the fair." The changer turned a leaf. "That's a woman who thinks the price is going to move."

Osric put his gloves on.

"Write to me if she takes another."

"My lord."

A baroness who borrowed before a fair was a baroness who intended to buy before a fair, and Osric filed it away in the place where he kept things that would matter in three years, and did not think about it again for a month, and would think about it a great deal in the spring.

He rode out that afternoon to look at the parcels, which was a thing he did twice a year and had done for eleven, and which nobody in Thornwall thought strange because a baron who never leaves his wall is a baron who is being cheated.

The eastern strip was four miles long and a mile wide and ran between the Harrow road and the Fenwick line, and it belonged, in the way that these things are always true in the marches, to nobody. There were eleven parcels in it. Four were held by men who had died without heirs and whose holdings had reverted to nobody in particular. Three were held by the Vale barony against a claim that was four generations old and written down nowhere. Two belonged to the mill at Vale and were flooded half the year. One was a wood. One was a piece of ground with a stone on it that nobody would plough.

He walked the whole of it with Weyl and a boy to hold the horses, and it took four hours, and he knew every yard of it.

That was the thing about Osric that men who disliked him never gave him. He was not a man in a chair making marks on a map. He had walked every field he had ever bought, in the boots of a man who had spent his twenties as a steward and had learned it before he inherited it, and he could tell you what a slope would grow and what it would not, and he had never once in thirty years bought a piece of ground he had not stood on.

"There's a ditch gone in the second parcel," he said.

"Two years back."

"Put it in the account and don't put it in the account as mine." He looked at the ground. "If it's on the books as a repair I've done, then I've improved a thing I don't own yet, and a man who improves a thing he doesn't own is a man with a claim. Leave it. It'll be a reason the price is low."

"Nobody's selling the second parcel."

"Nobody's selling any of it. That's not the point of a walk." He turned his horse. "The point of a walk is that in three years, when somebody does sell, I'll be the only man in this county who knows what's under the grass."

---

He was, in the way that mattered to the people who actually lived under him, a good lord.

That was another thing nobody gave him. Thornwall's rents had not gone up in nine years. He had put in the town well at his own cost and had complained about it for a decade. He settled disputes quickly and in public and did not accept gifts in them, and a man could get an hour of his time on a Tuesday if he had a real problem, and Osric would listen to the whole of it and then tell him plainly what he could and could not do, which was rarer in a baron than generosity.

His tenants did not love him. They would have found the idea strange. But they would also have told you, if you asked, that he was fair, and that a fair baron was worth more than a loving one because a fair baron did not change his mind.

Which is why, when he rode through the lower town on the way back and a woman stopped him about a boundary, he got down off the horse and walked the boundary with her, and settled it, and did not make her feel foolish.

"You've the patience of a man twice your worth," Weyl said afterwards, which was as close to fondness as Weyl came.

"Her husband worked eleven years for my father and there's a hedge that's wrong on the east side and she's right about it and it'll cost me a mark to fix." Osric swung up. "Fix it."

"My lord."

"And Weyl. That's precisely the difficulty with the Vales."

"My lord?"

"I do the small things properly and I do them for eleven years and nobody writes it down. The Baron of Vale writes a letter to Harven about a burned holding and does nothing at all about the burned holding, and men in this county say he's a good man and I'm a careful one." He gathered the reins. "The distinction is not in my favour among people who have never had to find a hundred marks in a bad spring."

---

The trouble with the eastern marches had always been that they were worth more than they cost and nobody had ever bothered to take them.

That was the fact and it was not complicated. The Vale barony sat on the Harrow road where it came down off the shoulder and turned north, and a man who held that road held the toll and the grain and the wool, and the Vales had held it for four generations by doing nothing, because being the only house on a road is the same as owning it if nobody contests you.

Osric had been contesting it for eleven years. Slowly, in the way that he did everything, through paper and merchants and the patient buying of debts, and he had come to a simple conclusion three years ago, which was that the Vales would never be taken by paper because they did not understand paper. They were a sentimental house. They kept a walled garden. The baron wrote letters to Harven and to Varyn, both of whom were a hundred miles away and neither of whom had ever answered one.

And then there was the other thing, which was Hask Veyr.

Osric had gone up the valley once, four years ago, in a season of bad weather. He had ridden with eleven men to a hall with a stone floor and been met by a man who was polite and tired and who had, at that time, forty men and no title. They had spoken for two hours. Hask had asked three questions, all of them good ones, and had not once asked for anything.

That was the moment Osric had understood what he was looking at, and it had taken him the ride home to work out how to feel about it.

Hask Veyr was not a baron. He was a man with a hundred men and no title and no letters after his name, and a man in that position must either become a baron or be destroyed, and there was not a court in Varenthos that would make him one. Which meant he would eventually need to be destroyed, or he would do the destroying, and either way there would be a great deal of confusion in the eastern marches.

And Osric, who understood what things cost, understood precisely what a large amount of confusion in the eastern marches was worth to a careful man with paper on eleven houses and a baron's title to spend it from.

"Weyl."

"My lord."

"Write to the valley. Not to the hall — to the man, the quiet one on the grey horse, the one who runs their wagons." Osric pulled a sheet toward him. "I want to know where their grain comes from now, and where it goes, and what they would do about a house on the Harrow road that was suddenly worth burning."

"My lord."

"And Weyl — when he answers, do not write it down anywhere. And do not tell me in a room with a window open."

---

The Count's man came in the last week of August, which was inconvenient, and Osric gave him the small solar and the good wine and let him talk for an hour about the price of wool.

"Harven is troubled," the man said at the end, in the way men say a thing they have been sent to say.

"Harven is always troubled."

"Harven is troubled by the eastern road." The man put down his cup. "The count has had letters. Two of them. One from the Baron of Vale about a border matter, and one from the Baron of Vale about a border matter, and the count would like it understood that the marches are his jurisdiction and that letters are not the same as knowing, and that a man who can send an answer to Harven in the spring is a man who knows something Harven does not."

"And what does Harven propose?"

"Harven proposes to send a woman."

Osric looked up.

"His daughter," said the man. "She is to go east in the autumn to look at the raids and make a report, and the count would be pleased if the barons of the eastern road would give her what she needs and give her no more."

"A woman."

"A woman who is nineteen and who has read every account that comes into the count's hall for four years, my lord, and who has on two occasions told the count that he was wrong in front of his own marshal." The man smiled. "The count's words, not mine. He said it fondly."

---

Afterwards Osric walked the wall with Weyl.

"Seraine Varyn," said the steward. "In the autumn. That's a complication."

"It's an opportunity." Osric watched the road. "A young woman comes east to assess raids she will not be able to assess, because the raids will be over by then and the men who did them will be quiet, and she will write a report. And the question is only what will be in it."

"You could see to it that it's a good report."

"I could see to it that she sees a well-run barony and a road she doesn't understand, and she is nineteen and political, and she will come back in a year and forty years later she'll be a duchess and will remember that Thornwall was the one place in the marches where somebody had the road in hand." He turned. "That's worth more than the mill."

"And the Vale boy?"

"Will be sixteen in October," said Osric, "and will do something in the autumn that a boy of sixteen does, and then this will be a story about how the Vales had a clever son who made one good purchase and got frightened." He put his hand on the wall. "Write to the valley, Weyl. Tell them the parcels of the eastern strip will be settled in the spring and that a man who holds a road should be sure of his neighbours."

"My lord."

"And Weyl. The grey horse."

"Master Rennick says he was at the fair. The quiet one. Buying wool."

"He's buying wool." Osric stood with his hand on the wall for a moment. "There's a house down there with a boy in it who has just sent a man to buy wool in a fair eleven miles from my road, and he paid a hundred and thirty marks he did not owe for it, and I am told to think nothing of it because he is a boy." He took his hand off the wall. "I do not think it was wool."

---

The letter went out on the twelfth and the answer came back on the twenty-first, which was fast, and which Osric noted.

It was one page, in a good hand, and it said four things. That the chieftain was grateful for the honoured lord's regard. That the valley had lost a ford in September and would be grateful for any word of the honoured lord's grain merchants at Harrowmere and their present terms. That a thing might be done in the autumn about which the honoured lord would not wish to be written to. And that the honoured lord might consider whether, in a season in which a baron's estate passed to a woman and a neighbouring barony had shown itself unable to protect its own road, there was an argument to be made to the Count in the spring.

Osric read it three times.

"The fourth part is the one he wants," he said.

"My lord."

"Everything else is a request. The last is a man telling me he knows what I want and offering it." He turned the page over. "He's asking me to let him be useful in a way that makes me his debtor."

"Is that not what you wanted?"

"It is exactly what I wanted." Osric put the letter in the fire and watched it take. "Weyl, when a man tells you the shape of your own ambition in his own hand, you should ask yourself what he has decided about you. The answer is generally that he thinks you will be frightened by the third thing and grateful for the fourth." He picked up the poker. "I am not frightened by the third thing. I've had eleven years of the third thing."

"Then the fourth—"

"The fourth is a letter to Harven in the spring," said Osric, "and it will be a good letter, and it will be written by me and signed by somebody else and it will make a small barony look like a bad neighbour, and it will work, and in nine or ten years there will be a road through it." He put the poker down. "If the fourth thing is all that happens."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'll be a man who wished a thing in the autumn and did not stop it in the winter," said Osric, "which is the whole hazard of my profession and I have never once found a way round it."

---

He went out on the wall afterwards and stood where he always stood, at the corner where a man could see the road for three miles.

Thornwall was a fat little town on a good road run by a man who had been careful for thirty years. It had a well, and four money-changers, and a baron who got down off his horse.

And eleven miles east there was a house with a walled garden in it and a boy of sixteen who had bought a mill and paid the fraud as well, and that boy had not once written a letter to anybody.

*He'll be a story in the spring,* Osric thought, *and I'll be a fact.*

He went in and did not say what else he thought, because a careful man keeps his worries and spends his certainties.
