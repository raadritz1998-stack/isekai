The thing that fed Hask Veyr's valley was not the valley. It was a ford.

Casian had spent three weeks uncovering that fact, and the ease of the discovery was the detail that stayed with him longest. Maeve had the grain purchases tallied in her ledger. Ansel Trask held the notes of two carriers in Harrowmere. Corin, who had driven an empty hay-wain along the Ashgrove lane every Thursday, had returned to the Vale yard with a clear soldier's verdict: "Nothing leaves the hills by the Ashgrove road on iron tires. If a cart had used that track, I would have found its ruts in the clay."

The supply wagons did not use the Ashgrove lane, and they could not use the sheep drove over the Common. A drove-path could carry five hundred wethers, but it would shatter an axle bearing two tons of pickled beef.

That left the river.

The Veyr poured out of the eastern crags, cutting a deep trench through forty miles of alder scrub and limestone before emptying into the Harrowmere basin. In all those forty miles there was only one crossing that could bear a loaded wain: an ancient ford where two hundred years ago an upland baron had laid a submerged causeway of dressed freestone across the gravel shallows.

At the end of August, Casian inspected the tallies at the Ashgrove crossroad.

The crossroad inn was kept by Watt, an old carter whose mortgage Ansel Trask had acquired two seasons back. In exchange for four silver groats and an hour of quiet conversation, Watt produced his tally board. Every wain that had crossed the Veyr since the great frost had stopped at his trough to water the team.

One hundred and forty heavy wagons every autumn, between harvest and the first mountain blizzard.

"Winter stores," Casian murmured, tracing the chalk marks with his thumb.

*Winter stores,* Argius agreed in the silence of his thoughts. *A hundred lances and eighty highland mounts cannot graze on frostbitten heather. They require grain, oats, smoked bacon, and salted beef. That freight must move through the river gap before November, and this causeway is the only stone that can support an axle.*

*And if the causeway were gone?* Casian thought.

A pause followed, the measured interval Casian had known since childhood as Argius weighing a thought.

"If the stones fail," Argius said quietly, "those hundred and forty wains must detour sixty miles south through the Harrowmere turnpike and claw their way over the high notch. That journey adds five days in fair weather, and becomes an impassable morass after the first snow. If the crossing is lost before October, a warlord in the hills faces five months of ice with empty cribs and horses dying in their stalls."

*Can the stones be loosened?*

"They can be removed," said Argius. "They are forty-one blocks of dressed freestone laid side by side in a river trough. They remain in place because they are heavy, and because two centuries of sand and river silt have packed the joints as tight as lime mortar. Anything held together by gravity and compacted gravel can be undone."

*And the effort?*

"No effort at all," Argius murmured. "Which is the true hazard of your craft. When it is finished, a hundred and forty teams will halt. Teamsters will lose their hire, and mountain crofters will face a bitter winter with half their flour. No magistrate will ask you to account for it, because no living man will believe a boy on a garden bench could dissolve a stone road nineteen miles away. The terrible danger for you, Casian, is that it will be entirely effortless."

---

Casian rode out to see the ford with his own eyes. It was a principle he had established when he first walked the boundary ditch with his father: he would never direct his will against ground he had not walked.

He took his bay gelding across thirty miles of dark bridle-paths between dusk and dawn, returning before the hall woke for breakfast.

The ford lay in a wooded ravine below an abandoned timber mill. The causeway stretched thirty yards across the green current, submerged under four inches of clear water. The freestone blocks had been chiseled true and fitted without cement, their edges rounded by two centuries of river gravel. Heavy ruts scored the approaches on either bank.

Casian dismounted, tied the gelding to an alder branch, and rolled his wool trousers above his knees. He waded into the cold, swift stream until he stood in the center of the causeway.

He crouched, plunging his bare hands beneath the surface to feel the seams between the stones.

The blocks were locked solid. Decades of silty sand and river mud had cemented the cracks into an unyielding pavement. The river pressed against the stone face, but the mass of rock held fast, turning the current aside in a long, frothing riffle.

*It was built by men who understood water,* Casian thought.

"A sound piece of masonry," Argius murmured in his mind. "It has anchored the commerce of this valley since the old lords held the ridge. You are about to starve a garrison by breaking a bridge."

*I am stopping a war before it reaches my father's gate,* Casian answered. *Hask burned Mirra Cott's holding to test our strength. Next month he would burn the lower granary. If his horses starve in the hills, his lances stay in their valley.*

"A tidy calculation," Argius said. "And entirely merciless."

*It is bloodless,* Casian replied. *That makes it the cheapest answer I have.*

He waded ashore, dried his legs with a linen rag, and mounted his horse for the long ride home.

---

He broke the crossing on the second evening of September, from the bench in the walled garden.

It was supper hour, and the hall behind him was filled with the warm, familiar clatter of household life. Through the open kitchen shutters drifted the scent of roasted mutton and leeks. Brennan was laughing near the buttery door, and Gareth was arguing with Captain Jory about the balance of a new boar spear.

Casian sat alone beneath the pergola, a pewter cup of small beer resting upon the timber seat. He closed his eyes and breathed the scent of damp loam and crushed mint.

Nineteen miles to the northeast, the river Veyr rushed through its dark ravine.

In his mind, Casian did not see the distance as a barrier. The Current did not travel across miles like an arrow; it was an unbroken fabric connecting the stone bench beneath his thighs to the freestone blocks beneath the water.

He reached through the riverbed.

He did not shatter the stone with brute force. He did not conjure thunder or split the rock. He simply touched the ancient pact between the river and the masonry.

He dissolved the silt.

Deep in the dark water, the compacted sand that filled the hairline cracks between the forty-one blocks turned into loose, fluid slurry. The gravel that wedged the foundation stones into the bedrock gave way, sliding out into the current like loose grain from an unstitched sack. The massive stones remained intact, but they were no longer bound together. They rested on slippery, shifting beds of waterlogged mud.

Then the weather turned, as if the autumn sky had agreed to the deed.

At midnight, a cold upland downpour broke over the hills. The river rose six inches in two hours, the swollen brown current hammering against the unbraced freestone. Water forced its way under the loose footings, lifting the upstream edges. One block tilted, slipped, and rolled into the deep scours below. The gap gave the torrent a purchase. Within forty minutes, the entire thirty-yard line of stone crumbled, tumbling into an eleven-foot channel gouged out by the raging water.

By dawn, the ford was gone.

---

He came down to the hall for breakfast on the third, finding his father already seated at the head of the oak board with a market letter in hand. Maren was setting a platter of crusty bread and a crock of salt butter between them, her sleeves rolled past her elbows.

"The price of grain has jumped," Edric said, without raising his head from the parchment.

Casian pulled out his stool and buttered a piece of bread. "How much, Father?"

"Eleven marks on the quarter in Harrowmere," Edric said, his brow creased with worry. "The bailiff's rider says the lower ford on the Veyr washed out in Tuesday's rain. Three hundred wagons have been turned back, and every factor in the county thinks the eastern towns will be short before frost."

"That seems a heavy stir for an old ford," Casian remarked, chewing slowly.

"It is a heavy stir when winter is six weeks off," his father answered, tapping the table with his knuckles. "You do not remember the winter of '09, Casian. I do. A stone bridge washed out at Harren, and the price of oats doubled in a fortnight. Not a soul starved, but the panic cost this county more than five years of taxes. That is the true function of the King's law: not merely to punish criminals, but to keep four hundred merchants from deciding the world has ended when a single culvert fails."

Maren set down a pewter pitcher of sweet milk with a firm clatter.

"Eat your bread, both of you," she said, her voice brooking no argument. "The ford is nineteen miles away, the Vale granary is full of dry rye, and I will not have my breakfast table turned into a council of despair before the horn blows for terce."

"Yes, Mother," Casian murmured.

Edric sighed, folded his letter, and took up his knife, unaware that his youngest son had just reshaped the trade of the eastern marches while sitting on a garden bench with a cup of beer.

---

The four teamsters reached the Ashgrove crossroad two days later on foot, their leather coats soaked with mud.

They crowded into Watt's low-beamed tavern room, shivering around the peat hearth while Watt ladled hot pea soup into wooden bowls.

"The whole crossing," the eldest teamster repeated for the fourth time, his hands shaking around his mug of cider. "Two wains loaded with salt beef standing on the east bank. We had the second team unhitched and were walking the lead wagon down the slope just at dusk. A heavy river mist rolled out of the willows. Then there came a rumble like an old wall collapsing under a cartwheel."

"A flood?" Watt asked, wiping a trencher with a cloth.

"No flood," the teamster snapped. "The water wasn't three inches over our boots when we stepped in. Then the bottom just gave out. The stones rolled away like dry peas. Young Wat went in up to his shoulders, and we had to drag him out by his collar. When the fog lifted, there wasn't a paving stone left to be seen. Just a black channel deep enough to drown an ox."

"And no one on the bank?" Watt asked, remembering Ansel's strict instructions.

"Not a soul," said the driver. "Nobody but the river and the mist."

---

The news reached the slate hall in the high valley before sundown.

Tolan heard the report from an outrider and rode down to the river within the hour, accompanied by two mounted scouts.

He spent two days at the ravine. He walked every yard of both banks, his sharp eyes searching the gravel for iron crowbars, wedge marks, or the trampled grass of a working party. He found two loaded supply wains resting upright on the eastern verge, their tarpaulins secure, the horses grazing quietly in a nearby clover meadow.

He borrowed a skiff from a downstream charcoal-burner and dragged the riverbed with an iron grapnel. He hooked eleven massive blocks of dressed stone lying scattered across a deep pool three hundred yards below the old crossing, but their mortar was gone and their faces showed no chisel marks.

He rode to the hamlet and questioned all four teamsters separately, listening with the cold, methodical patience of an experienced garrison officer.

They gave the exact same account: the dusk, the mist, the sudden collapse of the bottom, the total absence of any sound except rushing water.

Tolan sat on the edge of a timber bench, turning an iron horseshoe in his calloused fingers.

Honest men recounting an accident always disagreed on minor details. One remembered the rain starting earlier; another insisted the near horse had balked; a third swore he heard a shout. But when four independent carters told an impossible story using the exact same phrasing, it was not an embellishment. It was an account of something that had occurred outside their understanding.

*A river does not scour out forty tons of stone in twenty minutes,* Tolan thought, looking at the swollen green water. *Not without taking the alder trees off the bank. Someone loosened those stones from underneath.*

He mounted his horse and rode back to the high valley to report to Hask Veyr.

---

The slate hall was crowded with forty hill lances drinking sour beer around the hearth when Tolan entered.

Hask sat at the high table beneath an elk skull, gnawing meat from a roasted mutton bone. When Tolan stood before the dais and delivered his report, Hask paused, his greasy knife hovering in midair.

"The crossing is broken?" Hask said, his gravelly voice cutting through the hall chatter.

"The crossing is gone," Tolan said steadily. "A channel twelve feet deep runs where the freestone sat. We cannot move a cart across the Veyr before spring. The winter grain and salt meat must sit on the far bank or detour through Harrowmere."

A murmur of disbelief ran through the armsmen.

"A freshet took it," scoffed Captain Brand, a burly reiver with an unlaced leather vest. "We had four hours of hard rain on Tuesday. The river came up, the old stones cracked, and the mud carried them off. My grandfather remembered the lower weir washing out the same way."

"The weir was timber," Tolan said, his voice flat and hard. "The causeway was dressed freestone, two centuries old, forty-one blocks weighing two tons apiece. A seasonal rise of six inches does not lift forty tons of rock and leave two wagons undisturbed on the approach."

"Then what did?" Brand sneered, leaning across the trestle. "Did Baron Edric swim nineteen miles upstream with a crowbar in his teeth?"

A roar of coarse laughter erupted along the benches.

Tolan did not smile. He looked directly at Hask Veyr, who was watching him with cold, calculating grey eyes.

"I do not know who did it," Tolan said quietly. "I know only that it was not the rain. And if someone can take our supply crossing without showing a single man on the bank, we are not as secure in these hills as you think."

Hask tossed his bone to a hunting hound beneath the bench and wiped his greasy fingers on his sleeve.

"Brand is right," Hask said, dismissing the matter with a wave. "It was an autumn freshet. If the wagons cannot cross the river, send carriers down the high notch to buy flour from Thornwall's merchants. I have a letter from their factor promising terms."

Tolan bowed his head, said nothing more, and stepped down from the dais.

He understood the mood of the room. The men laughed because laughter was cheaper than fear. If an enemy had destroyed their winter lifeline without a sword drawn, the warlord's army was already besieged by a power they could not see. It was far easier to curse the rain.

---

That midnight, in his small chamber behind the armory, Tolan trimmed his tallow candle and opened a narrow, leather-bound notebook.

On the opening page, beneath the heading *Things that should not have been so*, he dipped his quill and wrote in his neat, compact script:

*Third of September. Causeway on the Veyr destroyed. Water rose six inches; no true flood. Approaching wains untouched. Forty-one stones displaced without tool marks.*

*Teamsters agree on every detail: mist, sound of water, collapse in minutes.*

*Hask believes it was the river. Brand laughed.*

*If this was done by human hands, it was done by someone who understood how silt keys stone into a channel, and who was able to loosen the foundation without being seen. There is no mason in this valley capable of such work.*

*Keep a lookout on the Harrow road. The house that profits from an empty grain wain is the house to watch.*

He set the pen aside, closed the clasp, and slid the journal beneath his pallet.

---

A week later, Maeve Corran walked up the path to the Vale walled garden, carrying a folded sheet of mill receipts.

Casian was pruning suckers from the damask roses near the brick wall when she approached, working alone in the quiet autumn sunlight.

"The price of grain has jumped twelve marks on the quarter in Harrowmere," Maeve said, handing him the parchment. "Four factors from the northern hills came through the market yesterday, buying every bushel of rye they could find at whatever price was named."

Casian glanced over the columns of figures. "Hask's buyers."

"Hask's buyers," Maeve confirmed, her sharp gaze studying his face. "The carters say the lower ford on the Veyr gave way in the storm, and no grain can reach the high valley by cart. They are desperate. They are paying Thornwall merchants in silver coin."

Casian handed the parchment back to her. "Let them pay. A man who spends his silver on rye has less to spend on lances."

Maeve folded the receipt carefully. "Ansel Trask sent a message with the flour dray. Captain Tolan spent three days dragging the riverbed and interrogating teamsters. Ansel says the captain has begun keeping a private record of odd events along the road."

Casian paused, his shears hovering over a green shoot. "Tolan is thorough."

"He is an armsman who thinks," Maeve warned. "In this county, that makes him very rare."

She turned and walked back toward the village, her dark wool cloak trailing over the fallen leaves.

Casian set his shears upon the stone bench and looked out across the valley toward the distant blue line of the eastern hills.

"She is right," Argius murmured within his skull. "Tolan is the one adversary in these hills who possesses a soldier's mind. He has begun to write down what he sees. A man who records anomalies will eventually see the pattern."

*He has no army that will listen to him,* Casian thought. *Hask and his captains laughed at him in the hall.*

"They laughed today," Argius said softly. "They will not laugh when winter sets in and their granaries are empty. And when the end comes, Tolan will be the only one who remembers why the road vanished."

Casian looked at the quiet garden, the roses resting in the pale autumn sunlight, and picked up his shears.

*Then let him write,* Casian murmured. *A man with a book is easier to manage than a man with a torch.*
