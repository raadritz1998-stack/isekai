The thing that fed Hask Veyr's valley was not the valley. It was a ford.

Casian had spent three weeks finding that out and it had not been difficult, and that was the part he thought about afterwards. It had not been difficult at all. Maeve had the grain figures in her book. Ansel had the debts of two carriers. Corin, who had been up and down the Ashgrove road eleven times that month in a wagon with nothing on it, had come back and said it in one sentence: "Nothing comes out of the hills by the Ashgrove road with a cart on it. If it did, I'd have met it."

So the carts did not use the Ashgrove road, and they did not use the drove over the Common, because a drove can carry a flock and cannot carry a wagon with a ton of salt beef in it.

Which left the river.

The Veyr ran out of the hills east to west and came down into the low country through a place where the old road forded it, and the ford was shallow because two hundred years ago somebody had put a causeway of dressed stone across the channel and kept it up, and because a causeway is cheaper to keep up than a bridge is to build.

At the end of August, Casian found the bills of it.

They were in a book at the inn at Ashgrove — a real inn, with a bed, a man who looked like an innkeeper, and a dog — which had been arranged for him by Ansel Trask in exchange for eleven marks and a great deal of grumbling. The innkeeper's name was Watt and he had kept a tally of every cart that had crossed that ford in eight years because a man who sells drink to carters keeps a tally of carters.

One hundred and forty carts a year. Most of them between September and November.

"Winter supply," said Casian.

"Winter supply," Argius agreed. "They cannot graze a hundred horses through the frost. That is grain and salt meat and hay, and it must come up the valley before the passes close, and this is the only crossing on the river a wagon can use."

*How long is the river?*

"From the hills to the Harrowmere cut, forty miles, and in those forty miles there is one ford with a causeway and two places where a man can get a horse across and no more."

*And if the causeway were gone?*

There was a pause, which Casian had learned over sixteen years to recognise as Argius being careful.

"Then a hundred and forty carts a year would need to go to Harrowmere and come back up the cut and over the top," said Argius, "which is four days' extra in good weather and impossible after the first snow. If the causeway is gone before the frost, then a man with a hundred horses and a stone floor in his barn has a winter with no grain in it and no way to buy any."

*Can it be gone?*

"It can be gone," said Argius. "It is a line of dressed stone laid in a channel, and it has been there for two hundred years, and it is holding because it is heavy and the water is slow. Anything that is holding because it is heavy can be made to stop holding."

*And it would cost me?*

"Nothing," said Argius. "That is not the question you should be asking."

*What's the question?*

"Whether you have understood that you are about to do a thing that cannot be undone, and that there is no argument about it and no third person to blame, and that when it is done a hundred and forty carts a year will stop, and the men who drove them will have nothing to drive, and their families will have a winter with less in it than they expected." A pause. "You are not going to be asked to justify it. Nobody will ever know it was you. That is the difficulty. It will be very easy for you."

---

He went and looked at it first. That was a thing he had decided at some point in September and had not examined: he would not do a thing to a place he had not seen.

It was thirty miles by road and he did it in a night on horseback, and he was back at breakfast, and nobody asked. The ford was at the bottom of a shallow valley with a mill that was not working and four houses and a great deal of willow. The causeway was a line of dressed stone with the water running over it three inches deep, wide enough for a cart, and a man could see the two hundred years in it — the way the stone had been cut square and laid tight and then the river had spent two centuries trying to get between them and had been losing.

There were cart tracks going both ways. A good deal of cart tracks. Some of them old, some of them from the week before last.

*It's a good road,* he thought, standing on the bank. *It's a better road than the Ashgrove road. That's why they use it.*

"It is also," said Argius, "the only structure in that valley that was built by somebody other than the man who now holds it. Everything else up there is timber and turf and a stone floor in a barn. That is a piece of engineering from before the Veyrs and it holds their economy up."

*Don't use that word.*

"Which word?"

*Engineering.*

"Ah," said Argius. "I forget, sometimes. I have no other vocabulary for it and you have no other word for it either, and so we use yours and yours is worse." A pause. "The stone was laid by men who knew what water does. So does the man who put it there. If I were him I would be sentimental about it."

*He's not sentimental. He's careful.*

"Those are not opposites."

Casian stood on the bank a while longer.

Then he did a thing that he would remember later with more discomfort than anything else in the autumn: he walked the whole length of the causeway, in the daylight, in front of four carters and a man fishing, in a good coat, and nobody stopped him, because a boy of sixteen on a causeway is a boy of sixteen on a causeway.

*If I do this,* he thought, *the man who built this is going to have a very bad year.*

"He has had a very bad year already," said Argius. "He has been having them since he arrived."

*I know.*

"And you are going to have a think about it and then do it anyway, and the thinking will not be for his sake. It will be so that you can tell yourself later that you thought about it."

Casian got back on his horse.

*Yes,* he said.

---

Casian sat on the inn's bench in the last of the day with the tally book open in front of him and did the thing he had been doing all summer, which was looking at a thing properly before he decided.

It took him a while.

*They're Hask's men,* he said at last. *They eat because he holds the valley. If I take the ford, they get thin, and some of them leave, and the ones who stay are weaker and quieter and take fewer villages next spring. That's the whole of it. I'd rather do this than do the other thing.*

"And what is the other thing?"

*What I'll do eventually. You know what it is.*

"Yes," said Argius. "I do."

*Then this is the cheaper way. It's the cheapest way there is. Nobody dies and nothing burns and a hundred and forty carts don't come.*

"And you will enjoy it."

Casian closed the book.

*Yes,* he said. *I'm going to enjoy it very much.*

---

He did it on the second night of September, from the garden, because he had company at supper and had to be at the table, and because he had discovered over the summer that distance made no difference at all and he had begun to be interested in that.

The Veyr ford was nineteen miles away. He was sitting on the bench with a cup in his hand and his mother talking about the harvest and Gareth arguing with Brennan about a dog.

The causeway was a line of dressed stone, forty-one blocks, laid in a channel two hundred years old. It held because the blocks were heavy and the current was slow and the spaces between them had filled with sand and gravel that had set like mortar and had been set for two centuries.

He took out the set of it. Not the stone — the *agreement* the stone had with the water. The sand and the gravel, the way a river and a wall ever agree to leave each other alone. He took that out, all along the line, in a way that would not show until the water had a reason to move.

Then it rained, which was luck and which he did not make and would remember later as the first time the world did him a favour.

That night the river came up six inches and ran hard in the new channels where the gravel had been, and the water worked at the blocks the way it had wanted to work at them for two hundred years and had never been allowed.

Three gave way before dawn. The gaps let the current in underneath and the rest went in an hour, and by morning there was a channel eleven feet deep where the crossing had been, and forty-one blocks of dressed stone lying along the bottom of it in an order nobody would ever be able to explain.

---

---

He came down to breakfast on the third and found his father already at the table with a paper, and Maren putting bread in front of both of them, and the ordinary noise of a house starting.

"Grain's up," said Edric, without looking up.

"Up how much?"

"Eleven marks on the quarter, and Harrowmere says it's the ford." He turned the paper. "There's a ford gone on the Veyr and there's four hundred carts that can't use it and every merchant in the county has decided the whole country's short."

Casian buttered his bread.

"That's a great deal of fuss about a ford," he said.

"It's a great deal of fuss about grain in October," said his father. "You'll not remember '09. I do. There was a bridge went out at Harren and the price of oats doubled in a fortnight and nobody starved, and it was the nearest thing to a bad year this county has had in my lifetime, and it was a bridge." He put the paper down. "That's what the law is for, in the end. Not to punish the men who do a thing. To stop the other four hundred deciding the country's short when it isn't."

Maren set down the pot.

"Eat," she said. "Both of you. It's a ford and it's October and there's a harvest to get in and I'll not have the pair of you sitting at my table being gloomy about the price of oats when the granary is full."

"Yes, mam," said Casian.

"Mm," said his father, and ate, and read, and did not know that he had just described the whole of what his son had done to the eastern marches in one sentence over bread.

---

The four drivers came down the Ashgrove road two days later, on foot.

They had a combined story and no agreement about it. They had been on the causeway at dusk on the third with the second cart half across — that part they all had. Then mist, which was normal on a river in September. Then a noise none of them could describe, which one of them said was like a wall falling and one of them said was like nothing at all, and which two of them could not remember hearing.

And then they were standing in water to the waist on the western bank with the current pulling at them, in the dark, and the crossing was gone.

"All of it?" said Watt, who had heard it three times by then.

"All of it. Every stone. Gone under."

"And the carts?"

"On the far side. Two of them, and the salt beef." The driver drank. "We've walked from the ford since Tuesday."

"And you didn't see anybody."

"There was nobody to see."

"There's always somebody to see," said Watt, who had spent his life around carters and had a low opinion of them and liked them anyway. "On a river. In September."

The driver looked at him for a moment with an expression Watt would think about later, and then said the thing that would be repeated in that valley for four years.

"There was nobody to see," he said. "There was a mist."

---

Tolan heard it in the hall and did not like it.

He did not like it for a reason he could not have defended to the man who told it, which was that a river does not take a causeway out in September. A flood takes one out. A bad winter takes one out. A river in a dry September takes out a causeway when something has been done to it, and the drivers had walked home through four villages telling the story of a mist, and men with a story that good tell it worse and worse, and instead they told it exactly the same every time.

That was wrong. That was the thing that was wrong. A story that does not get better is a story that somebody has been thinking about carefully.

He rode down on the sixth with two men and spent two days on it.

He walked the west bank up and down a mile looking for the place the carts had stood. He found it: two carts, upright, untouched, with the traces still on and the horses gone. He found the horses in a meadow four miles off with their legs sound and their mouths full of grass. He dragged the channel with a grapnel and a rope for a morning and turned up eleven blocks and an old iron ring from a bridle that had been in the water since before he was born.

He interviewed all four drivers separately, which took a day, and asked each of them the same twenty questions in the same order.

They agreed on everything that mattered and disagreed on everything that did not, which is what honest men do — except that all four of them, when asked whether they had heard anything before the water, gave the same answer in the same order of words.

He sat across from the fourth man and did not move for a while.

"You've talked to the others," he said.

"Not since it happened."

"Then why are you saying it the way they say it?"

The man looked at him blankly.

"Because that's how it happened."

---

---

Watt had been the man on the Ashgrove road for six weeks and had not yet had to do anything, and he had begun to think of himself as a man who was being paid for a dog and a bed, and he did not care for how comfortable that had become.

He heard the drivers before he saw them, because men walking forty miles make a particular noise on a road, and he got the door open and the fire up and the pot on and did not ask a question until they had eaten.

Then he asked the questions in the order Ansel had told him to ask them, which was: what did you see, what did you hear, what did you do, who else was there, and what time was it, and he wrote the answers down on the back of a tally sheet in the bad hand of a man who had never had much use for writing.

"Going to be trouble for you?" said the eldest driver. "All that writing."

"It's not for me," said Watt. "It's for a man in Harrowmere who bought some paper off me and asks questions."

"And what's he pay?"

"Nine marks a quarter and a barrel at Michaelmas."

The driver considered this.

"I'd take that," he said.

"Everyone says that until the second quarter," said Watt, and gave him another cup.

---

He went up to the hall and reported it the way a good soldier reports a thing, which is plainly, and in front of the men, because a soldier who reports a thing quietly is storing it up.

"There's no ford," he said. "There won't be a ford before spring and it'll take four hundred men and a dry summer to put one back. The winter grain's not coming up the valley. It's on the far bank in two carts and a wagon that never got loaded, and if we don't have it by the middle of October we'll be buying grain in Harrowmere at Harrowmere prices all winter."

The chieftain — a lean, grey, careful man called Veyr who was not this man's father and was nobody's friend — listened with his hands folded.

"Flood," he said.

"No."

"No?"

"Not a flood." Tolan put both hands flat on the table. "A flood doesn't take a causeway out in September and leave two carts standing upright with the horses in a meadow and every trace gone off the road. It didn't go by weather and it didn't go by accident."

"Then how?"

"I don't know."

There was a silence of the kind that a man can use if he is quick or that will use him if he is not.

"There's forty men in this hall," said one of the other captains, comfortably, "and half of them grew up on that river, and any of them will tell you a causeway goes in a wet autumn. My mother's brother saw the lower ford go in his father's time."

"That was a flood and it took the banks with it."

"You're saying a thing was done."

"I'm saying it wasn't the weather, and if it wasn't the weather then a thing was done, and if a thing was done then a man did it, and if a man did it then we should be looking for the man."

"Looking where?" said the captain, and got the laugh, and the laugh was the point.

Tolan did not laugh. He sat in the hall with forty men and a table and no proof of anything and understood, with a clarity that made him feel old, that he was never going to win this argument in that room. Not because he was wrong. Because the alternative was worse: a thing done to them, from no direction, by nobody, in a valley where nobody had ever been able to do a thing to them.

Men would rather have a bad winter than that.

---

He wrote it down that night, in a book, in a small square hand.

He had started the book after the wagon with nothing on it had gone down the Ashgrove road five times. At the front he had written the date and one line: *Things that should not have been so.*

*Sixth of September. Ford gone. No flood. Two carts upright, horses sound, in the meadow by the mill. Ways gone off the road like they were lifted.*

*Drivers: four. All tell it the same. This is not how men tell a thing.*

*I asked for lookouts on the Veyr and on the top. Nine men on the hills and no man on the crossing. That is my own fault and I will not make it twice.*

He sat back.

Then he wrote the last line of that evening's entry, and it was the line he would come back to in a year and read again, and it said:

*If it was done, it was done from a distance and by someone who knew the stones were held by sand. Nobody in this valley knows that. I have known it since I was nine. So either it was a flood, or it was a man who has stood in that channel with his hands in the gravel, and there is no such man.*

---

Casian read the report four days later — Ansel had a copy of it through a carrier for nine marks, because Ansel bought everything — and he read it twice, and then sat with it for a while.

*He's good,* he thought.

"He is the best man in this volume," said Argius. "You should know that now, because you are going to have to do something about him and you are going to do it badly."

*He's got nothing. He's got a book and a theory and forty men who laughed.*

"He has a book and a theory and forty men who laughed," Argius agreed, "and no proof, and he went and looked at the water anyway, and he put his own failure in writing at the bottom of the page where a man looking for a scapegoat would find it. That is not the behaviour of a man who will stay laughed at."

*He can't learn anything. There's nothing to learn.*

"He learned that the weather did not do it. That is not nothing. In a year or two it will be the only thing anyone remembers."

---

Maeve had the grain figures in the third week of September and came up to the house herself to say them, which she had not done before and would not do again.

"Four hundred and ten bushels left the mill for the Harrowmere cut this quarter," she said. "That's a third more than this time last year and it's not going to Vale and it's not going to Fenwick."

"Where's it going?"

"North, and then east, and then I lose it, because a carrier's paper only tells you so much." She put a folded sheet on the table. "The price has gone up eleven marks on the quarter since August. I've had two men in the mill this week asking what I'd take for the winter store."

"Selling?"

"Nobody sells grain in September unless they're frightened," said Maeve, "and the only thing frightened in this county in September is a man who has been told to buy it."

Casian looked at the sheet for a while.

*He's buying before the frost. He's buying everything.*

"He is buying everything," said Argius. "Which means Osric's letter has already gone, or else a man up a valley with no ford would not have found the money to buy a county's grain in a fortnight."

*So it's already started.*

"It started in August," said Argius. "You are simply now reading it."

---

Casian folded the report and put it behind the stone in the east wall with the rest.

*Then I'd better be finished before the year or two.*

"That," said Argius, "is the first thing you have said all summer that frightens me."
