Vesna Orrow was nineteen years old and had been walking in the dark since she was eleven, and she had never once thought of it as a skill until a man offered her money for it.

The uplands were not the marches. People down in the low country lumped them together because both were windy and green, but they were not the same country at all. The uplands were three hundred square miles of high, rough sheep pasture rising above the Fenwick line where a person reckoned wealth in ewes and distance in hours of marching. The nearest neighbor was a mile away across the peat, the nearest baker four, and everyone learned to walk in the dark because there was nothing else to do after the tallow burned down.

Her father kept two hundred black-faced sheep and had lost forty of them in the bad frost seven years back, a disaster he spoke of every winter as though the frost had been aimed at him personally. Her mother had died of the lung-rot when Vesna was nine. Her two younger brothers were useless in different ways—Bran was clumsy with a crook and Tom was terrified of water—so Vesna had spent eight seasons being the one who went out into the heather when a stray lamb had to be dragged from a gully. Because she was the one who did not get frightened, she had gradually become the one nobody ever thought to ask before sending.

The man who hired her was Corin Ahl. He was an armsman in the service of Baron Edric of Vale, and he had found her in the lower yard at Fenwick, asking whether she could carry a pouch forty miles across the moor and back between dusk and sunrise.

"Yes," she had said.

"Across the peat moor? In whatever weather comes?"

"Yes."

Corin had studied her boots, which were grease-treated cowhide patched twice at the heel, and then met her eyes. "How?"

It was the first time in her life a man with iron on his belt had asked her *how* she could do a task rather than commanding it. She gave him the plain upland truth: "Because a track is a ditch by daylight, but in the dark it's a difference in how the turf gives under your boot. I go by the sound of the becks, the smell of the heather, and the cold air off the hollows. I know where I am on that moor to within fifty yards, and I don't lose my way."

"How do you know you don't?"

"Because I got lost twice when I was twelve," Vesna said, "and both times I had to sleep under a gorse bush until dawn. It hasn't happened since."

---

The difference between the uplands and the low baronies was simple: in the low country folk were tenants, while on the high moor folk were neighbors.

A tenant answered to a bailiff. A neighbor answered to nobody, which meant neighbors had to keep their minds on three realities and nothing else: the weather, the sheep, and the willingness of other poor folk to lend a spade when it cost them little and refuse when it cost them much.

Vesna had grown up inside that hard arithmetic. She could sew a gash in an ewe's flank, reset a dislocated shoulder, and identify which sheep in a flock of two hundred had favored its off-hind leg on Tuesday. She carried a short horn-handled knife, a peeled ash cudgel, and a level, unblinking stare that made drunken shearers think twice about lingering by her path. She could read, slowly and with difficulty, having been taught by an old hedge-priest who had wintered in her father's barn, and she was the only one in her family who could make sense of a surveyor's mark.

She had crossed the boundary into Fenwick thirty times without ever speaking to Baroness Calla. A shepherd's daughter did not stroll up to a manor gate to pass the time of day.

She had seen the Baroness only twice. Once from afar at the Harrowmere wool weigh-in, and once during the great frost, when Calla had ridden up into the hill hamlets with four pack-ponies laden with rye meal. The Baroness had stood in the sleet, wrapped in an otter-trimmed cloak, distributing the sacks with the brisk dispatch of a factor tallying fleeces. She had told the gathered crofters that another consignment would arrive in a fortnight, and warned them not to thank her, because she expected every bushel repaid in wool when the clip came off the hills. She preferred an honest trade to cheap charity.

Vesna had remembered that speech. It was the only time she had ever heard a noble speak without putting on the false voice of a churchwarden.

---

Her first run for House Vale was on the fourth of September.

It was a small, sealed packet wrapped in oiled calfskin. She took her shoulder-pack with two barley bannocks, a small flask of well water, and set out as the red sun sank behind the High Crag. The night was crisp and clear, with three-quarters of a moon lighting the limestone scars. She reached the Ashgrove crossroad before the church bells down in the valley struck midnight.

The tavern keeper, Watt, was waiting in his taproom with a tallow candle burning. He was a broad, quiet man who kept a ferocious brindle mastiff and watched the road with the steady patience of an old trapper. He took the oiled packet, handed her a different sealed letter, and pushed a wooden bowl of hot mutton broth in front of her.

"Going back the same way?" Watt asked, watching her eat.

"The sheep drove over the ridge," she said between spoonfuls.

"Stay clear of the marsh lane."

"I know where the water sits, Master Watt."

"Aye," Watt grunted, leaning against the counter. "You do. Eat your broth."

She was back in the Vale yard an hour before first light. She found Corin sitting on an overturned ale cask outside the brew-house, cleaning an oily rag across his whetstone. When she dropped the packet into his hand, he broke the wax, scanned the single line within, and looked up at her with genuine surprise.

"You covered forty miles in a single night," Corin said.

"You said dark to dark."

"I said dark to dark expecting you to turn up at midday," Corin admitted, tucking the paper inside his leather jerkin. "Sit down by the brazier. Take a loaf from the buttery."

She remained standing, her pack resting against her hip. "I want to ask something."

"Ask."

"You are paying me silver pennies to run sealed dispatches between an armsman and an innkeeper who tallies carts, while a burned holding sits three miles down the road." Vesna adjusted her pack strap. "I want to know what I am carrying."

Corin's eyes were grey and unreadable. "It's sealed."

"I know it's sealed. I don't care what is written on the parchment. I want to know what I am part of."

Corin Ahl, who had been an armsman at Vale for eighteen years, looked at his boots for a long moment before answering.

"I don't know the full shape of it myself," Corin said honestly. "And you have earned that truth. Ask me again when the harvest is in."

---

By the second week of October, Vesna had run that moor road nine times.

She ran it through blinding mist, through freezing autumn drizzle, and once with an upland terrier that followed her for seven miles across the peat before turning back toward its sheepfold. She ran it with a sprained wrist sustained when a peat-bank gave way beneath her boot near Bradeley, binding the joint with a strip torn from her linen shift and telling no one.

She learned that Watt's mastiff answered to Bram and liked dried mutton skin. She learned that Kael, the young armsman Corin sent to meet her when household duties kept him in the yard, was twenty-two, alert, and talked far too much when the moon was high.

And on the seventh run, she found the thing that changed the work.

It happened near midnight, on the high shoulder where the moor road crossed toward the cut. The wind had dropped, leaving the heather completely silent under a frost-white sky.

Vesna had knelt beside a sheepfold wall to tighten a loosened bootlace when she heard the faint, rhythmic strike of an iron shoe against granite.

She dropped flat into the deep heather without a sound, pressing her cheek against the cold moss.

A horse passed along the drove eleven paces from her hiding place. Two men walked beside its bridle, their cloaks wrapped tightly, hoods pulled low against the frost. They were not speaking. They carried no lanterns and wore no jingling mail. They moved north toward the Fenwick border, their pace unhurried and steady.

She lay motionless in the peat, counting two hundred heartbeats after the sound of hooves had faded into the night, then rose and ran.

She reached Watt's crossroad inn forty minutes faster than her usual time.

"Two men and a mount on the high moor," she said, before Watt could take her pack. "Heading north toward the cut. Midnight."

Watt froze with his hand on the cider tap. "Heading north?"

"Toward the high wall above Fenwick."

"Did they see you?"

"Nobody sees me on that moor," Vesna said coldly.

Watt stood motionless for several moments. Then he walked to the heavy oak door, slipped the bolt, and peered out into the empty road before returning to the taproom.

"Right," Watt said, his voice dropped to a murmur. "You tell Corin. Tell him the hour, the place, and their direction. But don't give him your notions of what they were about."

"Why not?"

"Because Corin will ask, and the person Corin reports to will write down your words," Watt warned. "In the marches, a guess written down as a fact gets an innocent man hanged three months later."

---

Two days later, Vesna went back to the high ridge to scout the cut.

The cut was a desolate limestone scar twenty miles long, separating the high moors from the deep ravines of the Veyr valley. No drover brought sheep across it; the rock was too sharp, the gullies too treacherous.

She spent twenty-four hours searching the line of the old boundary wall that crowned the crest. It was a massive, dry-stone barrier, ancient beyond memory, tumbling into ruins in many places, running unbroken along the ridge for nineteen miles.

And there, three miles above where any sensible track would lie, she found the crossings.

There were four of them.

Each was identical: a clean, six-foot gap in the wall framed by two upright monolithic stone posts, without timber hinges or iron pintles. They were empty gateways built into an ancient wall above the clouds, leading from barren rock into barren rock.

She walked the first gap, examining the turf. Then she marched six miles through the gorse to the second, and found the same upright pillars framing empty air.

She sat upon the flat coping of the wall, nursing a split boot-welt, and looked down across the vast basin.

"They aren't field gates," she said aloud to the whistling wind. "They are crossings."

Any ordinary traveler climbing over the wall had to scramble through tumbled stones, leaving scuffed moss and shifted rocks that a tracker could spot from half a mile off. But if four paved gaps existed along nineteen miles of crest, a band of riders who knew their locations could slip across the ridge in the dead of night without disturbing a single pebble.

And the stones were ancient. The granite posts were weathered to the same grey lichen as the wall itself.

*Hask's raiders didn't build these,* Vesna thought, running her fingers over the cold stone. *These were made before anyone in this valley was born. Someone turned this entire ridge into a secret highway centuries ago.*

She stood up, tightened the twine holding her split boot together, and headed south for Vale.

---

She reported the findings to Corin Ahl in the lower harness room.

She laid out the details cleanly: the hour, the location, the two walkers, the single mount, the direction of travel, and the four empty gateways along the high cut. She kept her voice flat and offered no speculation.

Corin listened with his hands propped on his knees. When she finished, he remained quiet for a long moment.

"You're holding back the end of it," Corin said.

"What end?"

"You've had that upland scowl on your face since you walked through the wicket. Say what you think."

"They weren't looking for sheep," Vesna said. "There is nothing to plunder north of Bradeley. Those men were traveling to an appointment."

Corin nodded once. "Come with me to the house. The person who needs to hear this has been waiting for you."

---

The fourth son of Vale was not what Vesna had imagined.

She had expected a pampered young lord in a fur-trimmed doublet. Instead she was led into the stillroom behind the kitchen, where a lad of sixteen with rolled linen sleeves was wiping flour from a timber table. He wore an ordinary wool tunic and had a smear of dried berry juice on his forearm, but when he looked up, his brown eyes were terrifyingly still.

"You have run forty miles a night for me since August, Vesna," Casian said, pulling out a rush-bottomed chair. "And I have not thanked you. Sit down."

"Save the thanks for after," Vesna replied, dropping her pack to the flagstones. "Nobody trusts a thank you from a gentleman who is about to demand something harder."

The room went dead silent. Corin leaned against the door jamb, his arms folded.

Casian looked at her, his lips parting in a brief, genuine smile. "Fair enough. Something harder, then. Two men passed north across the moor toward the high cut at midnight. I want to know where they went, who received them, and what words were exchanged. And I need the answer by Friday."

Vesna looked at him steadily. "Before I agree to that, there is a condition. It is not silver."

"Name it."

"Fenwick," she said. "The lower pasture. If raiders come through the high cut, the first stone house they reach is Fenwick, and the first open pasture is my father's sheep. Baroness Calla has been fair to the uplanders twice in seven years, and she never offered charity without asking for an honest trade. I want her told that there is an upland girl who can cross the cut in a night. And I want it told by a baron's son, not by a shepherd's daughter with a ripped skirt."

Casian studied her for several quiet heartbeats. "You want me to tell a border baroness that I have placed an eye on her moor."

"I want you to tell a neighbor that she has another neighbor watching her wall," Vesna retorted. "You can dress the words in whatever court clothes you prefer."

Casian inclined his head. "Agreed. I will speak to Calla myself."

Vesna nodded, satisfied, and turned her mind back to the hills. "Friday is impossible. The cut is twenty miles of treacherous shale. It will take a day and a half each way, and I must cross the valley in daylight. If there are scouts on the far slope, I need time to wait them out. I can return by Tuesday."

"Tuesday, then," Casian said. "I would prefer you were not taken."

"So would I."

Casian leaned forward, resting his palms upon the timber table. His face became serious, stripped of all pleasant youthfulness. "Vesna. If you cross the cut into the high valley, you are entering country where I have no friends and no riders. If you are caught, I cannot send men to pull you out. You must understand that before you leave this yard."

"I understood that on the first night Corin handed me an oiled packet," she said.

"Then why did you take the work?"

"Because three silver pennies buy sixty pounds of salt meal for my brothers," Vesna said simply. "And because an armsman with an honest face came up to the moor and asked me how I walked in the dark instead of ordering me about."

She stepped closer to the table, her dark eyes searching his face.

"I still don't know what you are, Master Casian," she said quietly. "You are a fourth son who prunes roses, yet you have a miller keeping a ledger, an armsman running an empty wagon as bait, an innkeeper tallying carts, and now me running across the cut. I have carried your dispatches for five weeks, and nobody has told me what I am building."

The room was silent save for the crackle of the wood in the fireplace.

"I am building a warning," Casian said softly. "The next time men ride down the Ashgrove road to burn a cottage, I want them marked three days before they reach the boundary ditch. That is the whole of it."

Vesna lifted her chin, her mouth hardening. "That is a worthy goal. But it is not the whole of it. That is merely what you are doing first."

Corin straightened up from the door. "Tell her the truth, Casian. She is an uplander. She knows the smell of a hidden snare."

Casian looked at the girl with the split boot and the scratch upon her jaw, and made his choice.

"In the eastern hills sits a warlord with a hundred riders who has just lost his winter grain crossing," Casian said evenly. "In Thornwall sits a baron who has bought up mortgages across the county and intends to claim the eastern highway. In Harrowmere sits an old receiver who has given me eleven names of men who take bribes. And along the high cut sit four ancient stone gates that no map in this county records. That is the board. There are fourteen people tied to this work, and none of them hold rank, and there will never be a name or an oath for what we do. But if we hold the roads, no foreign lord will ever burn a holding in this valley again."

Vesna listened, absorbing every word.

"That is the truth," she said at last.

"That is the whole truth."

"Then I will leave the second question for another season," Vesna said, slinging her pack over her shoulder. "Because when a man answers an unasked question so cleanly, it means he has another plan locked in the back room."

She turned toward the door.

"Tuesday, then," Casian said behind her. "Be careful on the cut."

"I am always careful," said Vesna Orrow. "It is the only thing about me that does not rely on luck."
