Vesna Orrow was nineteen and had been walking in the dark since she was eleven, and she had never once thought of it as a skill until a man offered her money for it.

The uplands were not the marches. People in the low country said they were and did not mean it. The uplands were three hundred square miles of rough grazing above the Fenwick line where a man measured his wealth in sheep and his distance in hours, and where the nearest neighbour was a mile and the nearest bread was four, and where everybody could walk in the dark because there was nothing else to do after supper.

Her father kept two hundred sheep and had lost forty of them in the bad winter and had never got over it. Her mother had died when she was nine. She had two brothers who were both younger and both useless in different ways, and she had spent eight years being the one who went out at night when something had to go out at night, because she was the only one who did not get frightened and because being the one who did not get frightened had turned, at some point she could not identify, into being the one who was never asked whether she wanted to.

The man who hired her was named Corin and he worked for the Vales and he had asked her, in the yard at Fenwick, whether she could take a thing forty miles and back between dark and dark.

"Yes."

"Across the moor."

"Yes."

"At night. On your own. In whatever the weather is."

"Yes."

Corin had looked at her for a moment and then said, "How?"

And that was the first time in her life that a man in a good coat had asked her why she could do a thing instead of whether she could, so she told him the truth, which was: "Because a track's a track in the light and a shape in the dark is a shape, and when it's dark I go by the ground and the sound and the water, and I know where I am on the moor to within a hundred paces and I'm not wrong."

"How do you know you're not wrong?"

"Because I've been wrong twice," said Vesna, "and both times I found out in the morning, and it's not happened in six years."

---

---

The difference between the uplands and the marches, which nobody in the low country ever understood, was that in the marches a person was a tenant and in the uplands a person was a neighbour, and those are not the same thing at all.

A tenant has a lord. A neighbour has nobody, and so a neighbour has to be careful about three things and nothing else: the weather, the sheep, and the ordinary willingness of people to help each other when it costs them nothing and not to when it costs them.

Vesna had grown up in that. She could sew a wound and put a sheep's shoulder back in and tell you which of the two hundred had been limping on Tuesday. She had a knife and a stick and a way of standing that made men decide against a thing. She could read, badly, having been taught by a hedge-priest with one eye who had died in the year of the bad winter, and she was the only person in her family who could.

And she had been across the Fenwick line perhaps thirty times in her life and had never once spoken to the Baroness Calla, because Fenwick was a barony and the uplands were grazing and a woman with two hundred sheep does not go up to a wall and ask after the household.

She had seen her twice. Once at a distance at a sheep fair, and once in the year of the bad winter, when Calla had ridden up to the upland with four men and a cart of meal and had stood in the cold and given it out and had said, in the flat voice of a woman doing arithmetic, that there would be another cart in a fortnight and that she would not be thanked, because next year she would want the favour returned and she would rather it were a trade than a charity.

Vesna had thought about that for a long time afterwards. It was the only sentence she had ever heard from a person in authority that had not made her want to be somewhere else.

---

The first run was on the fourth of September and it was nothing.

A sealed thing wrapped in oiled cloth, a pack with bread and a flask, and a hundred and forty miles in her boots since spring, and a night that was clear and cold and had a moon for the last third of it. She left Fenwick at dark and was at the inn at Ashgrove before the middle of the night, and the man there — Watt, who had a bed and a dog and kept a tally — took the thing and gave her a different sealed thing and a bowl of soup she did not want and made her sit and eat it.

"Come back the same way?"

"Same way."

"Don't take the low road."

"I wasn't going to take the low road."

"No," said Watt, "you weren't. Sit and eat."

She was home at Vale before dawn and gave the second thing to Corin, who opened it and read it and then looked at her for a moment.

"You've run it in a night."

"You said dark to dark."

"I said dark to dark and I thought you'd be two." Corin folded the paper. "Sit down. Have something. There's bread in that press and don't tell the household."

She did not sit down.

"There's a thing I want to ask," she said.

"Ask it."

"You're hiring me to carry a thing you don't read out loud, from a man who keeps a tally of carts, on a road where a holding burned in August." Vesna put her pack down. "I'd like to know what I'm carrying."

Corin looked at her for a long moment.

"Sealed," he said.

"I know it's sealed. I'm not asking what's in it. I'm asking what I'm part of."

And Corin, who had been an armsman for thirty-five years and had thought about that exact question for most of them, said: "I don't know yet. That's the honest answer and you're owed it. Ask me again in a month."

She came back in a month.

---

By October she had run that road nine times.

She ran it in rain and in fog and once with the ground frozen and once with a dog she found on the moor that followed her eleven miles and then went home. She ran it with a sprained wrist from a fall at Bradeley, which she bound with a strip of her own shirt and did not mention to anyone until it was healed. She learned Watt's dog's name and Watt's opinions. She learned that the man Corin sent in his place when he could not go himself, Kael, was twenty-two and good at his work and would be better in three years, and that he talked too much on a road.

And she learned the shape of a thing, which was the part nobody had expected.

It came on the seventh run, in the middle of a clear night on the high moor, when she stopped to fix a bootlace and heard a horse.

She went flat behind a wall without thinking about it, and the horse came up the track eleven paces away with two men on the road beside it, and they were not talking and they were not hurrying and they went north toward the Fenwick line, and she lay behind the wall and counted them and then lay there for the count of two hundred before she got up.

*Two men and a horse going north on the moor road at midnight in October.*

She ran the rest of it faster than she had ever run it and got to Ashgrove an hour earlier than she should have.

"Men on the moor road," she said, before Watt could offer her soup. "Two and a horse. North of Bradeley, at the middle of the night."

Watt put the ladle down.

"Going north," he said.

"Toward the Fenwick line and then the cut."

"Did they see you?"

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"I'm sure."

Watt was quiet for a moment. Then he did something she had not seen him do, which was to go to the door and look out at the road for a while before he came back.

"Right," he said. "You'll tell Corin. And you'll tell him the way you told me, which is with the time and the place and which way they were going, and you'll not tell him what you think it means."

"Why not?"

"Because he'll ask," said Watt, "and because the man he works for will want to know what you think, and the way you tell it the first time is the way it'll be written down, and I have been in this country long enough to know that a thing written down wrong gets a man killed three months later."

---

---

The cut was twenty-two miles of nothing.

That was the whole of what she found on the Tuesday, and she took a boot to find it and a day longer than she had said, and she came back to Vale in the dark on the Wednesday with her left boot split along the welt and a graze on her jaw and a thing to say that she had been turning over for two days.

It was not a track. A track would have been easy. A track would have been two ruts and a broken hedge and a man could have found it in an afternoon.

What was there was worse and smaller. Along the top of the cut, above the line where any road would go, above the place where a man would take a cart or a flock, there were gates.

Four of them. All the same. A gap in a wall with two posts and no gate in it, set in a line along the top of the cut in places where there was nothing at all to go through — no track, no field, no fold, no house.

She had walked the first one twice, looking at it from every side, and had found nothing. Then she had walked to the second one, six miles on, and found the same thing. And then she had understood, and had sat down on the wall like a fool and stayed there for a while.

"They're not gates into anything," she said, out loud, on the moor, to nobody. "They're a way of crossing the wall."

The wall ran the whole length of the cut. It was older than anything on the moor and nobody maintained it, and it went over everything, up and down, for nineteen miles. A man crossing it anywhere crossed on a broken place, and broke it more, and the broken places were known.

But if there were four places where the wall had been built with two posts and a gap in it, in a line, on the top of the cut, in places with no reason for anything — then a man who knew where they were could cross the whole of that country in a night without leaving one mark that anybody would notice.

And they had been there a long time. Long enough that the posts had weathered the way the wall had. Long enough that there was grass in the gaps.

*That's not Hask's,* she thought, sitting on the wall. *That's older than a man with a hundred horses. Somebody made that country into a way of going across it and it was a long time ago.*

She got up and looked at the sun and worked out that she had eleven miles to go and a split boot, and went, and did not sleep for another day.

---

She told Corin.

She told him the times and the places and which way they were going and how far she had been from them and how many there were, and she did not tell him what she thought it meant, and Corin listened to the whole of it and then said, "Say the last part."

"What last part?"

"You've been standing there with the last part in your mouth for a minute. Say it."

"They weren't raiding," said Vesna. "There's nothing north of Bradeley to raid. They were going to a place."

Corin nodded slowly.

"Come up to the house," he said. "There's somebody wants to meet you and he'll be annoyed it took this long."

---

The fourth son of the Vale was not what she had expected, and she thought about that a good deal afterwards.

She had expected a boy. What she got was a boy who was extremely good at being a boy — he was in the kitchen with his sleeves rolled and flour on him, and he made her sit, and he asked about the moor road in a way that suggested he had already thought about the moor road and wanted to know if she had, and he was pleasant in a manner so practised that it was almost restful.

Almost.

"You've been carrying things for me since September," he said, "and I've never said thank you, and I'd like to, and then I'd like to ask you for something harder."

"You'll want to do the second thing first," said Vesna. "Nobody believes a thank you from a man who's about to ask for something."

The kitchen went quiet. Corin, by the door, did not move.

Casian Vale looked at her and then, unexpectedly, laughed — a real one, short and surprised.

"All right," he said. "Something harder. There are two men on the moor road going north at midnight and there's a horse and no load, and I'd like to know where they went and who they met and what was said, and I'd like to know it by Friday."

"By Friday."

"By Friday."

Vesna considered it, and considered him, and considered the thing that had been at the back of her mind since the kitchen went quiet.

"Before I say yes to that," she said, "there's a thing I want, and it's not money."

"Say it."

"Fenwick. The lower field. If a man comes out of the hills and comes west, the first walled thing he meets is Fenwick and the first unwalled thing is the sheep." She said it flatly, because it was flat. "The Baroness has been good to the uplands twice in six years and she's never once asked for a thing that wasn't a trade. I'd like her to know there's a girl who can walk the cut in a night, and I'd like it said by you and not by me, because if I go up to that wall myself I'm a shepherd's daughter with a story."

Casian looked at her for a long moment.

"You want me to walk up to a baroness and tell her she's got a spy," he said.

"I want you to walk up to a baroness and tell her she's got a neighbour," said Vesna. "You can call it what you like on the way."

"Friday's four days. If they went over the cut, that's a day and a half each way at my pace and I'd be in daylight on the far side, which I don't know." She turned it over. "I can't do it by Friday."

"No?"

"I can do it by Tuesday," she said, "and I'll lose a boot doing it, and if there's more than two of them on the road I'll be seen."

Casian put the cloth down.

"Tuesday, then," he said. "And I'd rather you weren't seen."

"So would I."

"Vesna." He said her name the way a man says a thing he has decided to say plainly. "I'm going to send you over that cut into a country where I have nobody, and if it goes wrong I can't get to you, and there's no one to send after you, and I want you to understand that before you go rather than after."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"Yes," she said. "I understood that the first night."

"Then why did you take the first one?"

"Because it was a hundred and forty miles in my boots and a night's work and you paid me more than a month of sheep," said Vesna, "and because a man in a good coat came down to Fenwick and asked me how I did it."

There was a pause.

"I don't know what you are," she said. "You're the fourth son and everyone says you're the clever one and I've met clever ones and they talk. You don't talk. Two men on a moor road at midnight is a thing that happened and you'd already thought about it and you've got a man with a tally at an inn and a man with a wagon on the Ashgrove road and a miller who keeps a book, and I've been running your letters for a month and nobody has told me what I'm part of."

"No," said Casian. "Nobody has."

"So tell me."

The kitchen fire ticked.

"I'm going to make it so that the next time nine men come up the Ashgrove road, they're seen before they're within a day of a house that has people in it," he said. "That's all of it. That's the whole of what I'm doing and there's no more to it than that."

Vesna stood up and picked up her pack.

"That's a good thing to do," she said, "and it's not what you're doing. It's what you're doing *first*."

There was a silence in the kitchen of the kind that makes a person aware of the fire.

"You want to be told what you're part of," said Casian.

"I want to be told what I'm part of before I'm part of it," said Vesna, "and I'd like you to notice that I asked, and not to decide afterwards that I was always going to be told when I was useful enough to be told."

Corin moved from the doorway.

"She'll want telling properly," he said. "And she'll not be fobbed off, and I'd be grateful if you didn't try, because I'd have to stand here and watch it."

"I'm not going to try."

"Then I'll be outside," said Corin, and went, and shut the door, which was not something a man did in that house unless he meant it.

Casian looked at the girl with the split boot and the graze on her jaw and made a decision he would think about afterwards.

"There's a man in the eastern hills with a hundred men and no grain. There's a baron in Thornwall who's been buying paper in this county for four years and has just lost the first paper he ever lost. There's a miller who keeps a book, a wagon that drives up and down a road as bait, and an old man in Harrowmere who has eleven names. And there are four gates on the cut with no gates in them, which I've never seen and which you found in three days." He put his hands flat on the table. "What that is, is a thing I've been building since August out of people who mostly don't know about each other. There's fourteen of us and no name for it and there isn't going to be one."

Vesna stood with her pack in her hand.

"That's the truth," she said.

"That's the whole of it."

"Then I'll ask the other question some other time," she said, "because you've answered a thing I didn't ask, and a man who does that is being careful, and careful men are the ones who've got something in the next room."

She was at the door before he answered.

"You're right," he said.

"I know I'm right."

"Tuesday, then. Be careful on the cut."

"I'm always careful," said Vesna Orrow. "It's the only thing about me that isn't lucky."
