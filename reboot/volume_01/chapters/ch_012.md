On the eleventh of October the harvest came in, and for eleven days nobody in the Vale barony thought about anything except the weather.

That was the great corrective, and Casian was grateful for it. For eleven days he pitched barley sheaves onto high wains, drank small beer at midday with the field hands, and slept the dreamless sleep of bone-weary muscle. His father spent every daylight hour in the fields with his boots white with dust; his mother fed forty harvest folk twice a day from cauldrons of mutton stew; Elna had been entrusted with a wooden rake and had lost it in a drainage ditch before noon; and Emric, one year old, sat upon a sack of grain in the stubble, solemnly examining stalks of yellow straw.

On the fourth afternoon of the cutting, Casian stood in the middle of the lower field with his linen shirt unlaced, his forearms scratched by the awns, holding a handful of heavy barley ears. For four uninterrupted hours he touched nothing with the Current, enjoying the rare peace of being merely one of forty harvesters racing the autumn rain.

*This is the good part,* he thought, letting the grain slip between his fingers.

"It is," Argius answered in his mind, his voice like the rustle of dry parchment. "It is also a necessary reminder. The grain is real. The coming frost is real. If this crop failed, forty people would starve before spring. You have spent two months weaving a invisible web that has shed no blood, but a week of hail would make your politics entirely irrelevant."

*I know,* Casian thought.

"Accept the labor, then," said Argius. "You look far less alarming with chaff in your hair."

---

The room they met in was the disused dairy behind the lower brew-house. It had stood empty since Casian was six years old, built of thick limestone with a heavy oak door that barred from within and two narrow arrow-slits set too high for anyone outside to peer through. Casian had spent two quiet mornings in late summer sweeping out cobwebs, scrubbing the stone floor, and carrying in an oak trestle and five mismatched stools.

On the twentieth of October, five people gathered around that table.

Maeve Corran sat at the far end with her account book and an oil lantern, wearing the resolute expression of a woman who had walked out into the night and decided to be practical rather than frightened. Corin Ahl stood with his arms folded across his leather brigandine; he was too much an armsman to sit at a table with an unrolled map. Vesna Orrow perched on the timber corner with her booted feet swinging, her sheepskin jacket still smelling of peat smoke. Ansel Trask had claimed the single high-backed armchair, having brought his own velvet cushion, for which he offered no explanation.

Across the county sat the others who belonged to the work: Watt at the crossroad tavern, with eight years of carters tallied on his slate; young Kael, patrolling the ditch; and eleven names written in Casian's private notebook hidden behind a loose stone in the walled garden.

"Right," Casian said, smoothing the parchment map across the oak planks. "I am going to lay out the whole board once, and then we are going to tear it apart. I would rather be told I am a fool tonight than find out in December."

He weighted the corners with four iron horseshoes.

The survey was in his own hand, rough but accurate. The Vale barony sat in the center, small and quiet, straddling the Harrow road. To the east lay Fenwick and the barren Common. Above them rose the high moor and the jagged limestone ridge of the cut. Taking up the entire eastern quadrant were the rugged Veyr hills, with the river winding out of the gorges and the ancient ford marked with a heavy red cross.

"Four facts," Casian said, his voice calm and level.

"First: Hask Veyr has a hundred lances and eighty highland mounts in his valley, and no grain to feed them through the winter. His supply wagons cannot cross the river because the freestone causeway is eleven feet under water. That leaves him three choices. He can buy flour in Harrowmere at three times the fair price; he can raid neighboring estates to fill his sacks; or he can hire out his lances to someone with deep pockets."

"Second: Baron Osric of Thornwall has been purchasing mortgages across our border for four years, and in August he lost his first claim when we bought out Maeve's mill. Osric wants the Harrow highway. He cannot seize royal highway tolls with legal writs alone. He needs violence along the border to prove Vale cannot protect its roads, but he cannot afford to have that violence traced back to Thornwall."

"Third: Osric and Hask are already in correspondence. A factor on a grey mare carried Osric's terms into the hills twelve days ago."

"Fourth: The Ashgrove lane is watched, the turf drove is watched, the high cut is scouted, and the river crossing is gone. Whatever Hask does next, he will strike where he thinks we are blind. The only question is where the blow falls."

A deep silence fell over the room. The lantern wick sputtered, throwing long shadows against the whitewashed masonry.

"Fenwick," Vesna said bluntly.

Casian looked at her. "Tell us why."

"Because it is the nearest holding, and because a woman rules it alone," Vesna said, her dark eyes sharp in the lamplight. "When a border reiver has hungry men and freezing horses, he does not plan a grand siege. He rides against the easiest gate. Fenwick manor has a stone wall, but its lower fields are open, its flour mill has no ditch, and two thousand highland sheep are grazing in the heather."

"She is right," Corin grunted from the shadows. "Hask's raiders know the Fenwick ditches. They can be over the wall and gone before Calla mounts a single horse."

"She is entirely right," Casian agreed, placing his finger on Fenwick's boundary. "And that is precisely the danger. If Hask strikes Fenwick, Baroness Calla will not write a weeping petition to the local bailiff. She will dispatch a rider straight to Duke Varyn in Harven. Harven will demand an accounting from the Crown, and by spring every baron in the eastern marches will be answering royal commissioners."

"And that ruins our position," Maeve said, tapping her ledger.

"That ruins Thornwall," Ansel Trask interjected, his dry, rasping voice cutting through the discussion.

Every head turned toward the old fence.

"Explain that, Master Trask," Casian said.

Ansel adjusted his velvet cushion with a thin, liver-spotted hand. "Your father writes polite letters to Harven, and the Duke ignores them because Edric is a gentle soul who makes no trouble. But if Baroness Calla—who is kin to the Chancellor and pays her scutage in gold coin—writes to the Duke that border reivers are burning her folds, Varyn will dispatch an armed bailiff within a month. And when that bailiff starts asking who financed the raiders, every merchant who took Osric's silver will start sweating."

Ansel leaned forward, his watery eyes gleaming. "You don't need to defeat Osric in a ditch, lad. You need Osric to be seen. You need his fingers caught in the lock."

Casian stood unmoving for several seconds.

*Argius,* he thought.

"The old receiver is correct," Argius murmured in his mind. "It is a cleaner trap than yours, and it relies on your adversary's own overreach. Take note of it."

"That is sound advice," Casian said aloud. "Now the cost."

Corin stepped up to the table, placing his heavy fists on the wood. "Two mounted outriders. Not one. A single rider on a border road is easy prey; two men with lances make a recognized patrol. They must be quartered near the Ashgrove road, supplied with oats, and paid a wage that keeps their mouths shut."

"The figure?" Casian asked.

"Eight marks a quarter apiece, plus five marks for forage," Corin said. "Watt's inn has a loft and stalls for four mounts. Watt is fifty-one and cannot manage an inn and four horses alone."

Casian calculated swiftly. "Twenty-one marks a quarter. I have fourteen left in my private purse."

The room grew quiet.

"I have nine marks in the mill box," Maeve said, not looking up from her book. "An interest-free loan, repaid in three years. No paperwork, and no thanks spoken before other folk."

"I can provide four marks," Ansel said softly. "The origin of the coin is my own business."

"That makes thirteen," Casian said. "We are seven short."

"Seven marks," Vesna muttered from the table corner, "is what a girl who risks her neck across the cut is worth."

Nobody smiled. The tension in the dairy was as taut as an unstrung bow.

"Seven short," Casian said, his voice dropping into hard authority. "I will find the remaining silver myself. And nobody in this room lends another penny."

Maeve raised her head. "You are turning down coin?"

"I am," said Casian. "This enterprise cannot be funded by dipping into your life savings. The day it costs you more than it costs me is the day you demand a vote in my decisions. And I am not giving votes."

Maeve closed her ledger with a dry slap. "That was spoken like a man who understands power. I will hold you to that."

For another hour they debated the deployment. Corin insisted on choosing the outriders himself, selecting two veteran archers from the lower valley. Vesna demanded clear markers for her scouting runs along the cut, refusing vague instructions.

"Tell me what to watch for," Vesna insisted, leaning toward Casian. "Not shapes in the mist. What does an enemy leave behind?"

"Look for a trail that runs parallel to the crest," Casian instructed. "Every natural sheep path climbs down the gullies toward water. If you discover a trodden way running horizontally along the high cut above the tree line, someone built it to bypass the toll roads."

"Understood," Vesna said. "I can track that."

Ansel Trask waited until the arguments had subsided before delivering his final warning.

"You are all fretting over the riders coming down from the hills," Ansel said, tapping his gnarled finger on the timber. "None of you are looking at the men who already sleep in this valley."

The room grew still once more.

"There are forty men between here and Harrowmere who owe their bread to someone outside this county," Ansel continued. "I gave the lad eleven names whose debts are held by Thornwall factors. But those eleven are clerks and shopkeepers. The men who will strike a spark are the ones whose debts are not recorded in ink: a tenant whose brother was hanged for sheep-stealing; a blacksmith whose holding was turned over to sheep; a carter who hates Baron Edric for a court fine levied ten years ago. When Hask's torches appear on the horizon, those forty men will open your barn doors from the inside."

Casian looked at the old fence, feeling the cold weight of the warning.

*He is entirely right,* Casian thought.

He straightened up and surveyed the four faces before him: the miller's widow, the veteran armsman, the old receiver of stolen goods, and the hill-girl with mud on her skirts.

"I want to say one thing plainly before we leave this room," Casian said, resting both hands on the table. "I did not build this thing," Casian said, resting both hands on the table. "Watt provided eight years of road tallies. Maeve unlocked the grain ledgers. Ansel knows every hidden debt in the county. Corin put his life on an empty wagon. Vesna walked forty miles of midnight heather. I brought an unrolled map, a purse, and a dangerous ambition. Everything else was contributed by you."

Maeve picked up her lantern. "That was worth coming out into the frost to hear. Be certain you remember it when the lances begin to shatter."

One by one, they left the dairy through the postern gate, slipping into the dark at twenty-minute intervals to avoid notice.

---

After the latch clicked behind Ansel Trask, Casian lingered alone in the dairy, the lone tallow candle guttering on the oak board.

*What is the tally?* Casian asked into the cold quiet of his skull.

"Fourteen," Argius replied within his mind. "Five in this chamber, Watt at the crossroad, young Kael, and seven tenants whose holdings now depend upon your purse. It is a modest start. It is also fourteen more than existed in August."

Casian looked down at the map, his gaze fixing upon the high Veyr valley.

*Hask will make his move before the first snowfall,* Casian thought.

"He will," Argius agreed. "Osric will guide his hand toward a strike that looks like common border reiving. How will you respond?"

*I will let him make one move,* Casian answered.

Argius was silent for a long moment. "Explain yourself."

*If I strike Hask now—if I take his hall and scatter his men before November—Osric will merely find another desperate warlord in the spring,* Casian reflected, his thoughts cold and resolved. *I would be fighting shadows for ten years. But if I permit Hask one move, every hidden friend Osric possesses in this county must reveal himself to profit from it. The merchants, the forgers, the forty men with grudges. I will let Hask step into the trap, and when he strikes, we will take the entire hand.*

"You have crossed the threshold between a boy protecting his home and a ruler preparing an execution," Argius observed, the dry impression of his thought lingering in the silence. "I pray you understand the difference."

Casian rolled the sheepskin map, snuffed the candle, and stepped out into the crisp autumn night.

---

The manor house was quiet when he crossed the courtyard. The frost had begun to silver the grass around the well.

Inside the back entryway, sitting on the third step of the stair with a stub of tallow candle, was Elna. Her woolen shawl was pulled tight around her shoulders, her bare toes curled against the cold pine.

"You were in the old dairy," she whispered as he pushed the door closed.

"Brennan should learn to keep his mouth shut," Casian murmured, walking to the foot of the stairs.

"Brennan didn't see you," Elna replied, tilting her head. "I watched from the loft window. I saw the miller's widow, an old gentleman with a cushion, and Corin. Corin never leaves the guardroom after compline."

Casian sat down on the stair beside his twelve-year-old sister.

"Ell," he said gently, "I need to ask you something. It is not a game."

"Good."

"Has any stranger come down the lane this year and asked questions about the house? How many men we keep, or when Father rides to market?"

Elna scrunched her nose, thinking.

"The man in the spring," she said. "The one with the grey mare. He gave me a copper penny for pointing out the Fenwick road."

Casian kept his face entirely still, not letting a muscle twitch. "Tell me about him."

"He was about forty, with grey hair and stubble on his chin," she said softly. "His coat was threadbare, but his boots had silver buckles. He smelled of horse lather and pipe-weed. He asked if this was Vale manor, and I said it was. Then he asked how many mouths sat at the table, and I told him eleven, and that nobody left any roast pork on their plate."

Casian put his arm around her shoulders. *A scout on our threshold in May,* he thought. *They have been counting our spoons since the frost.*

"You did nothing wrong, Ell," Casian said, kissing the top of her head. "If another stranger asks about our gate, tell me first."

"I will," Elna whispered, standing up and shielding her candle with her palm. "And you will owe me another penny, because you have far more silver than you let on."

She slipped up the stairs without another sound.

---

Casian walked into the hall.

The hearth had burned down to a mound of red embers, casting long, crimson shadows across the high timber ceiling. At the head of the oak trestle sat Baron Edric, still fully dressed in his linen shirt and leather waistcoat, a single candle burning beside a column of harvest receipts.

He did not look up when Casian entered.

"There was a light in the disused dairy," Edric said, his voice deep and gravelly in the empty hall.

"I have put it out, Father," Casian answered, stopping three paces from the table.

"Three people left my courtyard twenty minutes ago in the dark," the baron continued, turning a sheet of parchment. "None of them were tenants of House Vale."

Casian stood with his hands behind his back, meeting his father's profile. "Yes, Father."

Edric slowly set down his iron pen. He turned his heavy, lined face toward his youngest son, his grey eyes weary and burdened with thirty years of border governance.

"I will not interrogate you," Edric said, his voice dropping to a low, solemn cadence. "I could say I do not care to know, but that would be a lie, and we do not speak falsehoods in this room. What I will say is this: there will come a night this winter when something occurs along this border that could never have happened by ordinary means. Every lord in the county will have a grand tale to explain it, and I will sit in this chair knowing that my fourth son is the only person who knows the truth."

The old baron turned his parchment face down upon the table.

"Go to your bed, Casian," Edric said softly. "You have work in the garden tomorrow, and I have work in the sheepfold, and I would prefer we remain father and son for as long as the world permits."

Casian bowed his head with deep, unfeigned reverence.

"Good night, Father," Casian murmured, and walked into the dark hallway toward his bed.
