On the eleventh of October the harvest came in, and for eleven days nobody in the Vale barony thought about anything except weather.

That was the great corrective, and Casian was grateful for it and did not say so. For eleven days he carried sheaves and drank small beer at eleven in the morning like a man with no other business, and his father was in the fields from light to dark and his mother fed forty people twice a day, and Elna was given a rake and lost it, and Emric was one year old and sat in the stubble being appalled by straw.

On the fourth day of it he stood in a field with his shirt off and his hands full of barley and did nothing at all with the Current, and had, for the first time in two months, four entire hours in which he was not the only person in the eastern marches who knew anything.

*This is the good part,* he thought.

"It is," said Argius.

*You're not going to tell me it's a distraction?*

"It is also a distinction," said Argius. "The grain is real and the winter is real and if this harvest failed, forty people would be thinner in March. You have spent two months on a thing that has killed nobody and it will kill nobody, and it is also the only thing you have ever done that a bad week of rain could make pointless." A pause. "That is a compliment. It is not a comfortable one."

*I'll take it.*

"Take it in the field, then. You look less strange there."

---

The room they used was the old dairy, which had been empty since Casian was six and had a door that locked and a window too high to look through, and which he had spent two days of the summer clearing out and three more making fit to sit in.

On the twentieth of October there were five people in it.

Maeve Corran sat at the end of the table with her book and a lamp and the expression of a woman who had been asked to come out at night and had decided to be interested rather than annoyed. Corin stood, because he could not sit at a table with a map on it. Vesna sat on the end of the table itself with her boots up, which nobody had told her not to do and nobody was going to. Ansel Trask had been given the one chair with arms and had brought his own cushion, which he had not explained.

And there was Watt, who was not present and who had sent a sheet of paper with eleven years of carters on it, and Kael, who was not present and did not know the room existed, and eleven names in a book behind a stone in the east wall.

"Right," said Casian. "I'm going to say the whole of it once and then we're going to argue about it, and I'd rather be told I'm wrong now than in December."

He put the map down.

He had drawn it himself, badly, and it covered four counties. There was the Vale barony in the middle, small and neat, with the Harrow road going north out of it. There was Fenwick to the east and the Common beyond it. There was the upland, and the cut, and— taking up a third of the sheet and drawn with the most care — the eastern hills, with the Veyr running out of them and the ford crossed out in red.

"Four things," he said.

"One. The hills have a hundred men and no grain, and the grain comes up the Veyr by the ford, and the ford is gone and will not be back before spring. That means a man up that valley has a winter in front of him with a hundred men and no bread, and he has three ways to get through it. He can buy in Harrowmere, which costs three times what he's used to and takes four days more on every cart. He can send men out to take what he needs. Or he can find somebody who wants something done and be paid for doing it."

"Two. There's a man in Thornwall who has been buying paper in this county for four years and has just lost the first one. He has a road he wants and a baron next to it he thinks is sentimental, and he cannot take a road with paper. He needs a thing that frightens people, and he cannot afford for it to be traceable to him."

"Three. Those two men have already found each other. I have the name of the man who carried the letter and I have the date of it, and it's four days after my mill."

"Four. The Ashgrove road is watched, the drove is watched, the cut is watched by a girl who can walk it in the dark, and the ford is under eleven feet of water." He looked up. "So the next thing that comes out of those hills will come out where we are not looking, and the only question is where that is."

There was a silence of the good kind, which is the kind that happens when people are thinking rather than waiting.

"Fenwick," said Vesna.

"Say why."

"Because it's the nearest and because it's a woman on her own," said Vesna, "and because a man who's cold and hungry sends his riders to the nearest thing and not the best thing."

"Fenwick's walled."

"Fenwick's walled and there's forty men within a day of it," said Vesna, "and the lower field isn't walled and the mill isn't walled and the sheep are not walled."

"She's right," said Corin.

"She is." Casian put his finger on Fenwick. "And that's the difficulty, because if he goes to Fenwick, then the woman he's raiding is the Baroness Calla, who has been running that barony for six years and who will write a letter to Harven within the hour, and Harven will send it to Varyn, and by the spring there will be an enquiry into the eastern marches and every man with paper in this county will be answering questions."

"And that's bad," said Maeve.

"That's bad for Thornwall," said Ansel, unexpectedly. "Not for us."

Everyone looked at him.

"Say that again," said Casian.

"Your father writes letters," said Ansel, "and the letters go to Harven and Harven does nothing, and the whole county knows Harven does nothing, and that is the reason a man like Osric can do what he has done for eleven years. But if a baroness with a wall and a county of her own writes to Harven about riders out of the eastern hills, and Harven does nothing about that either, then the next letter goes to Varyn with a name on it and the name is not Thornwall's." He turned his cushion over. "You don't need to do anything about Osric. You need Osric to be *seen*."

Casian stood at the table for a moment.

*Argius.*

"He is right," said Argius. "It is a better plan than yours and it requires you to do less, which is why you did not think of it."

*That's fair.*

"It is not a compliment. It is a warning about the shape of your mind."

---

They argued for two hours, and the first twenty minutes of it were about money, which Casian had not expected and should have.

"Two riders," said Corin. "Not one. A man on a road on his own is a man who can be got at and a man on a road with a boy behind him is a patrol." He stood over the map with his knuckles on the table. "And they'll want paying, and keeping, and a story that holds when a neighbour asks."

"How much?"

"Eight marks a quarter each and a roof."

"Where does the roof come from?"

"Watt's inn has two rooms and a loft and a stable that'll take four horses," said Corin, "and Watt is one man and cannot watch a road and run an inn and keep four horses, and he's fifty-one."

Casian looked at the map.

"That's twenty marks a quarter I haven't got," he said.

There was a silence.

"I've got nine," said Maeve, without looking up. "Not as a gift. As a loan at nothing, which is what you did for me, and I'll have it back in three years and I'll not be thanked for it in front of anyone."

"I've got four," said Ansel, "and I'd rather not explain where from."

"Then that's thirteen and we're seven short," said Casian.

"Seven marks," said Vesna, "is what a girl who walks the cut in the dark is worth, apparently."

Nobody laughed. She had not meant it as a joke.

"Seven short," said Casian, "and I'll find it, and nobody in this room lends me another mark." He put his hand flat on the table. "That's not pride. If this thing is going to hold, it can't be made of everybody's savings, because the day it costs me more than it costs you is the day you're entitled to a vote, and I'm not giving votes."

Maeve Corran looked at him over her book.

"That was well said," she said, "and it was the first time tonight you've talked like a man who's thought about what he'll be in ten years, and I'd like it noted that I noticed."

They argued for two hours, and the first twenty minutes of it were about money

It was, Casian thought afterwards, the best two hours of his life to that point, and it was because for the first time in sixteen years he was in a room with people who did not know what he could do and were not trying to be kind to him.

Maeve wanted to know what the winter would cost the mill if the Harrowmere road got busy, and said, when told, that if there was going to be a war on a trade road she would like three weeks' notice so that she could buy grain before the price went up. Corin wanted to know where the men would actually be, on the night, and said he would not put the wagon on the road again until he had a second man who could ride. Vesna wanted to know what she was looking for on the cut and was not satisfied with "anything" and made him say it properly, which took a quarter of an hour and produced the best line of the evening:

"Then say it like a thing a person could see," she said. "Not a shape. Not a feeling. A thing. What am I looking at?"

"You're looking at a road that goes the wrong way," said Casian. "Every road in the uplands goes down the valleys. If there's a track on that cut that goes *along* it, above the line where a man would put a road, then somebody made it and somebody keeps it."

"Good," said Vesna. "That's a thing I can find."

Ansel said almost nothing for the first hour and then, at the end, said the thing that decided the evening.

"You're all talking about the men who come out of the hills," he said. "Nobody's talking about the men who are already here."

The room went quiet.

"There's forty men in this county who owe somebody outside this county," said Ansel. "I've told you eleven names. Those eleven are the ones whose paper is held by Thornwall. But the eleven aren't the ones who'll do anything. The ones who'll do anything are the men whose paper isn't in a book at all, because they're not behind on money, they're behind on something else." He tapped the table. "A man with a grudge against your father for a fine he paid in '09. A man who was turned off Vale land. A man whose brother is in that valley because he was caught lifting a sheep off the Ashgrove road six years ago and was given a choice."

Casian sat down slowly.

*He's right.*

"He is right," said Argius, "and this is the second time tonight that the least impressive person in the room has said the most useful thing, and you should notice the pattern."

*I notice it.*

"Then say it to them. Aloud. Tonight."

Casian looked round the table.

"I've got a thing here that isn't a thing yet," he said. "It hasn't got a name and I'm not going to give it one, and if anyone asks either of you what you do for me, the answer is that you don't, and you'd like to be left alone about it." He put both hands on the table. "But I want to say the thing out loud in this room, once, because if I don't say it I'll start believing it's mine.

"I didn't build any of it. Watt's done eleven years of carts. Maeve's kept a book that nobody else could read. Ansel's had two hundred names in his head since before I was born. Corin's driven a wagon down a road eleven times with a target painted on it. Vesna's walked a hundred and forty miles a night for a month for a boy she doesn't trust." He looked up. "I've got a map and a purse and a bad idea. That's the whole of what I've brought."

Maeve Corran closed her book with a snap.

"Now that," she said, "is the first thing you've said all evening that I'd have paid to hear, and I'll be taking my three weeks' notice out of it."

---

---

Elna was sitting on the bottom step of the stairs with a candle when he came in, which she had clearly been doing for some time and had clearly planned.

"You've been in the dairy with people."

"Brennan talks too much."

"Brennan wasn't here. I saw a girl with a pack and an old man in a good coat and Corin, and Corin's never here at night." She moved up a step to make room. "And the miller. And the miller never comes."

Casian sat down beside his sister.

"Ell. I'm going to ask you a thing and it's not a game."

"Good."

"Has anyone ever come up the lane and asked you a question about this house? How many people live here. Where your father is on a Thursday. Whether the granary's full."

Elna thought about it.

"The man in the spring with the horse," she said. "He gave me a coin for telling him the way to Fenwick."

Casian went very still, and did not let the stillness touch his face, which was something he had not known he could do until that moment.

"Tell me about him."

"Old. Not old. Forty. A grey coat and he'd not shave in a fortnight and he had a horse that was too good for him, and Ma said afterwards he smelt of money and money goes somewhere." Elna swung her bare feet. "I told him the way to Fenwick and he said did I live here and I said I did, and he said that was a big pile for one family, and I said there were eleven at table and no one had any elbows."

Casian sat on the stair with his sister and listened to her tell the whole of it, which took four minutes, and asked two follow-up questions, and did not ask the question he wanted to ask.

*Eleven at table,* he thought. *In a lane. In the spring.*

"He was doing his job," said Argius. "So was she."

*She's twelve.*

"That does not change the arithmetic. It changes how it feels in your chest and it does not change the arithmetic."

Casian put his arm round his sister and said, "You'll tell me about the next one."

"Are there going to be more?"

"There might be."

"Then I'll tell you," said Elna, "and I'll charge you more than he did, because you're easier to afford."

---

Afterwards, when the others had gone, he sat in the old dairy with the lamp and the map and did not move for a while.

*Argius. What's the number?*

"Five. Four who take your coin and one who takes it and does not pretend that is all she is doing."

*That's nothing.*

"It is nothing," said Argius. "It is also five more than the number of people in the eastern marches who could do a thing about a burning in August, which was nobody. Do not confuse a number with an achievement. Do not let a small number stop you. And do not for a moment imagine it will still be small in a year."

Casian put his finger on the eastern hills. A hundred men. Ten days of riding. A valley, a hall, a stone floor, a man who remembered his stable boys' names.

*He's going to send them out. Not to Fenwick. He'll be cleverer than that.*

"He will be cleverer than that," Argius agreed. "Osric will write to him and Osric will be careful and Osric will suggest something that is not a raid, because Osric does not want a burning that can be traced. He will want it to look like banditry, or a feud, or a bad winter."

*Then I'll give him one move.*

There was a pause.

"Say that again."

*One move. Whatever it is. I'll let it happen.*

"Explain yourself."

Casian sat back in the chair.

*I could finish this in a week. I could take the hall and the chieftain and the stone floor and be done with it and be back in the garden by Michaelmas, and then in the spring Osric would go looking for another man in those hills and he'd find one, and I'd do it again, and the year after that.*

"Whereas if you allow one move—"

*Then every man who wants to profit from the eastern marches has to come out in the open to profit from it. Osric's merchants. Osric's letters. The men at the ford who didn't cross. The forty men in this county who owe somebody outside it.* He turned the map round. *One move. I want to see him use every friend he has.*

"And the people who are standing where the move lands?"

Casian was quiet for a moment.

"I'll be there," he said. "Not on the road with a sword and not in a lane in the dark. Everywhere they go, before they get there. But the move itself — I'll let it start."

"You have just described the difference between a man who is dangerous and a man who is a problem," said Argius. "I would like to tell you which one you are becoming. I genuinely do not know."

He put the map in its case and the lamp out and went out through the stubble to the house, where his mother had left a cloth over something on the table because she always did, and where his father was still up at the end of the room with a lamp and a book of figures, and looked at him over it.

"There's a light in the dairy," said Edric.

"There was. I've put it out."

"Three people walked through my yard an hour ago in the dark and none of them were tenants of mine."

Casian stopped in the doorway.

"Yes," he said.

Edric looked at his son for a long moment, and there was no anger in it, and that was somehow worse.

"I'll not ask," he said at last. "I'd say I don't want to know, but that's a lie and I don't lie in this room. What I'll say is this. There'll come a night when a thing happens in this county and it'll be a thing that couldn't be done by ordinary means, and every man in the eastern marches will have a story for it, and I will be sitting in this chair and I will know that one of the stories is true and that my son is the only man in the room who knows which."

He turned a page.

"Go to bed, lad," he said, without looking up. "You've work tomorrow and so have I, and I'd as soon we did it in the same year."
