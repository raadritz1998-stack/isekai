Hask Veyr had a hundred and six men and a hall with a slate roof and a winter coming with no bread in it.

He had come to the valley eleven years ago with fourteen men and a grudge, and the grudge had been specific and had lasted about two years and had then been replaced by something quieter and more durable, which was arithmetic.

He had been a captain in a household two hundred miles south, and he had held a bridge for a baron who had never come to see the bridge, and at the end of it he had been paid eleven marks less than a man who had spent the same month asleep in a barn. He had asked about it. He had been told, courteously, that the difference was a matter of standing rather than service. He had gone back to his men and thought about it for a week and then taken his pay, and eleven of the fourteen had come with him.

They had taken the upper valley the way he did everything: slowly, with a paper. He had bought the first farm and married into the second and killed only the one man who complained, and he had done that in a way that could not be traced, and he had been careful about it in a manner that had cost him two years of sleep and had been worth it.

And the whole of the plan had been: be worth a title. Be quiet and be worth a title and let somebody notice. Eleven years. One hundred and six men, a hall with a slate roof, a road out over the cut, and a valley that paid him.

Nobody had noticed.

That was the thing he had never been able to explain to Tolan and had stopped trying to. A man who has been careful for eleven years in a valley nobody looks at does not become a baron. He becomes a fact in a valley nobody looks at, and the carefulness, which was supposed to be the means, quietly turns into the whole of it.

He had done the arithmetic four timesHe had done the arithmetic four times, which was three more than he usually needed. A hundred and six men ate a bushel and a half a day between them in bread alone, and there were eighty horses, and the horses ate worse than the men because a horse that is not fed in November is a horse that is dead in February, and a man who loses eighty horses in a winter has a hundred and six men who cannot go anywhere.

The grain that should have come up the ford in September was on the wrong side of a river. He had sent men to buy in Harrowmere and they had come back with eleven wagons and a price that made his jaw ache, and the merchants there had been polite in the particular way that means a man has been told by somebody above him to be polite.

So there were three ways to get through the winter and he had thought about all of them and two of them were bad.

The first was to buy, at three times the price, until the money ran out in January. Then the hundred and six men would be a hundred and six men who had not been paid, in a hall with a slate roof, in a valley with one road out of it.

The second was to wait for the spring and let forty of them leave. That was the sensible thing and he had considered it seriously for two days, and had put it aside for a reason that was not rational and which he was honest enough with himself to name: a man who loses forty men in a winter does not get them back, and a man with sixty men and no title is a farmer with a wall.

The third was to take it.

He had known it would come to the third since the middle of September. What he had not known was how much he would dislike it.

---

"You'll not be at Lowmere," said Tolan.

"I'll not be at Lowmere."

"Because it's a village of forty people and one of them is a child, and if you're not there the men will do what men do when there's nobody to see them."

"I know what men do."

"Then be there."

Hask turned round.

"Tolan. If I am at Lowmere, then the men who go to Lowmere are my men and the thing that happens at Lowmere is my thing, and every man in that village will remember my face and the shape of my horse. And in the spring a Warden of the Ash will ride through the marches asking what happened at Lowmere in October, and there will be forty people in the eastern marches who can answer him."

"And if you're not there?"

"Then the thing that happens at Lowmere happened, and I was at home, and it was men who had not been paid in three months doing a thing that men who have not been paid do." Hask put his hands behind his back. "I'll not be at Lowmere because I want to be able to say, truthfully, in front of a Warden, that I was not at Lowmere."

Tolan looked at him for a long moment.

"That's a fine distinction," he said.

"It's the only kind I've got."

"It's not, though." Tolan's voice was flat. "You've got a hundred and six men and a hall and you could take them south next month and hire out to a man who wants a wall held, and in three years you'd be a captain of somebody's guard with a stone floor and a wage and no valley to feed. I've watched you not do that for two years."

Hask did not answer, and the not answering was the whole of the answer, and both of them knew it.

---

The other thing that happened that week was that a captain named Berrin asked to march on Vale.

"The garrison's a hundred men and four of them are in the yard," he said. "There's a wall and there's forty men within a day of it and I've walked that ground four times. I could be through the wall in a morning and I could have their granary and their horses and be home by the second night."

"You could not," said Hask.

"I've walked it."

"You have walked it in a dry autumn with nobody watching you walk it, which tells me you have not walked it." Hask put his cup down. "The Vale wall is eight feet and has a gatehouse, and there are eleven men in that yard who sleep in a guardroom with their boots on, and the second son has a name in this county that men say without laughing. You would not have the granary. You would have a seige, and every man in the eastern marches would come and look at it, and the Count would be told in the spring by eleven separate people, and I would be a raider with a hundred men in a valley with one road out of it."

"Then we sit here and starve quietly."

"We sit here," said Hask, "and we are *quiet*, and quiet is worth a great deal in a county where nobody has looked east in eleven years." He looked at Berrin. "Do you know what I have spent eleven years buying? Not land. Not men. Silence. I have bought silence from eleven men in Thornwall and a Count who has never ridden further east than his own mill and forty farmers who have not once been asked what happens up the Veyr. And a man who has bought that much silence does not then set fire to a walled yard because it is nearly winter."

Berrin went out unconvinced, and Hask sat with the map and felt the whole of it move under his hands a fraction, the way a table moves when a leg is short.

"He's not wrong," said Tolan, from the doorway.

"He's wrong about everything. He's wrong about the wall and he's wrong about the spring." Hask did not look up. "What he's not wrong about is that we are running."

---

Lowmere was eleven miles east---

Lowmere was eleven miles east and slightly north of the Veyr and it had forty-one people in it and a mill that did not work and two barns.

They came at dusk with twenty-two men. The headman was a man called Ferrin who was fifty and had been headman for nine years and had never in that time had to do anything harder than decide who got the first use of the mill.

He came out into the yard with his hands visible and did the sensible thing, which was to say that the barns were full and the granary was full and they could take all of it.

"All of it," said the man leading them, who was not Tolan and was not the chieftain, and whose name was Berrin and who had been unpaid since August.

"All of it," Ferrin agreed. "And the horses. There's four and two are good."

"And the people."

Ferrin did not understand it at first.

"There's a thing we need," said Berrin. "There's a man in Thornwall paying for labour and he wants it quiet and he wants it moved. So there's eleven of you coming with us and you'll be back by spring and it's better than a burning."

"My wife is sixty-one."

"Then she'll be warm," said Berrin, "since the alternative is that she's not."

They took eleven. They took Ferrin because a headman who is taken is a headman who cannot organise a thing after; they took four men of working age, three women, a boy of fifteen, and a girl of nine who was Ferrin's granddaughter and who had been asleep.

Ferrin's wife did not go. She was sixty-one, and when they came for her she said she would stay in her own house, and the man Berrin stood in front of her for a moment and then said, "Suit yourself, mother," and took the granddaughter instead. She stood in her own yard and watched them take a nine-year-old in her shift in the dark and did not make a sound, because a woman of sixty-one in a yard with twenty-two men in it has already worked out what making a sound is worth.

She was still standing there at dawn.

They took the granary and the horses and the four good carts, and then they burned the two barns, because a village with grain in the barn on Thursday and no grain and no barn on Friday is a village that has been dealt with, and a village that has been dealt with does not send to Harven.

Ferrin's wife did not go. She was sixty-one and when they came for her she said she would stay in her own house, and the man Berrin stood in front of her for a moment and then said, "Suit yourself, mother," and took the granddaughter instead, and she stood in her own yard and watched them take a nine-year-old in her shift in the dark and did not make a sound, because a woman of sixty-one in a yard with twenty-two men in it has already worked out what making a sound is worth.

She was still standing there at dawn.

It took forty minutes.

---

The men were paid on the last day of the month and it took two hours because Hask paid in coin and counted it in front of them, which was one of the eleven things he did that had kept a hundred men with him for eleven years.

That month he paid from a chest that was a good deal lighter than the month before, and every man in the room could see it, and none of them said anything, and the silence was worse than a complaint.

Tolan watched it from the side of the hall.

"You'll have to say something," he said afterwards.

"I'll say something when I have something to say."

"They can count, Hask. They've been counting since September."

"Then they know exactly what I know and there is nothing to tell them."

"There's a difference between knowing a thing and being told it by the man who's paying you." Tolan came round in front of him. "I've been a soldier since I was fifteen and I'll tell you the one thing that breaks a company faster than hunger. It's not hunger. It's men deciding the man at the top is keeping something from them, and being right."

Hask put the chest lid down.

"Then tell them," he said. "You're the one they like."

"I'm not the one they like. I'm the one they're frightened of, and that's a different thing and it's the only reason I'm still alive in this hall." Tolan's voice was flat. "I'll not tell them, and you'll not make me, and when this is over you'll remember that I would have told them the truth in October and you'd rather have had the quiet."

---

He rode up to the barn on the Sunday, alone, which he told nobody about.

It was two hours from the hall and there were four men on it and they were surprised to see him and got up too quickly. He waved them down and went and stood at the door and looked in through the gap where it did not quite meet.

Eleven people. He counted them because he counted everything. An old woman with a girl asleep against her. Four men of working age, one of them asleep and snoring. A boy with a foot wrapped in something. Three women sitting together with their knees up. A man of about fifty sitting against the far wall with his eyes open, looking straight at the gap in the door.

Hask stepped back.

The man had not said anything and had not moved and his face had not changed, and he had been looking at the gap in the door for four days, which is what a headman does.

*He knows exactly what I am,* Hask thought. *He worked it out on the first night and he has been sitting in there for four days knowing it, and he has not told the others, because telling them would only make it worse.*

He rode back without saying a word to the four men, and by the time he got to the hall it was dark, and he did not eat, and Tolan found him in the passage and looked at his face and did not ask.

Hask was not there. He was in the hall with a fire and a map and he heard about it on the Saturday, when Berrin came back and made his report standing up, the way a man does when he knows what he has done and has decided to be brisk about it.

"Eleven, and the grain, and the horses. Two barns. No dead."

"No dead," Hask repeated.

"One man was struck. He'll live."

Hask looked at the map for a while.

"Where are they?"

"In the old sheep barn above the ford. It's got a dry floor and a lock and it holds them and it's two hours from the road, and I've put four men on it."

"Four men on eleven people."

"Four men on a locked barn in a country where nobody comes," said Berrin. "It's enough."

Hask nodded slowly, and Berrin went out, and Tolan came in, because Tolan had been waiting in the passage for the whole of it, which was not the sort of thing he usually did.

"Say it," said Hask.

"There's nothing to say that you don't know."

"Say it anyway."

Tolan came and stood at the end of the table.

"You've just put eleven people in a barn on a hill and told a man in Thornwall he can have them for whatever he's paying, and you've done it in the same week that the ford went out and the same month a wagon with nothing on it drove down the Ashgrove road eleven times." He put his hands on the table. "That's four things happening in one season, and three of them are things nobody in this valley did."

Hask looked up. "You think they're one thing."

"I think a man who could take out a causeway could take eleven people out of a barn if he wanted to, and I think he hasn't, and I think that means either he can't, or he's waiting." Tolan straightened. "I'd like twenty men on that barn and I'd like them changed every night, and I'd like the road watched from the ford to the cut."

"You'll have four men and a lock," said Hask, "because I have eighty horses and no grain and I cannot put twenty men on a hill to guard eleven villagers from a thing that has not happened."

"That's the whole of your reasoning?"

"That's the whole of my reasoning."

Tolan stood there a moment longer.

"Then I'd like to write it down," he said, "that I asked."

"Write it down," said Hask. "You're the only man in this valley who does, and it will hang you one day, and I'd rather it hung you than me."

Ferrin was taken with his hands visible and did the sensible thing, and then he spent four days in a barn and had a good deal of time to think, and the thing he thought about most was that he was fifty and had been headman for nine years and had never once had to do anything harder than decide who got the first use of the mill.

There were four of them in that barn for four days and they argued, which is what people do.

Ferrin said that the whole of it would come down as soon as the Count heard, and that a Count with an interest in the eastern marches was a Count who would send somebody, and that they should be patient and quiet and keep the girl warm.

His wife said that the Count had never come in fifty years and would not come now, and that patience was a thing that men asked of women who had less.

The boy of fifteen with the bad foot said nothing at all for two days and then said the thing that turned out to be right, which was: "Ferrin, you're sat in a barn with a lock on it and you're telling us to be patient, and there's nobody in this valley who knows where we are except the men who put us here. It'll not be the Count that comes. It'll be somebody who wanted something that wasn't us."

Ferrin remembered that afterwards. He would remember it for years and would tell the story badly, and always with the same sentence in the middle: that it was the boy with the bad foot who understood it first, and he was fifteen, and he was right, and the thing that came for them did not come from the Count.

Hask went out on the wall afterwardsHask went out on the wall afterwards, which was a thing he did when he had made a decision he did not like and had no one to tell.

The valley lay below him in the dark. A hundred and six men in a hall and thirty houses and a stone floor in a barn and a road that went nowhere except out over the cut, which was seven miles of nothing above the line where any man would put a road.

He had come here eleven years ago with fourteen men and a grudge and a great deal of patience. He had bought the land rather than taken it. He had married into a family rather than killing them, and had only killed the one who complained, and had done that in a way that had not been traceable. He had paid on the day, every time, for eleven years, and he remembered the names of his stable boys, and there was not a man in that valley who would say he had been cheated.

And he was, this evening, a man who had sent twenty-two men to a village to take eleven people and put them in a barn for a buyer in Thornwall.

*That's the thing about patience,* he thought. *It is a way of not deciding. And then a winter comes and the decision is made for you and it is a smaller decision than the one you'd have made yourself.*

He had not wanted this. He wanted to be a baron. He had wanted it for eleven years in the specific, patient, arithmetic way that he wanted everything, and the whole of the plan had been to be worth a title and to wait until somebody noticed, and nobody had ever noticed, and now he was a man with a hundred and six men and a barn on a hill and a buyer in Thornwall.

He thought about the man of fifty against the far wall with his eyes open, who had been looking at the gap in the door for four days.

*He'd have made a decent headman in a bigger valley,* Hask thought. *He'd have held a road for me. He'd have counted the grain and told me when I was being a fool and I'd have kept him.*

That was the thing nobody who had never done it would ever understand: the waste. Not the burning. The waste of a man like that, sitting against a wall in a barn, because he had been born eleven miles the wrong side of a river and had never once been asked.

Behind him, in the hall, a hundred men were eating grain they did not have.

Hask Veyr stood on the wall for a while longer and then went in, and did not sleep, and was not the sort of man who would have said so.
