Hask Veyr had a hundred and six men, a hall with a slate roof, and a winter coming with no bread in it.

He had come to the high valley eleven years ago with fourteen men and a bitter grudge. The grudge had lasted two seasons before burning itself out, replaced by a colder and more durable discipline: arithmetic.

In his youth he had been an armsman in a great lord's household two hundred miles south, holding a timber bridge over the rapid waters of the Severn for a baron who never once rode out to inspect the piers. Hask had spent eight months in mail, living on salt herrings and sour ale, bandaging pike-wounds with sheep-grease. When the campaign ended and the muster rolled in, Hask had received eleven marks less than a well-born squire who had spent the entire month asleep in a dry feather-bed. When he had asked the paymaster why, he had been told courteously that the discrepancy was a matter of noble blood rather than honest service. He had returned to his tent, thought about that sentence for seven days, collected his silver, and marched north into the wild hills with eleven veterans who trusted his judgment.

They had taken the high Veyr valley slowly, by purchase and marriage rather than slaughter. He had bought the first sheepfold with his severance coin, married the widow of the second holding, and executed only the single bailiff who had attempted to set fire to his winter hay. He had lived with the relentless care of a man walking a narrow plank over a ravine, spending eleven years proving he was worth a patent of nobility.

He had built a sound stone hall with blue slate on the roof, quarried from the high crags. He had maintained eighty sound highland horses, cleared a cart road over the rocky cut, and kept the peace across three marcher parishes so thoroughly that peddlers traveled without armed escorts.

Nobody had noticed.

That was the bitter truth he had stopped trying to explain to Tolan. A warlord who behaves with absolute propriety in an isolated valley does not receive a patent from the King. He merely becomes a permanent, uncompensated fixture in the heather. Prudence, which was supposed to be the tool to purchase standing, had quietly become the cage that held him.

Now the arithmetic had turned against him. A hundred and six men consumed a bushel and a half of grain each day in bread alone. Eighty horses required double that weight in oats and hay, for a mount that starved in November was dead in February, and a captain without mounts had a hundred footmen who could not leave the valley.

The winter supply that should have crossed the Veyr river in September lay stranded on the southern bank, forty miles off. The causeway had failed, drowned beneath eleven feet of green water. He had dispatched factors to Harrowmere, but the grain merchants had demanded thirty-four shillings on the quarter—a price that made his teeth grind—and smiled with the icy politeness of men instructed by their betters to bleed him dry.

There were only three ways to survive the frost, and two were ruinous.

The first was to purchase grain at exorbitant prices until his strongbox was empty in January, leaving him with a hundred and six armed men who had not received their wage.

The second was to disband forty horsemen before Michaelmas. That was the cautious choice, but Hask had rejected it after a single sleepless night: a captain who dismisses forty veterans never recovers them, and a man with sixty riders and no noble title is merely a wealthy farmer waiting for an ambitious neighbor to hang him.

The third was to take what he needed with steel.

He had known since mid-September that it would come to steel. What he had not anticipated was how deeply he would despise the necessity.

---

"You will not go to Lowmere," Tolan said, leaning against the timber post in the private solar.

Hask did not look up from his account roll. "I will not."

"It is a hamlet of eight cottages and forty souls, and one of them is a child," Tolan continued, his voice hard and even. "If you are absent, the men will do what unmastered riders do when there is no captain to watch them."

"I know what riders do."

"Then mount your horse and lead them."

Hask turned around, laying both hands upon the table.

"Tolan. If I ride into Lowmere, then the raid is my raid, the plunder is my plunder, and every crofter will remember my grey mare and my badger-skin collar. When spring comes, a Warden of the Ash will ride east to investigate the outrage, and forty marcher folk will swear under oath that Hask Veyr led the fire."

"And if you remain here?"

"Then an outrage occurred on the border, committed by unruly hirelings whose wages were in arrears while their lord sat in his hall," Hask said coldly. "I will not be at Lowmere because I intend to tell the Warden, truthfully and under formal interrogation, that I had no hand in the violence."

Tolan looked at him for several seconds, his jaw set like granite.

"That is a very fine distinction."

"It is the only distinction between a reiver and a prospective baron."

"It is neither," Tolan countered. "You have a hundred and six experienced lances and an intact fortress. You could march south next week, offer your swords to Duke Varyn, and hold a royal border with a guaranteed wage and no starving crofters on your conscience. I have watched you refuse that road for two years."

Hask said nothing. His silence was complete and deliberate, and Tolan knew the argument was finished.

---

Later that afternoon, Captain Berrin stood before the high board, his thumbs hooked in his sword belt.

"We strike Vale manor," Berrin urged, his broad face flushed with impatient arrogance. "Their outer wall is eight feet of rubble-stone, and they keep four armsmen in the yard. I walked their ditch three weeks ago. I can breach their gatehouse before noon, take their winter oats and thirty prime horses, and be back in the high valley before Edric's tenants have fetched their billhooks."

"You could not," Hask said calmly.

"I walked the ground myself."

"You walked the lane in fair weather with your cloak tucked under your arm, which proves you have never looked at a defensive position in your life," Hask retorted, setting his pewter cup down with a sharp ring. "The Vale rampart has a stone gatehouse and four heavy timber leaves. Corin Ahl sleeps in the guardroom with his boots laced, and the second son has broken three men's ribs in the tournament lists. You would not capture their granary. You would find yourself entangled in a protracted siege, every tenant in the valley would muster with bows, the Duke would receive three petitions before nightfall, and I would be branded a common outlaw trapped in a box canyon."

"So we starve in this hall like dogs?" Berrin spat.

"We act with discretion," Hask commanded. "Discretion is the commodity I have spent eleven years acquiring. I have purchased silence from eleven factors in Thornwall, from a Duke who dislikes traveling east of his cathedral, and from forty bailiffs who ask no questions about the Veyr passes. A commander who has purchased that much peace does not burn a nobleman's courtyard because winter is approaching."

He tapped the table with his knuckles.

"You will take twenty-two men to Lowmere at dusk. You will secure their grain store, take their sound cart-horses, and bring back four wagonloads of rye. If a man resists, knock him down with a cudgel. You will touch no women and shed no blood. If you kill a single cottager, Berrin, I will hang you from the gatehouse arch before your saddles are cold."

Berrin scowled, muttered a sullen acknowledgment, and left the hall.

Hask sat alone, looking at the sheepskin map. The border felt unstable under his palms, like a heavy oak table resting on uneven flagstones.

"He is wrong about the Vale gate," Tolan said, stepping from the shadow of the doorway. "What he is not wrong about is that we have run out of ground."

---

Lowmere lay eleven miles east of the Veyr, tucked into a marshy hollow beneath the high crags. It contained forty souls, an unworked watermill, two thatched barns, and a granary full of dry harvest rye.

Berrin's troop arrived at twilight, their horses trotting through the boundary ditch without a sound.

The village headman, Ferrin, was fifty years old. He had served as village reeve for nine seasons, during which his most challenging dispute had been allocating the first turn of the millstones after harvest. When twenty-two mounted men surrounded his threshing floor, he stepped into the courtyard with his palms raised and did the sensible thing: he offered them the grain.

"Take the rye," Ferrin said, his voice trembling but clear. "And the four draft horses in the byre. Two are sound for the harness."

"We will take the wagons," Berrin replied, dismounting with a heavy clatter of iron. "And we will take eleven laborers to drive them."

Ferrin went rigid. "Laborers?"

"Thornwall merchants have contracted for stone-clearing along the high cut," Berrin lied, gesturing to his men. "They need hands, and they will pay your keep until Candlemas. You come with us, Ferrin, along with four of your field hands, three women for the bakery, a boy for the horses, and two others."

"My wife is sixty-one," Ferrin said desperately.

"Then she remains in her bed," Berrin snapped. "We will take your granddaughter instead."

Ferrin's wife stood in the kitchen doorway, wrapped in an old woolen shawl, watching twenty-two armed horsemen bundle their nine-year-old granddaughter into a wagon under an oiled tarpaulin. She did not scream, and she did not throw stones; an elderly woman in an isolated hamlet understands what a scream is worth when spears are drawn. She stood on the threshold in the biting dusk, unmoving, until the last hoofbeat died in the gorse.

The raiders loaded four farm wains with ninety bushels of grain, hitched the village teams, and fired the two empty barns to draw pursuit away from the mountain road.

The entire operation took forty minutes.

---

On the last day of October, Hask paid his garrison.

It was an ordeal that lasted two hours, conducted with meticulous formality. Hask sat at the head of the trestle with a small iron scales and an open chest, counting silver pennies into each man's palm. It was one of the eleven habits that had kept his troop intact through five winters of mountain cold.

This evening, the oak chest was visibly half-empty. The men took their silver in silence, their eyes darting to the bare bottom planks. The absence of complaint was far more alarming than mutiny; men who grumble are still bargaining, while men who fall silent are already calculating their departure.

Tolan stood against the wall, observing the reckoning with crossed arms.

"You will have to address them," Tolan said when the hall had cleared.

"I will address them when I have secured our winter stores," Hask replied, closing the iron clasp.

"They can count silver, Hask. They have known our position since the causeway went under."

"Then they know precisely what I know, and further speeches are superfluous."

"There is a difference between enduring hardship and suspecting your captain is concealing failure," Tolan said, stepping forward. "I have served as a soldier since I was fifteen years old. The thing that breaks men faster than hunger is never hunger; it is the realization that the commander has lost his grip on the road."

Hask rested his hand upon the strongbox. "Then you tell them. They respect you."

"They do not respect me; they fear my judgment, which is why I remain alive in your hall," Tolan said flatly. "I will not soothe them with falsehoods. And when this season unravels, remember that I offered you an honest deployment in October, and you preferred silence."

---

Berrin made his report an hour later, standing with his spurs jingling on the flagstones.

"Four wagons of rye, four horses, and eleven captives," Berrin announced, grinning broadly. "Two barns burned to the turf. Not a single cottager was slain."

"No dead," Hask repeated, looking up from his parchment.

"One reeve took a blow from an ash haft. He will mend."

Hask paused. "Where have you secured the captives?"

"In the old sheep barn above the river gorge," Berrin said. "The timber is sound, the iron lock is secure, and it sits two hours off the common drove. I placed four guards on the door."

"Four men to watch eleven hostages?"

"Four veteran archers over a bolted barn in an unmapped ravine," Berrin said with a shrug. "A regiment could not find them."

Hask nodded, dismissing him with a brief gesture.

As the heavy oak door swung shut, Tolan stepped out of the shadows. He had listened to the entire exchange without moving a muscle.

"Speak your mind," Hask sighed, rubbing his temples.

"You have imprisoned eleven marcher peasants in an isolated barn," Tolan said, his voice dangerously low. "You have accepted a contract from Thornwall to seize labor, and you have committed this outrage in the exact month that our river causeway dissolved without a flood, and an empty Vale wagon began patrolling the Ashgrove road."

Hask looked up sharply. "You see a connection."

"A commander who can dissolve forty tons of underwater masonry without leaving a footstep can extract eleven captives from a sheepfold whenever he chooses," Tolan said, leaning across the table. "He has not struck because he is observing us. He is waiting for you to overextend your line. I want twenty archers stationed at that barn, relieved every twenty-four hours, and outposts established from the river to the high cut."

Hask shook his head. "You will have four men and an iron padlock. I have eighty starving horses and three months of frost ahead. I cannot dedicate twenty men to guard eleven villagers against an imaginary enemy."

"That is your final order?"

"That is my final order."

Tolan stood in silence for several long heartbeats.

"Then I will record in my journal that I formally requested twenty lances," Tolan said.

"Record whatever you please," Hask replied wearily. "You are the only man in this troop who wastes ink on history. It will hang you one day, and I would rather it hung you than me."

---

On Sunday morning, Hask rode out to the sheep barn alone.

He told no one in the hall of his destination. He rode a sure-footed black cob through the tangled birch scrub, following the deer trails up the gorge until he reached the clearing.

The four sentries were huddled around a small charcoal brazier beneath an overhang of rock. When they saw their chieftain emerge from the birch thicket, they sprang to their feet in frantic haste, adjusting their billhooks.

Hask waved them back to their fire without speaking.

He walked to the heavy barn door. The oak planks were weathered grey and reinforced with iron strapwork, secured by a heavy chain and padlock. Between the third and fourth planks, the timber had shrunk, leaving a narrow crack an inch wide.

Hask stepped close and peered into the dim interior.

He counted eleven figures by the grey light filtering through the roof slats.

In the far corner, a woman sat on a bed of bracken, holding the sleeping nine-year-old girl wrapped in an old woolen cloak. Four field hands sat with their heads propped against their knees, one of them snoring with exhausted rhythm. A young boy with a bandaged ankle was whittling a stick of ash with an iron nail.

Directly opposite the door sat the headman, Ferrin.

The fifty-year-old reeve was sitting upright against the stone wall, his hands folded quietly in his lap. His face was bruised and lined with deep fatigue, but his eyes were open, steady, and fixed directly upon the crack in the door.

He did not call out. He did not plead for water or curse his captors. He sat in the cold shadows with the patient, unmoving dignity of an elder who understood exactly what had befallen his people, and who refused to grant his enemies the satisfaction of his fear.

Hask stepped back from the timber, his throat suddenly dry.

*He knows what I am,* Hask thought, a strange, sickening coldness settling behind his ribs. *He worked it out the moment Berrin's riders rode into his yard. And he has been sitting in that dark corner for four days, keeping his folk quiet so they are not slaughtered before help arrives.*

Hask turned his back on the barn and mounted his horse.

He did not exchange a single word with the four guards. He spurred the cob down the rocky gully, riding hard through the freezing mist toward his stone hall.

The valley below was grey, barren, and wrapped in November frost. Above the rooflines, the slate tiles of his manor gleamed with cold moisture.

He had spent eleven years building an honorable name in this frontier. He had paid every debt on the appointed day, remembered the names of his stable boys, and refused to pillage his neighbors.

And now, for four wagons of rye and a purse of Thornwall silver, he was a warlord who starved children in a locked barn.

*Patience is merely a respectable name for cowardice,* Hask thought, dismounting in the empty courtyard. *You wait eleven years for a lord to acknowledge your worth, and when the winter comes, you discover you have become the very monster you refused to serve.*

He walked into the dark hall, barred the door behind him, and sat before his dying fire without removing his spurs.
