The news reached Vale on Saturday night, and Corin Ahl heard it before the baron did.

Watt had sent an upland lad on a blown pony that collapsed in the lower lane, and the boy had come staggering up the gravel shouting of fire. By the time the dogs in the kennels began to bay, Corin was already running across the yard with his boots laced and a torch in his fist.

Lowmere had been struck at dusk. Four wagonloads of harvest rye seized. Two barns burned to the sod. Eleven people dragged into the hills, including Headman Ferrin and a nine-year-old child.

Corin climbed the tower stairs two at a time, finding Baron Edric in the upper corridor with a wool mantle thrown hastily over his linen nightshirt. Corin delivered the report without softening a single syllable; an armsman who minces words in the dark is worse than a coward.

Edric stood unmoving in the draughty passage, his hand resting against the cold lime-wash of the wall.

"Wake Gareth," the baron said at last. "And wake Captain Jory. Leave the others."

"My lord."

"Where were the riders based, Corin?"

"Nobody knows," Corin answered grimly. "Lowmere lies eleven miles east of the Veyr ravine. There has never been an outpost on that marsh."

"There should have been an outpost on that marsh," Edric said, his voice heavy with self-reproach.

"Aye, my lord," said Corin Ahl. "There should."

He went down to the barracks to rouse Gareth and Jory, acutely aware of the sinister pattern hardening across the autumn: an empty hay-wain run down the Ashgrove road as bait; an ancient river causeway dissolved without tool marks; a shepherd girl scouting empty monolithic gates along the high cut; and now eleven villagers chained in a mountain sheepfold. Every thread was tightening, yet he could not see the hand pulling the cords.

---

The baron did what a lawful marcher lord was trained to do, and Corin stood by the stable archway and watched him do it.

By Sunday afternoon, four dispatches had been drafted on the study desk. One to the royal bailiff at Harven, which was Edric's fifth petition in two seasons and would likely languish in an antechamber for twelve weeks. One to Duke Varyn, which Edric wrote twice, read aloud to Jory, and burned in the hearth before drafting a shorter, more desperate appeal. One to Baron Osric of Thornwall, phrased with formal courtesy, asking whether Thornwall patrols had observed armed horsemen crossing the northern border.

The fourth letter went to the Bishop of the Ashen Word at Harrowmere. Edric had sworn three years ago that he would never petition the church for temporal protection, and the humiliation of signing the parchment scored deep lines into his forehead. Corin watched him press his signet ring into the red wax, feeling the sorrow of an honorable lord whose authority was leaking away like water through cracked slate.

On Monday dawn, Edric rode out with Gareth, Jory, and nine mounted armsmen. They scouted east through forty miles of sodden heath, returning at nightfall on Tuesday with exhausted horses and nothing accomplished.

Corin met them at the gate, took the baron's foaming gelding, and followed Edric into the hall. The baron stood before the cold hearth with his travel-stained cloak still pinned at his throat.

"Did you reach the hamlet, my lord?" Corin asked quietly.

"I reached the ashes," Edric replied, not turning around. "The cottage walls stand empty. The thatch is charcoal. There is no garrison to confront, Corin. No enemy line, no banner, no camp to besiege. There is only a wild valley eleven miles east held by a hundred highland lances, behind a river where the ford is gone. I have eleven armsmen in my yard, four unanswered letters on my desk, and eleven marcher subjects chained in a sheepfold. Mirra Cott died in August, and I could not save her; forty souls were plundered at Lowmere, and I cannot fetch them back."

Gareth stepped forward into the firelight, his young face flushed with dark, furious misery. He was twenty-one years old, tall and broad-shouldered, with the scabbard of his broadsword banging against his iron greaves.

"Let me take ten horsemen into the hills, Father," Gareth urged, his voice cracking with strain. "We can cross the upper cut by night and burn their winter stables."

"We would be ten corpses in a mountain ravine," Edric said flatly. "You have fought in border skirmishes, Gareth. You know the highland terrain. The moment you enter the Veyr passes, you are in a stone cul-de-sac. What you are offering is not a tactical deployment; it is a temper tantrum."

Gareth stood rigid, his fists clenched at his sides. He had never been addressed with such stinging contempt in his life.

"Then what is the purpose of me?" Gareth shouted, his voice echoing off the timber beams. "What is the purpose of the sword, the drills, the armor, and the name I have earned in this province? If we cannot ride out when eleven of our folk are stolen, what is our house for?"

Edric turned his grey, weary gaze upon his third son.

"It is for the day a crisis arrives that can be met by stone walls and honest steel," the baron said softly. "I am grieved that today is not that day. And I will not throw your life away in a rocky ditch merely to make you feel like a knight."

The baron walked out of the hall, his boots dragging on the flagstones.

Gareth stood alone by the trestle, his shoulders trembling. Corin remained motionless in the doorway, saying nothing, knowing that any word of comfort would sound like an insult to a young man whose honor had just been stripped bare.

---

By Friday, the paralysis of House Vale was common knowledge across the parish.

At midday, Vesna Orrow walked through the postern gate into the stable courtyard. Her dark wool skirt was caked with red upland mud, her woolen wrap pulled tight against the bitter wind, her horn-handled knife resting against her hip.

She stopped in front of Corin as he was greasing an axle-tree.

"When are you riding out?" she demanded without greeting.

Corin set the tallow rag upon the spoke. "We are not riding out."

"Eleven people are in that sheepfold," she said, stepping closer. "Four field hands, three women, a reeve, two lads, and a nine-year-old child."

"I have the tally," Corin answered steadily. "And Hask has a hundred lances and forty archers guarding the valley approaches. What path would you have us take?"

"Cross the high cut in the dark," Vesna snapped. "Take four men with short bows, slip through the monolith gates, reach the sheepfold before dawn, and lead them out."

"And then?" Corin challenged. "How do you march an old reeve, three weeping women, and a nine-year-old girl forty miles across open shale in broad daylight without eighty highland horsemen running you down? You would be butchered before you crossed the second beck."

"I would rather take the risk than rot in this yard," Vesna retorted.

"I know you would," said Corin. "And so would Gareth. But the only thing worse than eleven villagers in an upland barn is eleven villagers dead in a gully alongside the only three people who might have saved them."

Vesna stared at him, her dark eyes fierce with disgust. "Then what was the point of all the running? What was the point of carrying your sealed letters across the moor?"

Corin took a slow breath. He had anticipated that question for six days, and he had no defense that sounded noble.

"Come into the guardroom," Corin said. "I will give you an honest answer, even if you hate the sound of it."

---

The guardroom was warm, smelling of peat smoke and gun-metal oil. A rack of eleven ash-hafted spears stood against the plastered wall.

Corin sat upon the heavy bench, resting his elbows on his knees. "Sit down, Vesna. I am thirty-five years old, my knees ache when the frost bites, and I will not deliver hard truths while standing on one leg."

Vesna took the stool opposite, perching on its edge like a hawk ready to fly.

"Here is the truth," Corin said. "We are waiting because the person who directs this enterprise has not given the word."

"That is a coward's shield," she said.

"It is a soldier's discipline," Corin corrected. "I have served in this yard for eighteen years. I have stood at gates, checked bridles, and carried out small duties for men who did not know what was happening beyond their own orchard. But in that manor house sits a boy of sixteen who has not uttered three words about Lowmere all week. I know how Casian looks when he is calculating ground. He has looked like that since Saturday. He is deciding the move, and when he gives the command, he will be right."

Vesna tilted her head, her brow furrowing with incredulity. "You are trusting an untried boy."

"I am trusting what my own eyes have witnessed," Corin said, leaning toward her. "In eighteen years I have never seen an armsman site an ambush cleaner than that empty wagon on the Ashgrove road. I watched him dismantle a freestone river causeway nineteen miles away without a sound. I do not understand his power, and I have stopped asking how it operates. But in this county, most folk spend their lives taking orders from fools who know nothing. Casian knows. And I decided, in this very room after midnight, that I would rather follow a master who understands the board than be proper, slow, and dead."

He paused, studying her face.

"Now consider your own conduct, Vesna. When you walked the high cut and found those four ancient stone gates, why did you bring the news to a sixteen-year-old gardener rather than Baroness Calla?"

Vesna sat motionless, her fingers tightening around her ash cudgel.

"You don't need to answer," Corin said softly. "You walked forty miles a night through freezing rain for that lad. You came down off the moor today because you wanted to strike a blow, but you also came because you wanted to know what he intends. You are waiting for him just as I am."

"I want to know what he is," Vesna whispered.

Corin looked at the timber floor. "So do I."

---

Corin walked to his quarters behind the brew-house after compline. The frost had settled over the cobbles, crackling beneath his boots.

Inside the four-room cottage, a single tallow candle burned on the pine table. His wife, Ivet, was seated by the hearth, mending a wool stocking.

"The upland girl was shouting in the yard," Ivet observed, not looking up from her needle.

"Aye."

"Good," she said. "You have paced this house like a man hauling a granite block since Sunday. When your baron faces a border trouble, you look merely irritated. When you carry something secret, you look like a condemned man."

Corin sat down at the bench, laying his calloused hands upon the timber. "I have committed myself to a road, Ivet. And the boy who laid the stones knows far more than he admits. I told Vesna tonight that I would rather follow someone who understands the ground than be slow and correct."

"That is the soundest sentence you have spoken in five seasons," she said calmly.

"I drove the wagon myself," Corin murmured. "For eighteen years I stayed within the palisade, escorting carts and minding gates. I took the reins because it felt like the first purposeful service I had ever been offered. But eleven people are locked in a mountain barn tonight, and if they are carried into slavery, it is because we did not intervene."

Ivet set her mending in her lap and met his gaze.

"Did you alert the boy?"

"I did."

"Then the responsibility rests upon his shoulders," she said firmly. "You are an armsman, Corin, not Providence. You have served eighteen years without losing your sleep. Do your duty, keep your feet dry, and let the boy carry the weight he asked for."

Corin looked at her level, compassionate face, letting out a long, ragged sigh. "That is why I married you."

"A sensible choice," she said, picking up her needle. "Go to bed."

---

On Sunday evening, Corin sat in the guardroom with a sheepskin map pinned beneath two iron candlesticks.

Vesna sat near the door, having returned from the upland at dusk to await the resolution.

The oak door swung inward, and Casian entered alone. He carried an unlit brass lantern and a folded sheet of parchment, his breath misting in the freezing air of the yard.

Casian closed the heavy door, dropped the iron latch into place, and laid the parchment upon the table. His youthful face was stripped of all casual charm; his brown eyes were cold, steady, and terrifyingly clear.

"You have had a bitter week, Corin," Casian said, pulling out a stool.

"Aye," Corin replied.

"And you believe the failure is yours."

"It is mine," Vesna interrupted from the corner, her voice sharp with suppressed anguish. "I scouted the high cut nine times. I discovered the four gates, and I never crossed the ridge to see what lay beyond. If I had walked four miles further east in September, I would have spotted their troop before they saddled a single horse."

Casian turned toward her, listening with grave stillness.

"You followed your instructions," Casian said.

"Following instructions left forty villagers burned out," she retorted. "I would rather have been insubordinate in September than blameless in November."

Casian studied her for several seconds. "From this night onward, Vesna, when I assign you a sector, you scout until you are certain or until you are observed. You will report what you found, not merely what you were instructed to seek."

"That is an undisciplined command," she said.

"It is the only command that keeps our folk alive," Casian answered. "Now listen to the ground."

He unfolded the map, tapping the marked sheepfold above the river gorge.

"The eleven captives are held in an upland barn two hours from the high cut," Casian said. "Four archers guard the door. They expect to move the prisoners down to Thornwall before the first heavy blizzard."

Corin leaned forward. "We can strike on Tuesday?"

"We strike on Wednesday," Casian said.

Corin frowned, his brow furrowing. "Why delay until Wednesday? Every night they spend in that freezing barn increases the hazard."

"Because I chose the moment that settles the entire account," Casian said, his voice dropping into absolute, unyielding clarity. "I could have extracted those villagers on Saturday night. I could have dissolved their chains before Berrin returned to his chieftain, and Lowmere would have eleven folk home with another tale of mountain fog."

Corin stared at him, his breath catching. "You could have freed them Saturday?"

"I could have," Casian said evenly. "And had I done so, Hask Veyr would still possess eighty starving horses and no grain. He would have spent all winter launching raids against every isolated farmstead along our border. I would be hunting ghosts in the heather for six months, while Hask grew more cautious and struck weaker targets."

He placed his palm flat upon the table.

"By waiting until Wednesday, Hask has committed his full strength. He has plundered rye, burned property, and entered into an open conspiracy with buyers in Thornwall. He has drawn every hidden accomplice into the light. I want the entire alliance exposed. Not in the spring. This week."

A heavy, suffocating silence descended upon the stone chamber.

"You let them sit in that barn as bait," Corin whispered, the realization striking him like a blow.

"I permitted an enemy to commit his crime so that I may eradicate him entirely," Casian answered without flinching. "Tell me what you think, Corin."

Corin Ahl looked across the table at the sixteen-year-old boy. The candle flame flickered, casting long, sharp shadows across Casian's unyielding features.

"I am thinking," Corin said slowly, "that I have served in this yard for eighteen years, and I have never feared an armed man, and I do not fear you. I am thinking that if those eleven captives return to their homes on Wednesday, I will follow your banner wherever it leads. And if they perish in that barn, I will be a fool who sat in a guardroom and let a boy explain murder as arithmetic."

"Fair terms," Casian murmured.

"And I am thinking," Corin added, "that you would have delivered that exact speech whether they live or die, because you do not alter your words to make a room comfortable."

Casian folded the map and rose from the table.

"On Wednesday night, Corin, have Watt's wagon ready at the crossroad. Take Vesna and two fresh mounts. Remain half a mile below the ridge."

"Will there be blood?" Corin asked.

"Four sentries will die," Casian said calmly. "I will manage the barrier myself, from a distance, without steel. I would prefer you remained where you cannot see the deed."

Corin stood up, his hand resting on the hilt of his heavy knife.

"I will be on the ridge," Corin said. "And I will watch. If I am to follow a power that knows, I intend to see what that power looks like when it strikes."

Casian inclined his head in silent acknowledgment, opened the door, and walked out into the freezing night.

---

Corin followed him to the threshold and watched the boy cross the dark gravel alone.

Casian walked with his hands tucked into his plain coat, his shoulders squared against the biting north wind, completely unhurried. There was no escort, no lantern light, no martial stride—only the solitary silhouette of a sixteen-year-old youth who had calmly decided when four men would die and when eleven captives would walk home.

Corin stood watching until the boy's dark figure dissolved beneath the bare black boughs of the walnut tree. The yard was utterly still, the frost glittering like salt upon the stones.

He thought of the four letters on the baron's study desk, the proud, futile riding of the lances, and the cold arithmetic of Wednesday night. He looked down at his broad, scarred palms, pulled the heavy oak door shut, and drove the iron bolt home.
