The news came to Vale on the Saturday night and Corin heard it before the baron did, because Watt sent a boy with a horse that had one hour of run left in it and the boy came up the lane shouting, and by the time the household was awake Corin was already in the yard with his boots on.

Forty-one people. Eleven taken. Two barns.

He went up to the house and found the baron in the passage in a coat over his nightshirt, and told him plainly, because there was no way to say it that was less than that.

Edric stood there for a long moment.

"Wake the two eldest," he said. "And Jory. Nobody else."

"My lord."

"And Corin. Where were the riders?"

"Nobody knows. Lowmere's eleven miles east of the ford and there's nobody watching it."

"There should have been somebody watching it."

"Yes," said Corin Ahl. "There should."

He went to wake Gareth and to wake Jory, and while he was doing it he was aware in a way he did not like of the shape of the autumn rearranging itself behind him: eleven men on a road with a wagon, a ford taken out by nobody, a girl on the cut, and now eleven people in a barn somewhere in the hills, and none of it connected by any line he could draw.

---

The baron did the things the baron could do, and Corin stood in the yard and watched him do them.

By Sunday afternoon there were four letters. One to Harven, which was the fifth letter to Harven in two years and would be answered in eleven weeks if it were answered at all. One to Varyn, which was the first, and which Edric wrote twice and read aloud and then burned and wrote again shorter. One to the Baron of Thornwall, which was courteous and asked whether any of Osric's people had seen riders in the north of his land. One to the Bishop of the Ashen Word at Harrowmere, which Edric said he would not write and wrote anyway, and which cost him something to write, and Corin could see what it cost him when he sealed it.

And on Monday he rode out with Gareth and eleven men and went east and came back at dark on Tuesday having accomplished nothing, which was not his fault and which he knew was his fault, and Corin walked into the hall and found the baron standing at the window with his coat still on.

"My lord. Did you get to Lowmere?"

"I got to the edge of it."

"And?"

"And there is nothing there." Edric did not turn round. "There are forty-one people in this county who have been burned out or taken, and there is no enemy in a place. There is no army to answer, no man to write to, no village to defend. There is a valley eleven miles east with a hundred and six men in it and a road to it that has one crossing and the crossing is gone, and I have eleven men, and I have written five letters and burned one of them, and the woman who made the bread in this house is dead and I could not do a thing about that in August and I cannot do a thing about this in October."

"Then let me go in."

"No."

"Da—"

"We would be eleven men in a valley with one road out of it and you know it, because you are the only one of my sons who has actually been in a fight, and you have been sitting in this hall for six days trying to work out how to say the thing that is in your head, and the thing in your head is not a plan, it is a wish."

Gareth, who was twenty-one and had never once been spoken to in that tone in his life, stood very still.

"Then what's the point of me?"

"Of you?"

"Of the sword," said Gareth. "Of all of it. The wall and the eleven men and the drills and the name I've got in this county that men say without laughing." His voice was not steady. "If it can't be used, what's it for?"

Edric turned round then.

"It is for the day a thing happens that could be met by a wall," he said, "and I am sorry that today is not that day, and I am not going to send you to die in a valley to make it feel like it was."

He went out. Gareth stood in the hall.

Corin, in the doorway, did not say anything, because there was nothing to say that would not have been an insult.

---

By the Tuesday it was clear that Vale was not going to do anything---

By the Tuesday it was clear that Vale was not going to do anything, and by the Thursday it was clear to everybody, and by the Friday Vesna Orrow had walked down off the upland and stood in the middle of the yard and told him so.

"I've come to ask a thing and then I'll go back."

"Ask it."

"When are you going in?"

Corin looked at her.

"I'm not."

"Eleven people."

"Eleven people, and a hundred and six men, and forty of them two hours from a road and the rest of them in a hall with a wall round it." He put down what he was carrying. "Show me the plan you'd like me to follow."

"Walk the cut to the ford in a night, take four men, and be at that barn before it's light."

"And then?"

"Then eleven people walk home."

"Through a hundred and six men in a valley with one road out of it." Corin shook his head. "You'd be seen before the barn. There's a mill and there's four houses and there's one crossing and it's gone. You'd be three days getting those eleven down off that hill, in daylight, and on the second day you'd be dead and so would they."

"I'd like to try it."

"I know you would," said Corin. "And I'd like to try it. And I'm not going to, and the reason I'm not going to is that the only thing worse than eleven people in a barn is eleven people in a barn and the only three men in this county who could get them out."

Vesna stood in the yard with her hands at her sides.

"Then what's the point of any of it?"

There it was. Corin had been waiting eleven days for somebody to ask him that and had been practising an answer and found, in the moment, that he did not have one.

"That's a good question," he said. "Come down to the guardroom and I'll tell you a bad answer."

---

The guardroom was warm and had a rack of eleven spears and a good floor and a chest with a map in it.

"Sit," said Corin.

"I'll stand."

"Sit down, girl. I'm thirty-five and my feet are bad and I'll not stand through my own bad answers."

Vesna sat.

"Here's the bad answer," said Corin. "We are waiting because the man who decides is not us."

"That's not an answer, that's a wall."

"It's a wall," Corin agreed. "And I've been standing behind it for thirty-five years, and the thing I've learned about walls is that they're only worth anything if you're on the right side of them, and I've never been sure which side that is." He rubbed his face. "There's a boy of sixteen in that house who has not said a word about Lowmere since Saturday, and I know what his face looks like when he's working something out, and his face has looked like that for six days, and if you asked me what he's doing I would say he is deciding, and that when he decides he will tell me, and that he will be right."

Vesna stared at him.

"You're trusting a boy."

"I'm trusting a thing I've seen," said Corin Ahl. "I've been an armsman thirty-five years and I've had eleven men and three words, and I have watched that boy put a light on the Ashgrove road and find a fire square on a drover's track and take a ford out of a river without showing anybody how, and I do not know how he does it and I have stopped wanting to know."

"You've stopped wanting to know."

"Girl, I've been in the dark counting. Most men spend their lives being told what's happening by somebody who doesn't know. That boy knows. And I have decided — this autumn, in this room, at some hour after midnight — that I would rather be a man who follows a thing that knows than a man who is correct and slow." He put both hands on his knees. "Now I'll ask you the question instead. When you ran the cut, and you found the gates with no gates in them, and you came back and told it to a boy of sixteen — what did you decide?"

Vesna said nothing.

"You don't have to answer it," said Corin. "I'm not the man who signs the letters and I'd bust you for lying to me. But you've walked a hundred and forty miles a night for two months for that boy in that house, and you've just told me in the yard that you'd rather be dead on a hill than waiting, and I'd like you to notice that both of those things are true and that they don't sit together."

"I want to know what he is," said Vesna.

There was a silence in the guardroom.

"So do I," said Corin.

---

He walked home in the dark because he did not sleep in the guardroom and had not for years, and Ivet was still up, which she never was.

"There's a girl in the yard shouting at you."

"Aye."

"Good," said Ivet. "Somebody should be. You've been walking round this house like a man carrying a stone up a hill since Saturday, and that's how I know it isn't a thing your baron can fix, because when it's a thing a baron can fix you look like a man carrying a small stone."

Corin sat down at the table and did not say anything for a while.

"Ivet. There's a thing I've started and I don't know what it is, and there's a boy in that big house with a walled garden who knows more than me and tells me nothing, and I've just this evening told a girl of nineteen that I'd rather follow a thing that knows than be correct and slow."

"That's the truest thing you've said in ten years."

"I took the wagon myself."

"I know you did."

"I've never taken a thing myself before," said Corin. "Thirty-five years. I've sent men and I've stood at gates and I have never once been the one who had to be somewhere. And I did it because it sounded like the first real thing that had come into that yard, and I've been trying all autumn to work out whether that's the best thing I ever did or the worst." He put his hands flat on the table. "Because there's eleven people on a hill and if they're sold in the spring then it's because nobody went in, and if nobody went in it's because a boy of sixteen was watching a road in August and the watching didn't stretch eleven miles east."

Ivet sat down opposite him.

"Did you not tell him?"

"I did tell him."

"Then the missing is his."

"That's not how it works," said Corin.

"No," said Ivet, "but it's how you sleep. And you've been doing this thirty-five years and I've never once seen you not sleep, so whatever you did then was right, so do it again."

Corin Ahl sat at his own table in the small hours.

"That's why I married you," he said, "and I'll not say it again this year."

---

He was in the yard before dawn on the Sunday, and so was she, and neither of them mentioned it.

"Master Corin."

"Mistress Orrow."

"I'll not apologise."

"Good."

"I'd like to be told when it's decided."

"You'll be told when it's decided," said Corin, "and I'll tell you myself, and I'll not be gentle about it." He turned. "And Vesna. If it comes to going in—"

"I'm going in."

"If it comes to going in," Corin said, "you'll go in front, because you're the only one of us who's ever been across that country at night, and I'll be behind you, and if there's a thing on the road I'll be the one who finds it out first."

Vesna Orrow looked at him.

"That's fair," she said, and went back to the upland.

---

The boy came to the guardroom that evening, alone, with a lamp and a folded paper and the face he had been wearing all week.

"Corin. Sit down."

"I'll stand."

"You'll sit," said Casian Vale. "It'll take an hour and it's a lot to carry, and I'd rather say it to a man who's sitting so that I can see whether it's a bad idea while I'm saying it."

The boy came in, and stood, and did not sit, and Vesna — who had come down off the upland that morning for no reason she would admit to — stayed where she was by the door.

"Corin's had a bad week," said Casian.

"Yes."

"He thinks it's his."

"It is not his." Vesna said it flatly, and then, because she had been thinking about it for six days and had to say it to somebody: "It's mine. I walked the cut nine times and I found four gates and I never once went east beyond them, because going east beyond them was not what I was paid for and nobody asked me to."

The room was quiet.

"That's a bad way round," said Casian.

"I know it's a bad way round. It's still true." She looked at him. "You gave me a thing to find and I found it and I stopped. And then eleven people were taken off a village that's four hours from the top of the cut, and I've been up and down that cut every week for two months and I never once wondered what was on the other side of the wall."

"You'd have been seen."

"Probably." Vesna's jaw was set. "I'd rather have been seen in September than useful in October."

Casian stood in the middle of the guardroom and looked at her for a long moment.

"Then from now on," he said, "when I give you a thing to find, you find it and then you keep going until you're seen or you're certain, and you tell me what you did and not what you were asked to do."

"That's a foolish thing to say to a person."

"Yes," said Casian. "It is. Ask me again in a year and I'll probably still say it."

Corin sat down.

"Lowmere," said Casian. "Eleven people in a barn above the ford, four men on it, and they'll be moved before the roads go."

"Go on."

"I could have gone in on Saturday night," said Casian. "I could have had them out of that barn before Berrin got back to the hall and nobody in that valley would ever have known how, and there would be eleven people home this week and a story about a mist."

Corin did not move.

"I didn't," said Casian.

"Why not?"

"Because of what happens in the spring." The boy put the paper down and did not open it. "If I take them out on Saturday, then in the spring Hask Veyr is a man with a hundred men and no grain and no hostages who will spend the whole winter sending out eleven men at a time to every village in this county, and I'd take those out too, and every time I do it he learns a little more about me and gets a little more careful, and the whole thing becomes a year of me doing small strange things in the dark and him doing worse things to smaller villages."

"And this way?"

"This way he's made a move," said Casian. "He's taken grain and people and burnt barns in a county that is not his, and he's done it with a buyer in Thornwall who is now holding eleven people in a barn, and every man in this county who was going to quietly profit from a war in the marches has now had a thing to profit from." He looked up. "I want them all in the light. All of them. Not next spring. Now."

There was a long silence in the guardroom.

"You're saying you let it happen," said Corin.

"I'm saying I chose what I'd answer, and I chose to answer it in a way that ends it this year, and eleven people are in a barn because of it." Casian's voice did not change at all. "I'd like you to say the thing you're thinking."

Corin Ahl looked at the boy across the table for a long moment.

"I'm thinking," he said, "that I have been in this yard thirty-five years and I have never once been afraid of a man and I am not afraid of you." He leaned forward. "I'm thinking that if those eleven come home, then I'll follow you into whatever's after, and if they don't, I'll be a man who sat in a guardroom and let a boy of sixteen explain arithmetic to me."

"Yes."

"And I'm thinking," said Corin, "that you'd have told me the same thing whether they came home or not, because you're not the sort who lies to make a room easier, and I do not know whether that frightens me more than the rest of it."

Casian folded the paper and stood up.

"Corin. Wednesday. I'll want the wagon, and I'll want Watt, and I'll want you and the girl and nobody else, and I'd like you not to ask me what I'm going to do."

"I'll ask you one thing."

"Ask it."

"Will anybody die?"

"Yes," said Casian Vale. "Four men, and I'll do it myself, and I'll do it at a distance and I'll do it quietly, and I'd like you to be at a place where you cannot see it."

Corin stood up too.

"Then I'll be there," he said, "and I'll watch, because if I'm going to follow a thing that knows, I'll know what it looks like when it does."

*He's a good man,* said Casian, on the way back up the lane, and did not say it out loud.

"He is the best of them," said Argius, "and you have just told him you are going to kill four men on Wednesday and he said he would watch. Do not console yourself that this is because he trusts you. It is because he has decided that being a man who sees is better than being a man who is told."

*It's the same thing.*

"It is not," said Argius. "And in a year you will understand why."
