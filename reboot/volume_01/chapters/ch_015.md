They left the Vale stables at dusk on Wednesday with a covered hay-wain and four fresh mounts, taking the high northern circuit over the cut. It added nine miles of treacherous shale, but it brought them down upon the sheep barn from the blind shoulder of the mountain.

Vesna walked thirty paces ahead of the team the entire night. She was the only one among them who had crossed those peat gullies in the dark, and Corin had instructed her to lead the column with an armsman's blunt economy.

It was bitter weather for an extraction. The moon had sunk behind cloudbanks by midnight, leaving only the hard, grey frost on the rock. They ascended through the four ancient stone gates along the high cut. Each time Vesna passed between the monolithic granite pillars, she reached out and touched the cold, weathered post with her bare knuckles, finding an odd comfort in their ancient, immovable weight. The stone was rough with centuries of lichen, smelling of frozen damp and old iron.

Behind her, Watt drove the wagon team. The crossroad innkeeper was fifty-one years old and had not ridden twenty miles in six seasons, but he sat on the rough ash bench in his heavy frieze coat without uttering a word of complaint. He held the lines with thick, calloused hands, keeping the horses at a slow, muffled walk, their iron shoes wrapped in oiled hemp to deaden the ring against the flint.

Beside the wagon walked Corin Ahl, his grey cloak pulled over his brigandine, an unstrung yew bow tucked beneath his arm. Casian sat in the bed among the dry straw, leaning his back against the sideboards, silent as an unlit candle.

Two hours before dawn, they crested the final limestone ridge overlooking the Veyr ravine.

The sheep barn sat three hundred feet below them on a peat shelf above the river: a squat, windowless structure of fieldstone and oak timbers, roofed with sod. Outside the heavy door, a small peat fire glowed dull red in the darkness.

Corin, Watt, and Vesna crouched behind an unmortared boundary wall four hundred paces up the slope. Casian stood beside them, wrapped in a dark woolen cloak, his hands resting quietly inside his sleeves.

Vesna peered through a chink in the dry stone, counting the shapes around the embers.

"Four at the fire," she whispered.

"There will be another watchman inside the timber," Corin murmured, checking his quiver.

"Four inside, and four at the hearth," Casian said quietly.

Corin glanced sideways at him in the gloom. "You can see through oak planks?"

"I know what is in the room," Casian answered, his voice devoid of boast or doubt. "Headman Ferrin is sitting against the northern wall. His nine-year-old granddaughter is asleep against his hip. The fifteen-year-old lad has a swollen ankle and cannot run."

He turned to Vesna, his gaze holding hers with steady intensity.

"When the door opens, their first impulse will be terror. They will attempt to bolt into the dark, and on this frozen shale a panicked child will break a limb. You will enter the barn, stand in the doorway where the light catches your face, and state your name three times. Then you will speak my mother's name: *Maren Vale sent us*. Once they hear that, you will walk them out in single file. You will move at the pace of the child. Not a stride faster."

"And the four sentries?" Vesna asked.

"Do not look at the sentries," Casian said. "Go down the eastern gully. Corin and Watt will cover the west approach."

Vesna looked at the boy of sixteen standing calmly on the frozen ridge. She did not ask how he intended to breach a locked door, and she did not ask what would become of the men at the fire. The silence of the mountain had swallowed all room for ordinary questions.

---

She descended the slope alone, moving down the eastern gully where the dry bracken muffled the sound of her boots.

The wind was blowing up from the river, carrying the smell of burning peat and roasting fat. The four sentries sat around the hearth. Two were murmuring in low voices, passing a horn cup between them. One sat propped against a water butt, dozing with his chin upon his chest. The fourth stood leaning against a billhook, staring fixedly down the valley toward the south—the direction of the Vale road.

He was watching the wrong road, exactly as Casian had predicted.

Vesna reached the perimeter of the clearing, pausing behind a clump of stunted birch forty paces from the fire. Her pulse hammered against her ribs, but her breath came slow and cold through her nostrils.

She gathered herself to sprint.

Then the world altered.

There was no sound. There was no sudden gust of wind, no flash of light, and no rattle of loose gravel.

The four men at the fire simply ceased to be upright.

The sentry with the billhook dropped straight down, his knees folding beneath him as if his sinews had been severed by an invisible shears. The two men on the log rolled sideways into the heather, their arms slack, their horn cup spilling cider into the cinders without a hiss. The dozing man against the water butt tilted backward, his head coming to rest against the stave.

The peat fire continued to burn, small yellow flames licking at the dry turf.

Vesna stood frozen in the birch scrub, the cold air burning in her throat. She looked from the four motionless bodies in the grass up toward the dark ridge four hundred paces away.

*He did not even draw a breath,* she thought, a shudder passing through her shoulders. *He stayed behind the wall, and the life went out of them.*

"Move," Corin's voice rasped from the gloom to her right.

She broke from the birch cover and ran to the door.

---

Inside the sheep barn, Headman Ferrin sat in the blackness, his bruised knuckles clasped around his knees.

For four days he had rehearsed this hour. He had calculated every detail: the raiders would come before dawn to move them; they would unbar the door with torches; and when the timber swung wide, he would shove his granddaughter behind the meal-chest and throw his fifty-year-old body into the lead guard's legs, screaming for the young men to scatter into the heather. It was a hopeless plan, but it was the only dignity a captive headman possessed.

He heard the heavy iron chain rattle against the oak.

Ferrin surged to his feet, his muscles screaming with stiffness, his jaw clenched to deliver his warning shout.

The heavy door swung inward.

Framed against the pale glow of the peat fire stood a young woman in an upland sheepskin wrap, her hands held open and empty before her chest.

"My name is Vesna Orrow," she said into the dark, her voice clear, ringing, and entirely steady. "I am from the high pastures above Fenwick, and I have come to take you home. My name is Vesna Orrow. I am from the high pastures above Fenwick, and I have come to take you home. Maren Vale sent us."

The name struck the cold barn like an iron bell.

From the straw in the corner, Ferrin's sister let out a ragged, choking sob. "Maren Vale... the Baroness sent a girl?"

"She sent an escort, and she expects you in her kitchen before noon," Vesna answered, stepping into the room and offering her hand. "Ferrin, take your granddaughter upon your hip. We move in single file. Nobody runs, and nobody looks behind."

Ferrin stepped over the threshold into the night air.

He looked toward the hearth. The peat was burning quietly. Four cloaked figures lay sprawled in the heather, unmoving, as if overcome by sudden sleep. There was no smell of blood, no splintered arrow-shafts, and no shout of triumph. The silence of the ravine was so complete that the loudest sound was the trembling sob of his nine-year-old granddaughter clinging to his neck.

*These were not soldiers who did this,* Ferrin thought, a cold awe seizing his heart. *Soldiers make noise. This is something else.*

"Walk," Vesna commanded softly, taking the lead. "Keep your eyes on my boots."

---

The ascent up the mountain shoulder was an ordeal of agonizing slowness.

The nine-year-old child grew stiff with cold; the fifteen-year-old lad stumbled on his bandaged ankle, supported by two gaunt field hands; and Ferrin, carrying his granddaughter, felt his fifty-year-old lungs burning with every upward step.

Halfway up the shale slope, a sudden flicker of yellow light appeared in the gorge below.

The door of the abandoned timber mill had creaked open, and a figure holding a grease lantern stepped out onto the gravel track, peering into the gloom.

"Hold," Vesna whispered down the line.

The eleven fugitives froze in the heather, pressing their bodies flat against the frozen shale. Vesna knelt beside Ferrin, her hand resting firmly on the small girl's shoulder.

"Is that an enemy?" the child whispered into Ferrin's beard.

"That is the miller," Vesna murmured in her ear. "His joints ache when the frost settles, and he has stepped out to look at the stars. If we remain like stone, he will see only rocks."

They counted two hundred slow heartbeats in the freezing dark.

The miller spat into the ditch, turned back inside, and pulled the timber door shut. The lantern light vanished.

"Rise," Vesna said. "Quietly."

---

While Vesna guided the line of captives over the crest toward Watt's waiting wagon, Corin Ahl and the innkeeper entered the sheep barn.

The four sentries had been carried inside and laid side by side along the stone wall, their rough wool mantles drawn over their faces. Watt stood over the fourth body, his breath pluming white in the lantern light, his hands trembling slightly as he adjusted the hem of the cloak.

"I will ask this once," Watt said, his voice hushed and solemn.

Corin leaned against the timber post, his arms folded. "Ask."

"Was there a way to accomplish this that did not require four corpses?"

Corin took a slow breath, looking down at the shrouded figures.

"Not tonight," Corin answered honestly. "In two years, perhaps, when we have forty horsemen and three waystations. Tonight we had four hours of darkness, an iron-bound door, and eleven villagers who could not run ten paces without falling. Had Casian not extinguished these four, two of them would have slipped into the ravine and alerted the garrison before we reached the ridge. We would have died on the shale."

Watt nodded slowly, wiping his damp brow with a rough sleeve.

"That is a grim answer," the innkeeper murmured. "And it is the only one I can live with."

---

They reached the gates of Fenwick manor as the eastern horizon turned the color of bruised slate.

Baroness Calla stood in the open archway, wrapped in her heavy wolfskin mantle, two grooms holding torches behind her. She did not ask for names, and she did not ask who had unlocked the sheep barn. She directed the four wagons into the inner courtyard, where servants were already carrying cauldrons of hot porridge and stacks of wool blankets into the heated bakehouse.

Ferrin carried his sleeping granddaughter through the door, his eyes wide with hollow, exhausted gratitude.

Calla watched the last crofter pass into the warmth, then turned her keen gaze upon Corin Ahl.

"They will have hot pottage, dry blankets, and sound straw," the Baroness said, her voice brisk and clear in the freezing dawn. "Tell Baron Edric his subjects are safe beneath Fenwick slate, and that I expect no repayment until the spring clip. But tell him also that I expect him to look me in the eye the next time our palfreys meet along the boundary ditch."

"I will deliver the message, my lady," Corin answered, bowing his head.

Vesna remained outside in the yard, leaning against the cold masonry of the gatepost. Her hands were shaking with such violent fatigue that she had to tuck them into her pockets.

Corin walked up beside her, staring at the grey moor.

"Four men," she whispered.

"Aye," Corin said. "Four men."

"He never moved from the ridge."

"No."

She turned her face toward him, her dark eyes hollow in the dawn. "If Hask had sent twenty men to Lowmere instead of twenty-two... if my brothers had been in that barn... would he have done the same?"

Corin Ahl looked out over the frost-whitened fields of Fenwick.

"He would have done the exact same thing," Corin said, his voice heavy with eighteen years of armsman's certainty. "He did not save those eleven because he loves them, Vesna. He saved them because they belonged to his father's border, and because an enemy took them without his consent. You had better accept that truth now, or you will find yourself weeping in a ditch before the spring."

Vesna looked at her boots, silent, and did not weep.

---

On the road back to Ashgrove, Vesna rode on the wagon bench beside Watt. The old innkeeper drove in silence for three miles, his gaze fixed on the horses' ears.

"You've been quiet since the ridge, Master Watt," Vesna said.

Watt spat over the buckboard into the dry ditch. "A man who spends thirty years pouring ale for carters hears a great deal of boasting about war. Young squires come into the taproom with iron on their heels, talking about glory and banners." He shook his head slowly. "Tonight there wasn't a banner in sight. There was a boy who sat in the straw, four men who fell over like empty sacks, and eleven crofters who walked out of a locked door without a blow struck. That is not war, girl. That is butchery done with clean linen."

"Would you rather the eleven stayed in the barn?" she asked.

"No," Watt said, turning his weathered face toward her. "I'd rather they were eating porridge at Fenwick, exactly as they are. But I'll not fool myself into thinking the boy who opened that door is an ordinary lad. He's a reckoning, Vesna. And when you hitch your wagon to a reckoning, you'd best be sure of your footing."

---

On Friday afternoon, Corin stood before the oak desk in Baron Edric's study at Vale.

"All eleven are quartered at Fenwick," Corin reported formally. "Calla has provided meal, fuel, and clean straw. The child is unhurt, and the reeve's foot is bound."

Edric sat with his hands propped against his forehead, staring at the unrolled border map.

"And the four watchmen at the barn?" the baron asked.

"Dead, my lord."

"How?"

Corin turned his straw hat over in his fingers. "I will not answer that, my lord."

The silence in the study grew thick and heavy.

"Corin," Edric said, his voice dropping into quiet authority. "You have served this house for eighteen years. You have never withheld a report from me."

"I am withholding this," Corin said stubbornly. "Because if I explain how those men died, you will possess knowledge you can neither acknowledge in court nor punish in your hall. You have enough burdens, my lord."

Edric looked up slowly, his grey eyes piercing and filled with ancient sorrow.

"It was Casian," Edric said.

It was not a question.

"My lord—"

"It was Casian," the baron repeated, his voice barely above a whisper. "There were four dead sentries. There is a miller's widow who visits my house at midnight. There is an upland girl who runs forty miles across the moor. And there is a locked chest in your barracks that contains a survey of every road in this province."

The old lord set his quill aside, his hands trembling slightly.

"I have suspected since August. I have said nothing, because every time I prepare to confront him, my fourth son saves eleven of my people from slaughter. If I demand the truth, I must act as his magistrate, and acting as his magistrate means forbidding his work. And if I forbid his work, the next eleven subjects who are dragged into the hills will die in chains."

Edric looked up, meeting his armsman's gaze.

"Was it clean, Corin?"

"Four men, my lord. They died in an instant, without a cry."

Edric closed his eyes, exhaling a breath that sounded like dry leaves. "God forgive me. That is the answer I prayed to hear."

---

The next morning, Vesna walked into the Vale courtyard to confirm the disposition of the Lowmere folk.

Maren Vale was standing by the dairy door, scattering barley to a flock of speckled hens. When she saw the upland girl approach, she set the wooden bowl upon the bench and wiped her hands on her linen apron.

"Ferrin's granddaughter," Maren said without preface. "Is she warm?"

"She is warm, my lady," Vesna replied. "She slept twelve hours under wool."

"Did she eat?"

"Two bowls of porridge and sweet milk."

"And the headman?"

"He sits by the bakehouse fire with his shoes off, watching the door."

Maren nodded once, her level gaze examining the dark hollows under Vesna's eyes.

"My husband will draft three letters to Harven celebrating the delivery," Maren said, her voice dry and steady. "Gareth will sulk in the armory because he did not strike the blow himself. I have only one question for you, Vesna, and then you will go into my buttery and take half a ham and a loaf of white bread."

"Ask it, my lady."

Maren stepped closer, her voice dropping so low the stable boys could not hear. "Was it quick for the men on the door?"

Vesna met the mother's eyes, remembering the four bodies collapsing like cut grass around the quiet embers.

"There was no sound, my lady," Vesna whispered. "Not a single groan."

Maren Vale stood motionless on the gravel for several heartbeats, her hand resting against her apron.

"Then that is the mercy I will remember," Maren said, and opened the buttery door.
