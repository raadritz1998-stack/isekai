They left Vale at dusk on the Wednesday with a wagon and four horses and came at the sheep barn from the north, which was nine miles further, and took most of the night.

Vesna walked in front the whole way, because she was the only one who had ever been across that country at night, and because Corin had said she would and had not been joking.

It was a bad night for it. There was no moon and no wind and the ground was iron, and they went up the cut through the four gateways that were not gateways, and each time she went through one she put her hand on the post, which she did not mention and would not have been able to explain.

The man called Watt was fifty-one and had not been on a horse in six years and did not complain once.

"Swift, fetch those two from the upland," Corin had said. "The brothers. They're not to know what for and they're not to be told anything at all and you'll pay them and send them home to their mother before it's light."

"That's done oddly."

"That's done the way it's done," said Corin. "There's four men in a barn and I'd like them to be looking at something that isn't the road from Vale."

---

The sheep barn was on the shoulder above the ford, with a dry floor and a stone wall and a door that had a bar on it and four men in front of it and a fire.

They came off the hill at the second hour and lay behind a wall four hundred paces off, and Vesna looked at the fire and the men and counted and counted again and it was still four.

"There'll be a fifth inside," said Corin.

"There's four inside."

"How do you know?"

Vesna did not answer, because she had known since she was nine and had never had to say it out loud, and because the answer was that a fire with four men around it is a fire with four men around it and a fire with five has one more shape in it.

"Four inside and four at the fire," said Casian Vale.

He had come with them, which nobody had expected and nobody had argued about.

Vesna looked at him.

"You're not coming down."

"No," said Casian. "I'm going to stay here and I'd like you to do exactly what I say and not a thing more, and I'd like you not to ask me why."

"All right."

"Ferrin is the headman and he's in the barn and his granddaughter is with him. There's a girl of nine. The boy of fifteen has a bad foot and cannot run." He said it plainly, like a man reading a list. "When the door opens, they'll be frightened and they'll want to go fast, and they cannot go fast, and the ones who can go fast are the four men who'll be sitting in the dark of the barn four hours after they've been taken. So you'll go in and you'll say who you are and you'll say it three times, and then you'll say my mother's name, and then you'll tell them to be quiet and you'll walk them out at the pace of the girl."

"How do you know what's in there?"

"Because I looked," said Casian. "That's all. I looked."

Vesna Orrow looked at him in the dark and understood, in a way she could not have put into a sentence and would spend a long time not being able to, that she was not going to ask him how.

---

They went down at the third hour.

Corin and Watt went along the wall to the west, because a thing has to be done properly, and Vesna went down the slope to the east with her hands empty, because she was the one who was not going to be a threat.

Four men at a fire. Two of them were talking and one was asleep sitting up and one was staring at the dark in the direction of the road from Vale.

That one was looking the wrong way. Vesna had been told that he would be, and she had not believed it, and there he was at the fire staring south at a road eleven miles off while she came down the slope on his blind side with her hands in front of her.

She was forty paces away when it happened.

There was no noise. That was the thing she would tell Watt afterwards and would tell badly, because there was no good way to tell it: there was no noise and no wind and no movement and the four men at the fire simply were not there any more, and the fire went on burning, and the boy sitting nearest it fell over slowly into the grass.

She stopped dead on the last slope with her heart going, looking at an empty fire and a shape in the grass, and nothing in the world had made a sound at all.

*He didn't stand up,* she thought. *He was behind a wall four hundred paces off and he didn't stand up.*

"Go," said Corin's voice, very quietly, from forty paces west.

---

The bar went back and the door stood open and Vesna Orrow stood in it with the fire behind her and her hands where everyone could see them.Ferrin heard the door and thought: they've come to move us.

That was the thing he had been dreading for four days. He had worked out the shape of it in his own head the way a man does in a barn with a lock on it, and the shape was that they would be moved at night because it was easier to move people at night, and that being moved would be worse than being kept, and that the moment of being moved would be the moment to do something — and that he was fifty, and there were three other men with him, and two of them were old.

So when the door opened he was already on his feet with his hands out, and had his mouth open to say a thing he had rehearsed.

And then a girl came in, out of breath, with her hands in front of her, and said her name three times.

He did not take it in properly, at the time. What he took in properly was the light behind her — a fire burning undisturbed on the shoulder with nobody at it, and four shapes in the grass, and no sound at all, and the whole of it so quiet that the loudest thing in the world was his own breathing.

*That's not men,* he thought. *That's not soldiers. A soldier makes a noise and it's a noise that means well.*

"Ferrin," said the girl, "you're to go second and you'll take your granddaughter and no one is to be hurried."

He had not told her the girl was his granddaughter. There was nothing on the girl that said whose she was.

He was aware of that in the moment and was aware of it for years afterwards, and it was the reason he told the story badly.

---

The bar was off the barn door and the door opened inward and eleven people were in there in the dark.---

The bar was off the barn door and the door opened inward and eleven people were in there in the dark.

They were frightened and they had been frightened for four days and there was a small one at the front of it.

Vesna went in with her hands out.

"My name's Vesna Orrow and I'm from the uplands above Fenwick and I've come to take you home, and I'll say it three times. My name's Vesna Orrow and I'm from the uplands above Fenwick and I've come to take you home. Vesna Orrow, I'm from the uplands, I've come to take you home. Maren Vale sent us."

There was a silence that had a shape to it.

"Maren," said a voice, which belonged to an old woman who had been at the back with her arms round a child. "Maren Vale sends a girl in the middle of the night."

"She'll want the whole story in person and she'll want it with bread," said Vesna. "Walk at the pace of the girl. Ferrin, you're to go second and you'll take your granddaughter and no one is to be hurried, and if a man tells you to run, don't, and if you hear a noise behind us, don't look at it."

"And if there's anybody in the way?"

"There isn't," said Vesna Orrow, and found that she believed it.

---

There was a bad quarter of an hour on the hill.

They were a mile up the slope and the girl of nine had gone slow and cold and frightened, and the boy with the bad foot was struggling, and one of the four women had begun to cry quite quietly, and Ferrin had his granddaughter on his hip and was fifty and had not carried anything up a hill for twenty years.

And somewhere below them, in the valley, a man with a lantern came out of the mill by the ford and stood in the road and looked up.

Everyone stopped.

Vesna Orrow came back down the line at a run, without a sound, and put her hand flat on the head of the crying woman and said, "Nobody move and nobody says anything and it's one man with a lantern going to his own house."

They waited the count of two hundred.

The lantern went back inside.

Ferrin's granddaughter said, in the enormous voice of a nine-year-old in a whisper, "Is that a bad man?"

"That's a miller," said Vesna.

"Is the miller going to tell?"

"The miller's been asleep since the second hour," said Vesna, "and if he's up now it's because his knee hurts and he'll think about that and not us. Now walk at the pace of the girl and don't look back down the hill again."

They walked.

Ferrin thought afterwards that he had spent four days in a barn deciding what he would do when he was moved, and had rehearsed it, and had been ready, and had come out of that door into a world where the men were already dead and nobody had made a sound and he had not been needed at all.

He was honest enough with himself to know that this was the most frightening thing that had ever happened to him, and that he was grateful for it, and that he had not yet worked out how those two things could both be true.

---

---

The four men at the fire were dealt with before Vesna reached them, and it fell to Watt, who was fifty-one and had never in his life done anything but keep an inn, to do the part nobody had planned for, which was the part where four men have to be left somewhere.

Corin did it with him. They carried them into the barn and laid them along the far wall, in a row, with their cloaks over their faces, because it seemed like the thing to do and neither of them could have said why it seemed like that.

"There's a thing I'll want explaining," said Watt, laying the last one down.

"Ask it in a year."

"No," said Watt. "I'll ask it now, because I've a pub and a dog and a bed on a road and I've been paid nine marks a quarter to keep a tally of carts, and there's four dead men in a barn that I helped carry." He straightened up. "I'm not going to say a word to anybody about what happened here. I'd like to say that before I ask, so you know I'm not buying an answer."

"Ask it."

"Was there a way to do this that didn't have four men in it?"

Corin Ahl considered the question properly, which took a while.

"Not tonight," he said. "In a year, maybe. There's a way of doing a thing that's better than this and it needs more men than we've got and more time than we had and a way of getting eleven people off a hill without carrying them down it. We hadn't got any of that." He looked at the row. "So tonight it was this, and I'll not pretend it was anything else, and if a better way turns up I'll take it and I'll not shed a tear for these four being spared it."

Watt nodded slowly.

"That's a better answer than the one I expected," he said, "and I'll hold you to the second half of it."

They walked for six hours.They walked for six hours.

Eleven people and a girl of nine and a boy of fifteen with a bad foot, moving at the pace of a nine-year-old across nine miles of hill in the dark, with Corin in front and Watt at the back and Vesna walking the outside of the whole line the way a dog walks the outside of a flock.

They came down into Fenwick at first light.They came down into Fenwick at first light.

The Baroness Calla was at the gate herself, because Vesna had asked for it to be arranged and had asked through Corin and had been told it would be done and had expected to be lied to and had not been.

There was bread and there was a fire and there were eleven people in a barn that happened to be Fenwick's and was warm and had a door that opened. The girl of nine was asleep in a quarter of an hour with a blanket over her boots.

Vesna was not asleep. She was standing in the yard at the wall with her hands shaking so badly that she had to put them behind her, and Corin came and stood next to her and did not look at her.

"Four men," she said.

"Four men."

"And none of them made a sound."

"No."

"She could have been nine in Lowmere and taken for the same reason," said Vesna. "And then somebody in a garden behind a wall would have—"

She stopped.

"No," said Corin.

"Would it have been the same?"

"Yes," said Corin Ahl. "And it was the same. And you'd better get used to the shape of that before you're a year older, because I've had thirty-five years of it and it never once stopped being a thing you can see clear and never gets smaller."

Corin reported it to the baron on the Friday, in the study, standing, with the door shut.

"Eleven at Fenwick. The girl's warm, the boy's foot is bound, and the Baroness has said they can stay as long as they need and has not once said the word 'favour' in my hearing."

Edric had his hands flat on the desk.

"How."

"I'm not going to tell you, my lord."

The silence went on for a while.

"Corin."

"I've been in this yard thirty-five years and I've never once refused you a thing and I'm refusing you this." Corin turned his hat over. "There's four men dead above that ford and nobody heard a thing, and if I tell you how it happened you'll be a man who knows a thing he cannot do anything with, and you've enough of those."

Edric looked at him for a long moment.

"It was my son," he said.

It was not a question.

"My lord—"

"It was my son, and there were four men, and there is a miller's widow who has been in this house twice this month at night, and there is a girl off the uplands who walks to Harrowmere and back in the dark, and there is a chest in my guardroom with a map in it that I have been told is a map of the county." He did not raise his voice at all. "I have known since August. I have decided nothing, because every time I decide to ask him he does something that keeps eleven people alive."

Corin stood absolutely still.

"Then ask him."

"And then what?" said Edric. "He is sixteen. If he tells me the truth, I have to be his father about it, and being his father about it means stopping him, and stopping him means that the next eleven people in a barn stay in the barn." He picked up his pen and put it down again. "So I will not ask him, and I will go on writing letters to Harven, and I would like you to say nothing of this to anybody, including my wife, who has already worked it out and is being kinder to me than I deserve."

He looked up.

"How bad was it?"

"Four men, my lord. Quick."

"God help me," said Edric Vale, "that's the answer I wanted."

---

She asked Watt, on the road back, because Watt was fifty-one and had a tally and a dog and did not frighten her.

"Do you know how they did it?"

"I do," said Watt.

"You're not going to tell me."

"No," said Watt. "Because I was told to keep a tally of carts, and I was never told that, and it's better for me that there's a thing in this county I don't know the size of."

Vesna rode on for a while.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer of a man who's fifty-one," said Watt, "and if you're lucky you'll be fifty-one yourself one day and you'll understand it."

---

She went up to Vale the next morning to say that the eleven were safe and where they were, and Maren Vale came out into the yard to hear it, and Vesna told it to her in the order it had happened.

Maren listened to all of it. Then she asked four questions and they were all the same question:

"Was the girl warm?"

"She was warm."

"Did she eat?"

"She ate twice."

"Was there a woman with her the whole way?"

"There was an old woman and she never let go of her."

"And the boy with the bad foot?"

"Rode the last three miles and complained about it."

Maren Vale nodded once.

"My family will talk about this for years," she said. "My husband will write a letter about it and never once want to know what actually happened. My second son will be furious that he wasn't there." She put her hand on the gatepost. "I want to know one thing from you and then you're to go into my kitchen and eat, and I'll not have it said you were sent home without."

"Ask it."

"Was it quick for the men on the door?"

Vesna Orrow looked at Maren Vale in her own yard in the morning.

"There wasn't a noise," she said. "There wasn't one sound."

His mother stood there a moment.

"Then that's what I'll hold onto," said Maren Vale.
