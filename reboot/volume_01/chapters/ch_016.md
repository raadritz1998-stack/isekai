Maeve Corran had kept the mill's ledger for eleven years, and before this summer nobody had ever asked her what the numbers meant.

The book was not glamorous. That was the truth she could never make anyone outside the trade grasp. It was four hundred pages of parchment recording twenty seasons of grain: bushels of rye weighed at the hopper, sacks of coarse meal hauled out by dray, water-rents paid on Michaelmas, timber repaired, and iron spindles replaced. It smelled of damp flour-dust, tallow grease, and old calfskin. The margins were cramped with her late husband's crude tally-scratches—notches carved with a dull clasp-knife beside the bushel columns to verify the count when his head grew thick with figures. It was tedious with the inescapable dullness of honest labor, and its very dullness was its integrity.

Because a dishonest merchant's ledger was never dull; a dishonest ledger was immaculate.

Maeve had noticed that unnatural precision in the papers brought down from Thornwall two years before she ever understood who held the pen. She had spent nineteen years married to a miller who could not multiply seven by eight without using dry peas, and five years keeping the mill solvent after a winter fever took him. Her husband, Peter, had been a strong, good-humored man who could lift a two-hundredweight sack of barley onto a wagon without catching his breath, but when a clerk presented an account roll, Peter would squint at the ink with the bewildered dread of an illiterate shepherd handed a Latin missal. He knew the bite of cold water against the paddle blades and the exact hum of a millstone when the grist was running dry, yet a column of pence terrified him into silence.

There is an unmistakable artificiality that appears in an account roll when an accountant has decided what the total must show before he enters the individual lines.

Rennick's papers had been clean. The rolls carried by eleven shops along Thornwall's market street were clean. In the final week of October, while the parish celebrated the delivery of the Lowmere captives, Maeve locked her kitchen shutters, trimmed her brass oil lamp, and compared six years of mill accounts against every carrier's docket in her chest.

It took three long nights.

On the first night she confirmed the regular trade: Vale rye traveled east through Thornwall to the coast, and the carters took their standard toll of three groats on the barrel. Every grain sack was stamped with the three-sheaf mark of the Vale water-mills, tallied as it passed the weigh-house, and checked off by the draymen before the mules turned into the eastern mire. The margins were thin, as they had always been, but every cooper, drayman, and boatman was accounted for.

On the second night she identified an anomaly: over the past two years, the wholesale price of flour in Thornwall had risen eleven marks on the hundredweight, while the carters' carriage wages had not increased by a single penny. The boatmen on the Harrowmere cut were hauling fifty tons more meal than they had in her husband's day, yet they were drinking thin cider and mending their own ropes. A substantial margin was disappearing into an unlisted coffer somewhere between the weigh-scale and the town gate.

On the third night she uncovered the tribute.

---

On Saturday night she walked out to the wheel-pit behind the mill, resting her palm against the great oak axle-tree. The stones were disengaged, the sluice gate lowered, the flume dripping with slow, rhythmic ticks into the dark pool below.

*You would have signed whatever document Rennick slid across the table,* she thought, addressing the ghost of her husband. *You were a sound judge of grain and a hopeless reader of law. You signed his debt-bond believing you were protecting your roof, and I spent four years cursing you for dying before I realized you had been swindled.*

On Sunday morning she took the ledger under her woolen shawl and walked up the drovers' track to Vale manor without sending a runner ahead.

The gathering in the old dairy had acquired a third stool, an extra tin candlestick, and a stone flagon of cider that Corin Ahl had tucked behind an empty butter churn. Casian, Corin, Ansel Trask, and Vesna Orrow were already seated around the timber table when the oak latch lifted.

"I have something to show you," Maeve said, setting the heavy, leather-bound volume upon the wood with a firm thud. "It is not a rumor, and it is not an upland tale. It is three sets of figures. Listen until I finish, or I will lose the thread of the arithmetic."

Corin closed his knife; Vesna leaned forward, her elbows propped on her knees; Ansel adjusted his velvet cushion.

"First," Maeve said, opening to a page scored with red ink. "There are two hundred and forty marks of silver moving out of this county every quarter under a heading marked *sundries*. It leaves Thornwall under the seal of their town clerk, and it has been moving for eight consecutive quarters. And this mill has been paying nine marks of that total every three months."

Casian looked up sharply. "You were paying a surcharge?"

"My husband was paying it," she said, her voice taut with lingering grief. "He was told it was an emergency road toll levied by the marcher bailiffs to maintain the stone bridges, to be rebated after three years. He never read the parchment. Nine other millers and carriers along this road signed the same agreement, and every one of us has contributed thirty-six marks a year to an anonymous purse."

The chamber grew cold and silent.

"Second," Maeve continued, tracing a column with her thumbnail. "The money arrives at Thornwall, and then it is reconsigned north. Not south to the ducal court at Harven, and not west toward the sea. It travels straight up to the high cut. There is nothing north of the cut except sheep pastures and rocks, and rocks do not require two hundred and forty marks of silver every quarter."

"It crosses into the Veyr valley," Ansel Trask murmured, nodding slowly. "I know the track. It goes by pack-pony above the alder swamps."

"It crosses the cut, and then the records cease," Maeve said. "Because border reivers do not maintain clerks."

She turned the leaf, smoothing the parchment flat.

"Third. This is the detail that should frighten everyone in this room. Forty-one men in this county have been paying that surcharge. I have nine by name because they are honest millers who showed me their tallies. The other thirty-two I traced from the carriers' waybills, because an artificial levy always leaves a visible imbalance in the ledger. And thirty of those thirty-two have one specific trait in common."

She paused, looking directly at Casian Vale.

"Not their guild. Not their kinship. Their grievances against House Vale."

She drew a folded sheet from her sleeve, reading the names aloud.

"Nine marks a quarter from forty-one men for two years amounts to seven hundred and thirty-eight marks of coin. That sum will not purchase an army of horsemen, and it did not build Hask's stone hall. What it purchases is the margin of survival. It is the reserve that allows a warlord with eighty horses to feed his mounts through the ice without selling his lances. It is why Hask could purchase grain in Harrowmere at three times the fair rate and pay in hard silver. It is a retainer."

"An administrative wage," Ansel whispered. "A standing bribe."

"Osric is not paying Hask to conquer the valley," Maeve said, her voice dropping like lead. "He is paying Hask to remain in the hills like a loaded crossbow pointed at your father's throat."

She pointed to the ledger.

"One of these men was fined fifty shillings by Baron Edric in '09 for neglecting a drainage ditch. One was evicted from a tenant holding during the great frost. Another had a brother banished to the hills six years ago for sheep-stealing. There are thirty householders between here and Harrowmere who harbor bitter grudges against your family, and for two years they have been financing the very warlord who burns your holdings."

The dairy was completely still. The flame in the brass lantern flickered against the damp limestone walls.

"Ansel," Casian said quietly.

"Do not look at me, lad," Ansel Trask replied, spreading his thin, age-spotted hands. "I had eleven names of corrupt factors, and I thought I held the board. This woman found an armed payroll hidden inside a baker's book in three nights. I am sixty years old, and I am profoundly humbled."

"How long has the arrangement run?" Casian asked Maeve.

"Two years without interruption," Maeve said. "It is not a road toll. It is an organized conspiracy."

---

The argument that followed lasted an hour.

Ansel argued that the thirty disgruntled tenants were harmless. Men with ancient grudges were merely bitter gossips who grumbled over ale; confronting them would only trigger defensive lies and alert Thornwall that their ledger had been deciphered.

"A grudge is not a blade," Ansel insisted, tapping the table with a bony finger. "A tenant who grumbles about a fifty-shilling fine in '09 will sit by his fire and nurse his resentment for twenty winters, but he will not mount a horse and charge a palisade. Leave them alone. If you confront them, Osric burns the papers before nightfall."

Vesna disagreed with fierce upland impatience.

"Ansel has spent forty years counting silver behind a shop counter," Vesna said, her dark eyes flashing. "On the moor, when thirty men contribute bread to an outlaw, they are not gossips. They are his lookouts. They leave gates unlatched and turn their sheep into the lower meadow so raiders have fresh mutton on the drove. If you do not break their fingers now, they will open your gates from the inside."

Casian raised his hand, and the room fell silent.

"Neither course is correct," Casian said, rising from the bench. "The thirty men with grudges are not an attacking force, and they are not a danger to our gate. They are evidence. For two years, forty-one independent ledger entries have recorded silver flowing from Thornwall into the Veyr hills. That is not an upland rumor about a river mist. That is written documentation."

He turned to Maeve. "Can we obtain a signed receipt?"

"From whom?" she asked.

"From one of the contributors who is frightened rather than angry," Casian said. "An angry man will run to Thornwall to report the inquiry. A frightened man will surrender his papers to ensure he is not hanged when the inquiry begins."

Maeve closed her account book. "Widow Rill's son, Harn. He operates a freight barge on the Harrowmere cut. He was turned off a Vale sheep-holding seven years ago, but he keeps every scrap of parchment his mother hands him. I will speak to him as a miller checking transit fees."

---

On Thursday afternoon, Maeve traveled four miles down the turnpike to the hamlet of Bradeley, where Harn Rill kept his barge moored among the willow thickets.

The cut was shrouded in cold autumn mist, the brown water lapping against the timber revetments. Along the towpath, tired carters were unharnessing heavy teams, their breath steaming in the damp air. Maeve walked past the freight sheds with her shawl drawn close, making certain she greeted two different flour factors on the wharf, establishing a visible reason for her journey.

She entered the low-ceilinged kitchen of Widow Rill's cottage. The old widow sat by the peat hearth, her seventy-year-old face weathered like dried oak, spinning grey wool with steady, deliberate turns of her spindle.

Harn, a stout man of thirty-one with sleeves rolled above muscular forearms, poured rough cider into two pottery mugs.

Maeve discussed freight rates for half an hour, examining the price of carrying grain sacks to the southern wharf. When the conversation drifted to road tolls, she set her mug upon the scrubbed pine table.

"Twice a quarter, Harn, an extra surcharge passes through your dockets," Maeve said quietly.

Harn paused with his hands resting on his knees. "That has nothing to do with mill flour, Mistress Corran."

"It has a great deal to do with me," Maeve said, leaning forward. "I have paid nine marks every three months for two years on that same surcharge. My husband signed the recognisance before he took sick, and neither of us was told who received the silver. I am forty years old, Harn. I have no children to inherit my mill, and I intend to understand what I have been purchasing before the bailiffs arrive."

The room grew very quiet. The only sound was the whir of the widow's spindle.

Old Widow Rill stopped her wheel, rose from her stool, and walked to a heavy iron-banded chest beneath the window.

"Show her the receipt, Harn," the old woman said.

"Mother—"

"Show her," the widow commanded, her voice raspy and stern. "You kept those vouchers because I instructed you to keep them. A poor man who cannot produce his receipts is the first man the bailiffs drag to the gallows when trouble starts. There is a storm gathering along this border. I have lived sixty years on this marsh, and I can hear the timber cracking. When the reckoning comes, I want proof of who took our bread."

Harn sighed, retrieved a small bundle of parchment tied with tarred twine from the chest, and untied the knot.

He extracted a soiled, yellowed slip of vellum and laid it before Maeve.

"I took no bribe for this," Harn said quietly. "And when the reckoning arrives, I want it remembered that I surrendered the document without a blow."

"It will be remembered," Maeve promised.

She picked up the vellum and stepped into the lane.

The text was written in a clerk's neat, upright hand. It acknowledged the receipt of nine marks from Harn Rill in settlement of the eastern transit surcharge, dated the previous June.

At the bottom of the slip, written in dark, faded ink, was the signature of Osric's steward, Weyl.

Beneath it, confirming the receipt of the coin at the Thornwall market cross, was the counter-signature of Master Rennick.

---

Maeve carried the parchment to Vale manor that evening, laying it upon the table before Casian in the old dairy.

Casian held the vellum close to the lantern, examining the signatures with cold, thorough concentration.

"This is the cornerstone," Casian said, looking up.

"It is a scrap of dirty sheepskin," Maeve answered, taking the opposite stool.

"It is a signed confession linking the Baron of Thornwall's household directly to the levy that feeds Hask's spears," Casian said, his voice quiet with absolute certainty. "I cannot present a river causeway to Duke Varyn's commissioners. I cannot ask a royal court to believe that four sentries fell dead because a boy wished it from a ridge. But a receipt signed by Osric's steward and countersigned by his factor is an unanswerable indictment. It transforms a border feud into high treason."

Maeve looked at him across the table, her hands resting flat upon her apron.

"Then hear me plainly, Master Casian," Maeve said. "I am no longer merely a miller who reviews numbers for your amusement. I have walked into a carter's cottage and carried away evidence that could see a baron stripped of his title. If Osric learns I hold this paper, his riders will burn my mill to the foundations and drown me in my own race."

"I know," Casian said.

"Then tell me the number," she demanded.

"The number?"

"You keep a ledger of souls in your head," Maeve said, her voice hard as millstone. "You have counted every man, horse, and debt in this parish since harvest. Tell me how many lives depend upon your success."

Casian was silent for several long moments. The candle sputtered, casting a pale circle over the map.

"Eleven villagers at Fenwick," Casian said. "Ansel Trask in Harrowmere. Corin Ahl. Vesna on the moor. Watt at the crossroad. Young Kael on the ditch. Seven tenant families on the eastern strip whose mortgages I hold. And a miller's widow who found the proof."

"Twenty-four," Maeve said softly.

"Twenty-four," Casian agreed. "And I cannot promise that none of you will face danger. I can move quickly, Maeve, but I cannot be on every road at the same instant. That is the limit of my reach."

Maeve Corran nodded slowly, rising from the table and pulling her dark woolen shawl tight around her shoulders.

"That was an honest answer," she said, her hand resting on the iron latch of the door. "And I can work with an honest master. But keep this truth in your thoughts through the winter: there are thirty men in this valley who paid that levy because they hate your family's name. When you unseat Baron Osric, those thirty grievances will not disappear. They will still be thirty men on your road, and you will have to decide what to do with their anger."

She pulled the door open and stepped out into the November frost, leaving the fourth son of Vale sitting alone with his lamp and his parchment.

As she walked the dark cart-track back toward the mill, she pulled her woolen shawl tight against the biting wind. The valley was quiet around her, the stars obscured by grey cloud. For thirty years she had ground grain, paid taxes, and buried her dead in an ordinary county; now she was an accomplice to a boy who intended to dismantle a barony without drawing a sword.

*God keep him,* Maeve thought, looking back once at the faint yellow slit of candlelight in the dairy wall. *And God keep the rest of us if his arithmetic fails.*
