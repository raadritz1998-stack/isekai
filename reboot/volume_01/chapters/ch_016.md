Maeve Corran had kept the mill's book for eleven years and had never once been asked what was in it, and had reached an age at which she had decided this said more about the asking than the book.

The book was not interesting. That was the thing she could never make anyone understand. It was four hundred pages of a life — a bushel in, a bushel out, a bad quarter, a good one, a cow sold, a roof — and it was dull in the way that all true records are dull, and the dullness was the whole of the argument.

Because a merchant's paper was not dull. A merchant's paper was neat.

That was the first thing she had ever noticed about the Thornwall men, two years before she had a name for any of it, and she had noticed it because she had spent nineteen years married to a man who could not keep a figure and four years keeping them herself, and there is a particular kind of neatness that only appears when a man has planned what the book is going to say before he writes it.

Rennick's paper was neat. Eleven shops on one street in Thornwall were neat. And in the last week of October she sat down with a lamp and did the thing she had wanted to do for a year, which was to lay the mill's own accounts for six years against everything she knew about where the grain went.

It took three nights.

On the first night she established what she already knew: that Vale's grain went out east, through Thornwall, and had done since before she was married, and that the price the carrier took was fair and the price the merchants paid was fair.

On the second night she established something that was not interesting yet: that in the last two years the grain price at Thornwall had gone up eleven marks and the price the carrier was paid had not gone up at all, and that the difference had therefore gone somewhere in the middle where there was nobody to ask.

On the third night she found the third direction.

---

She had gone to the mill wheel on the Saturday night and put her hand on the shaft, which was a thing she had not done since her husband died, and had stood there for a while in the dark with the stones stopped and the flume ticking.

*You'd have signed any paper a man put in front of you,* she thought. *You were a good miller and a poor reader and you'd have signed this and thought you were being careful, and I've spent four years being angry at you for dying and not once been angry at you for that.*

She went up to Vale on the Sunday morning with the book under her arm and did not send a boy.She went up to Vale on the Sunday morning with the book under her arm and did not send a boy.

There were five of them in the old dairy now, and sometimes six, and the room had acquired a stool and a second lamp and a stone bottle that Corin believed nobody knew about.

"I've found a thing," she said, before anyone had finished sitting down, "and it's not a story. It's three numbers. Sit still and let me say them or I'll lose my thread."

Nobody moved.

"One. There's two hundred and forty marks a quarter going out of this county on the eastern road that isn't for grain. I know it isn't for grain because grain is measured and this isn't. It goes out of Thornwall on a paper that says 'sundries' and it's been doing it for two years and the mill is paying a share of it."

"Paying?" said Casian.

"Paying. Nine marks a quarter, out of the mill, since the spring before last." Maeve turned the book round. "That's my husband's doing and he's dead and I'll not have him blamed for it, because he wasn't told what it was for. A man came and said there was a levy on the eastern carriers and it would be refunded and here's the paper, and my husband didn't read the paper, and neither did nine other men in this county, and every one of us has been paying nine marks a quarter to something called sundries for two years."

The room was very quiet.

"Two," said Maeve. "The sundries money goes to Thornwall and then it goes *north*." She put a finger on the page. "Not south to the capital, not east into the hills. North, to the cut, and then over it, and there's only one thing north of the cut on that side of the county and it's the upland grazing, and the upland grazing is worth nine marks a quarter about as much as the moon is."

"So it crosses the cut," said Ansel.

"It crosses the cut, and then nobody writes it down, because there's no town on the other side to write it in."

"Three," said Maeve, and closed the book. "Three is the one that matters and it's the one I'd like permission to be frightened of.

"There's forty-one men in this county paying that levy. I have nine of them by name because they're millers and carriers and they talk to each other. The other thirty-two I can name from the tallies because a levy leaves a hole in a book and I've read four hundred books in four years." She looked up. "And thirty of the thirty-two have one thing in common. Not their trade. Not their land. Their *grudges*."

She had the figures on a second sheet and she read them out, because a number said aloud is a number a room has to look at.

"Nine marks a quarter, forty-one men, two years. That's seven hundred and thirty-eight marks gone out of this county on a paper that says sundries. The mill's share is seventy-two, and I want it back, and I know I'm not getting it.

"Now here's what seven hundred marks buys. It doesn't buy a hundred men — a hundred men cost four times that in a year even when you're paying them badly. It doesn't buy a hall with a slate roof; that was built four years ago, before the levy started." She turned the sheet over. "It buys *the difference*. It's the money that means a man with no title doesn't have to sell a horse in February. It's the reason he could buy grain in Harrowmere in September at three times the price and not blink."

"So it's a subsidy," said Ansel.

"It's a wage for the valley," said Maeve. "Twenty odd marks a quarter out of every man in this county who's owed a living and can be bought for nine." She put the sheet down. "Osric isn't paying Hask to raid us. He's paying him to still be there in the spring."

She let it sit.

"One of them was fined by the Vale steward in '09 for a hedge he didn't cut. One was turned off Vale land in the bad winter. One had a brother who was caught lifting a sheep off the Ashgrove road six years ago and was given a choice — the valley or the rope — and took the valley." Maeve put her hands flat on the table. "There are thirty men in this county who have lost a thing to the Vales and have been paying nine marks a quarter for two years to a paper that goes over the cut to a man with a hundred men."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"Ansel," said Casian.

"Don't ask me," said Ansel Trask. "I've had eleven names and two hundred and I never once had this, and I'd like it noted that she found it in three nights with a miller's book and a grudge I didn't know I'd missed, and that I am sixty and would like a moment."

"How long?"

Two years," said Maeve. "Two years of nine marks a quarter. It's not a levy. It's a hiring."

---

They argued for an hour and it went badly, which was new, and the badness was useful.

Ansel's position was that the thirty men were irrelevant. A man with a grudge is not a man with a plan; he is a man who will do a small thing if he is asked and will deny it afterwards, and if the thing is small enough to be denied then it is not worth arranging a meeting about.

Vesna's was that Ansel had spent forty years thinking that way and that this was the first time in forty years that somebody had arranged thirty grudges into a payroll, and that if Ansel could not see the difference then he should stop being asked.

"And what would you have us do?" said Ansel, with a patience that was worse than temper. "Go to thirty men and say, we know about the nine marks, and be told to mind our business by thirty men who did lose a thing to the Vales and who have been waiting eleven years for somebody to notice?"

"No," said Casian.

Everyone looked at him.

"No," he said again, and got up and went to the wall, which was not a thing he did, and stood with his back to them for a moment.

"Ansel's right and Vesna's right and neither of them is the thing." He turned round. "The nine marks isn't a threat. It's a *record*. Two years of it, in forty-one books, in thirty men's hands, going over the cut on a paper that says sundries, and every one of those forty-one men can produce it if somebody asks them who they paid."

He came back to the table.

"I said in October that I'd let Hask make a move so that every concealed friend had to come out into the open. He made his move and it was eleven people in a barn, and I've been waiting a week to find out whether the rest of them would follow. They have. They've been paying into it for two years and it's written down in forty-one books in thirty men's hands that a levy goes over the cut to a man with no title."

"So what do you want?" said Maeve.

"I want a copy," said Casian. "Not the book. A copy of the paper. Of one of the eleven, in the man's own hand, that shows the levy and the date and the name of the man who collected it."

Maeve looked at him.

"You'll want it from a man who's frightened and not from one who's angry," she said. "An angry man will give you the paper and then go and tell the man he gave it to."

"Yes."

"Then I'll find you one," said Maeve Corran, "and I'll do it as a miller and not as anything else, and I'd like it written down that on the day I did it I was asking after the price of a carrier."

---

Maeve went to Harrowmere on the Thursday and did the thing she had decided to do on the Tuesday night, which was to sit in a carrier's office and ask about a levy in front of two other men.

She did not learn anything. That was not the point. The point was to be seen asking, by the two other men and by the clerk, so that in a fortnight when a paper went missing from a box in a cottage on the Harrowmere road, there would be a miller in this county who had been publicly curious for a week and no man would think twice about why.

She came home and wrote it in her book, in the margin, in the small hand she used for things she did not want read: *Asked at Harrowmere. Was seen.*

Then she sat for a while with her hands on the table, and the mill ticked at the bottom of its turn, and she thought: *I am forty and I have just done a thing that cannot be undone by being careful afterwards.*

She found him in four days, and his name was Widow Rill's son, and he was thirty-one and had been one of the men turned off Vale land in the bad winter, and he had kept every paper he had ever been given because his mother had told him to and his mother had been right.

It took two hours and a great deal of bread, and at the end of it Maeve Corran walked out of a cottage on the Harrowmere road with a folded, dirty, ordinary piece of paper in her sleeve that said, in a clerk's neat hand, that nine marks were received in respect of the eastern levy and would be remitted in the spring, and underneath it a signature and a date and a name.

She did not look at the name until she was out of the lane.

It was not Osric's. It was his steward's, whose name was Weyl, and it was countersigned by a man called Rennick.

---

Widow Rill's son was called Harn and he was thirty-one and he kept a barge on the Harrowmere cut, which is a trade that depends entirely on being trusted by men who cannot check on you.

Maeve went to him as a miller, with a miller's question, and sat in his mother's kitchen and talked for an hour about the price of carriage before she said a thing about a levy.

Then she said, "Twice a quarter there's a paper goes through your hands that isn't for carriage."

Harn put his cup down very slowly.

"What's that got to do with a miller?"

"Nothing at all," said Maeve. "I've been paying nine marks a quarter on it for two years and my husband signed for it before that and neither of us was told what it was, and I'm forty and I've a mill to leave to nobody and I've decided I'd like to know what I've been paying for."

There was a long silence in that kitchen, and his mother — who was seventy and had the flat hard face of a woman who had buried a husband and three children and had not let it break her — got up and went to a box.

"Show her," she said.

"Mother."

"Show her, Harn. You've kept every paper you've ever been given because I told you to and I told you to because a man with a paper is a man who can prove where he was." She sat back down. "There's a time coming in this county. I've lived in it sixty years and I can feel it in the way men talk about the east, and when it comes there'll be men who can prove a thing and men who can't, and I'd rather be the first sort whatever it costs."

Harn went to the box and took out a bundle of papers tied with string that had eleven years of dust on it.

"I'll not be paid for this," he said.

"You'll not be paid for this," Maeve agreed.

"And when it's done I'd like the miller to say that she came to me and I gave it to her and I didn't need asking twice."

She took it up to Vale that evening and put it on the table, and Casian read it and then read it again and then sat back.

"Thank you," he said.

"You'll not thank me. You'll tell me what it's for."

"It's for the one thing I can't do," said Casian. "I can take a causeway out of a river and I can take four men off a hillside without a sound. I cannot stand up in front of eleven barons and a Count in the spring and say a thing and be believed, because I'm sixteen and I've no letters to my name and no land and nothing but a story about a mist." He put the paper down. "But a miller's widow with a levy paper, and a fence with eleven names, and forty-one books that all show the same nine marks going over the cut — that's not a story. That's evidence, and it's the one thing in this county I can't make in a garden."

Maeve Corran sat down opposite him.

"Then I'll say a thing," she said, "and it's the last thing I'll say tonight.

"You told me in the summer you wanted to know what the mill knows. And I've spent three nights and four days and I've got a piece of paper out of a man who'd have been beaten for giving it to me, and the mill now knows a thing that could hang a baron, and I'm the woman who found it."

"Yes."

"Then you'll understand that from tonight I'm not a miller who keeps a book for a boy," said Maeve. "I'm a woman who has done a thing she cannot undo, and there are two men in this county who'd kill me for it if they knew, and I'd like you to say the number."

"The number?"

"You've got a number. You're a boy who counts things, I've watched you do it since August." Maeve put both hands on the table. "I'm asking you to say out loud how many people are in your debt and how many you're in."

Casian sat still for a moment.

"Eleven came out of a barn," he said. "Four work for me. One gives herself orders. One keeps a tally and won't say. One is sixty and has two hundred names. One is thirty-five and has been waiting his whole life. And there's a miller who's just made herself a thing that can't be undone."

"Twelve," said Maeve. "Say the rest of it."

"There's no way to make it so none of you can be hurt," said Casian. "That's the thing I thought I could build and I've found out in October that I can't. I can be anywhere. I can't be everywhere."

Maeve Corran nodded slowly and stood up and put her shawl on.

"Then that's the honest number," she said, "and I'll work with a boy who says the honest number. But I'll tell you the thing you haven't worked out yet, and you'll have it in your head for a long while.

"There's forty-one men paying that levy and thirty of them have a grudge against your father. In two years, when all this is done, they'll have no levy to pay and no baron to take it, and they'll still be thirty men in a county who've lost a thing to the Vales and have never once been asked whether they'd like it back." She opened the door. "Whatever you build out here, those thirty are in it, and nobody's asked them."

She went out. The door shut.

*She's right,* said Casian.

"She is right," said Argius. "And she is the fourth person this autumn to tell you something you did not want to hear, and you have written all four of them down, and that is the only good habit you have."
