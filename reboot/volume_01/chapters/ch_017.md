Seraine Varyn had been reading the eastern correspondence for four years, and in that time she had formed three opinions and had been persuaded out of two of them, and the surviving one was the reason her father sent her.

The first opinion, formed at seventeen, was that the eastern barons were liars. This had lasted a year and had been abandoned when she actually met one.

The second, formed at eighteen, was that the Count of Harven was the problem. This had lasted two years and had been abandoned in September, when her father had shown her the whole file and she had counted the letters and worked out that Harven had written to the crown eleven times about the eastern marches and had been answered twice, both times by a clerk.

The third opinion was the one she still held, and it was this: that the eastern marches were not dangerous and were not neglected, but were *unread*. Nobody who could write had ever been east. The reports came from men who had a reason, and the men who had a reason wrote reports that were shaped like the reasons, and the crown had been making policy about four hundred square miles of country on the basis of eleven letters and a great deal of assumption.

So when her father said she might go, she said yes before he had finished the sentence, and then spent nine days being afraid in private, and then went.

---

Duke Varyn was a careful man who had been at court for thirty years and had never once been on the losing side of anything, and was therefore regarded by everyone as a success and by himself as a disappointment.

"You'll go as far as Harrowmere and no further," he said. "You'll write me every week. You'll not go into the hills and you'll not go anywhere without four men, and if a baron offers you an escort you'll take it and count it."

"And what am I looking for?"

"You're not looking for anything. You're *listening*." He turned the file round. "I have eleven letters here from four men about the same four hundred square miles and not one of them agrees with another, and I have spent four years being told that the eastern marches are a problem and I do not know what the problem is. A man in a hall in the capital cannot find out. A young woman who is not important and who has read the whole of it can."

"Because nobody will bother to lie to me."

"Because they will bother," said her father, "and that's better. A man who lies to a duke's daughter has told you what he thinks is worth lying about, and that is worth more than a truth he'd say to anybody."

He closed the file.

"Seraine. When you come back you'll tell me a thing I don't want to hear and I'll not like it, and I'd like you to be certain of it before you say it. Not certain of the facts. Any fool can be certain of facts. Certain that it matters."

---

---

The thing Seraine Varyn was actually good at had nothing to do with being a duke's daughter and she had worked it out at fifteen, sitting at the back of a room while her father did business.

She was good at noticing when a man told her a thing that was true and was not the thing that mattered.

It was not a clever skill. It was an attention, the way some people can hear a note that is slightly flat in a room of forty singers. A man would say something entirely accurate and shaped exactly like the truth and Seraine would sit in her chair with the flat note in her ears and think: *that's true, and there's a wall behind it.*

Her father knew she could do it. He had never once said so. He had simply, over four years, started putting her in rooms, which she had noticed, and had decided not to mention, because being put in rooms was worth more than being praised.

---

She came east in the last week of OctoberShe came east in the last week of October with four men, a maid, two letters of introduction and a chest of books, and she was at Harrowmere for six days before she went anywhere, because the first thing she did was read.

That was the thing nobody at court would have predicted and the reason she was any good at this. She did not go to see the barons. She went to the county records, which were in a room above a chandler's shop and had not been opened in eleven years, and she read the tallies of the market, and the recognisances, and the fines, and the papers that had been lodged by men who would rather not have lodged them.

By the fourth day she knew three things that no one in the capital knew.

The first was that the eastern marches had been getting richer for six years. Not the barons. The *marches* — the markets, the carriers, the millers. It was in the tallies.

The second was that the pattern was wrong. A county getting richer shows it in the price of land and the number of men standing at the market on a Tuesday. This county showed it in the price of carriage and in the number of *new* papers lodged by men who had never lodged a paper before, and that was not a county getting richer. That was a county being *paid*.

The third was that in the last two years, eleven papers had been lodged in that office by a single clerk, on behalf of a single patron, and the patron's name was not written on any of them.

She wrote all three things down in her own hand, in a book she did not show her maid, and on the fifth day she went to see the Baron of Vale.

---

Vale was not what she had expected.

She had expected a fortified house in poor country with a tired man in it. What she found was a low house with a good roof in country that was being worked properly, and a walled garden on the eastern side that was the best-kept thing she had seen since the capital, and a gatehouse with an old man at it who took her name and did not look at her four men at all.

Edric Vale came out into the yard to meet her himself, which a duke's daughter would normally have counted as a manoeuvre and which she decided, watching him do it, was simply what he did.

"Lady Varyn."

"My lord."

"You'll be wanting the raids."

"I'll be wanting a great deal more than the raids, and I'd rather begin with something else, if you'll permit it." She got down. "I've been six days in the county records at Harrowmere and I'd like to ask you about a levy."

Edric Vale's face did not change in any way she could see.

"The sundries levy," he said.

She had been about to explain what she meant and found she did not have to.

"Come in," said the baron, "and I'll tell you what I know, which is that nine marks a quarter has been coming out of eleven mills in this county for two years on a paper that says the eastern carriers and I have asked three men about it and been told three different things."

They went in.

*He's not lying,* she thought. *He's not even being careful. He's simply been sitting in a small county for twenty years being told different things by three men and not knowing which of them to ask again.*

---

The fifth day was the baron. The sixth was the county.

She rode out with two of the Vale household and a boy from the stables, and it was the boy who turned out to be the interesting part, which she did not realise until much later.

He was the fourth son and he had been produced at supper as the one who could be spared, and he had the trick — she noted it within a mile — of asking her questions in a way that made her answer them at length and feel that she was being interesting.

By the time they were at Fenwick she had told him about the eleven papers and the carriage tallies and her theory about the county being paid, and had heard herself say the word "paid" in a tone she did not use to strangers.

At Fenwick the Baroness Calla received them, and did the thing nobody else in the eastern marches had done in six days, which was to ask her a question and then wait for the answer without helping.

"Lady Varyn. Before I give you anything — what have you already got?"

Seraine told her, briefly, because a woman who asks that question deserves a straight one back.

"And what's your view of it?" said Calla.

"That the raids are a symptom and the money is the disease."

Calla put down what she was holding.

"Say that again."

"The raids are a symptom and the money is the disease. A man in a valley with a hundred men does not begin burning villages in the autumn he has grain in the barn. He does it when he is being paid to, or when he is desperate, and either way there is a man behind him writing letters that are not in a book."

There was a pause of a length Seraine did not understand and would think about for a long time afterwards.

"Then you're the first person from the south in eleven years to say a thing that was worth saying," said the Baroness of Fenwick, "and I'll tell you what I know, and you'll not like the whole of it."

At Fenwick the Baroness Calla received them.

"Lady Varyn. You'll be the report."

"I'll be the report."

"Then I'll save you some time and tell you the three things people in this county will say to you." Calla poured. "They'll say the marches have always been a hard country and always will be, which is what a man says when he doesn't want to look. They'll say the Count of Harven has never once come, which is true. And they'll say there's an old thing in the hills that takes what it likes and nobody can do a thing about it, which is what they say after."

"And what do you say?"

"I say there are eleven people in a barn at Fenwick and they were taken out of a village four hours from the top of the cut by four men who died on a hillside without making a sound." Calla set the jug down. "That's not an old thing in the hills. That's a new thing and it's local and I have met it and I do not know what it is."

Seraine sat very still.

"You've met it."

"I've met the result. Wednesday before last, on the road, at Fenwick gate, at first light." Calla looked at her. "There were eleven people walked off a hill in the dark by a girl from the uplands and a man with a wagon, and there was a fire still burning on that hill with nobody at it and four men in the grass, and no one heard anything at all."

"And you believe that."

"I have eleven people in a barn and I have counted them myself." Calla turned her cup. "I don't know why you're surprised. You've been reading the tallies at Harrowmere for six days. You know what's been happening to this county better than any man in it."

*She's right,* Seraine thought, and was annoyed at how much it stung. *I do know. I've had it in a book for four days and I've been reading it as a good year.*

---

---

The seventh day she spent at the mill, and it was the most useful day of the month.

Maeve Corran was forty and did not offer her a chair and did not stand up when she came in, and Seraine, who had been in rooms with dukes since she was eleven, understood within ten seconds that this was a woman who had decided in advance not to be impressed and would not be moved from it by anything.

"I've come about the levy."

"I know what you've come about."

"Nine marks a quarter."

"Nine marks a quarter." Maeve turned a page. "And you'll want to know what I think it is and I'll tell you the same thing I told a boy in the summer, which is that if you ask a miller what a paper is for the miller will tell you what the paper says. It says sundries."

"You don't believe it says sundries."

"I believe it says sundries." Maeve looked up. "And I have been paying it for two years on a mill I nearly lost in the spring, and the man who bought the paper on my mill paid the fraudulent part of the debt as well, and I don't know why he did that and he's never told me."

Seraine sat down, uninvited, on the flour sack.

"Tell me about him."

"I said he bought the paper. That's what I said."

"You said he paid the fraudulent part and never told you why." Seraine held her eye. "That's not a fact about a debt. That's a thing you've noticed about a person and have been turning over since the spring, and I've been in this county six days and everybody has told me something about the fourth son of Vale without meaning to, and I'd like to know what you think."

Maeve Corran was quiet for a while. The mill ticked at the bottom of its turn.

"I think," she said, "that there's a man in this county who is doing a great many things and has told nobody what they add up to, and I think that's either because he doesn't know, or because he knows and thinks we'd all be safer not knowing, and I have not worked out which, and I have been working on it since August."

"And if it's the second?"

"Then he's wrong," said Maeve, "because a thing that a lot of people are doing and nobody has said out loud is a thing that falls apart the first time it's frightened." She turned the page back. "Now. You've come for the levy and I'll give you the levy, and it's three numbers and a name and I'd like it understood that I'm giving it to you as a miller, in a mill, in daylight, and that if anybody asks me I'll say a duke's daughter came and asked about the price of carriage and that's all."

---

Seraine wrote the three numbers down in her own hand and read them back and asked two questions and did not ask a third.

That evening she sat in a room at Harrowmere with her books and did the arithmetic four times, and it came out the same all four times, and the fourth time she sat back and looked at the wall.

*Flour and meal, the eastern carriers, sundries* — three lines, four hundred marks a quarter, and she had been reading county accounts since she was seventeen and she knew what a levy looked like and this was not one. Levies had a purpose and a name and a man who could be asked about them.

This had a name that meant nothing and no purpose that could be written down and forty-one men who paid it and never asked.

She wrote in her book, in her own hand: *Not a levy. A wage.*

They rode back at dusk and the boy was quiet for three miles, which was itself noticeable.

"You've a question," said Seraine.

"Several. I'm choosing."

"Choose the impertinent one. I'd rather be impertinent than polite, it's quicker."

"Then: you said the county is being paid. Paid to whom?"

She looked at him. He was sixteen and had a horse that was too good for a fourth son and had been pleasant to her all day in a way that had not once been stupid.

"To somebody who does not appear on the paper," she said. "There are eleven recognisances lodged in two years by one clerk for one patron and the patron's name is nowhere. Which means a man in this county can borrow without his name being in the book, and a man with no name in the book can be as large as he likes and no one will be able to say by how much." She gathered her reins. "Whoever they are, they are not a fool."

"No," said Casian Vale. "They're not."

"And that is the thing I cannot make anybody here hear. Everyone tells me about the raids. The raids are a symptom. A man in a valley with a hundred men does not start burning villages in an autumn where he has grain in the barn. He does it when he is being *paid* to, or when he is desperate, and either way there is a man behind him writing letters that are not in a book."

She stopped the horse.

"And that," she said, "is the one thing I cannot prove."

There was a pause of exactly the right length.

"There's a miller's widow at Vale," said Casian Vale. "Her name is Maeve Corran, she's forty, and she keeps a book that's better than the one at Harrowmere because she's had to live off it. She's been paying the levy for two years." He shrugged. "You'll find her easier to talk to than my father and you'll not have to be careful with her, which you will be with him, because he's a good man and it wears him out."

Seraine Varyn looked at him for a long moment.

"How do you know what she keeps?"

"I grew up here," said Casian. "Everyone knows what everyone else keeps. It's a small county and it's been raining since Friday."

They rode on.

*That was a lie,* she thought. *Not a lie. A sort of thing a person says that is entirely true and is standing in front of something else.*

She did not know what it was standing in front of. She made a note of it, in her own hand, in the book she did not show her maid, and the note said: *V. knows his tenants better than he can account for. Not suspicious. Odd.* She underlined *Odd* and then sat with the pen in her hand for a while and did not cross it out.

---

She wrote to her father that night, which was the eighth day, and wrote three pages and then burned all of it and wrote one.

*Father. The eastern marches are not what your file says. The county has been paid for two years out of a levy nobody can name, the Baron of Vale is honest and ineffective and knows it, the Baroness of Fenwick is the most capable person I have met in this part of the world, and a village was burned and eleven people taken and taken out again by something nobody will describe.*

*The Baron of Thornwall has asked me to stay with him and I am going, and I am going because I have been reading his county as well as Harrowmere and there is a difference in it I cannot yet account for.*

*You asked me to be certain a thing matters before I said it. Here is the thing I am certain of: there is somebody in these marches doing good and telling nobody, and the crown has been asking the wrong question in the wrong county for four years.*

She read it twice.

Then she wrote at the bottom, because it was true and because her father was a careful man who would understand it better than the rest:

*Also, do not send anyone yet. I do not have proof and if you send a man with a writ he will be told the wrong thing very courteously by eleven people who all believe it.*
