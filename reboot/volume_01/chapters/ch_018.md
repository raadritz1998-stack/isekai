The errand was entirely genuine, and that was its chief virtue.

His father required a heavy forged hinge for the granary door, twenty paces of stout trace-chain, and the mark of a licensed seal-cutter in Thornwall who could verify the parish timber tallies. A fourth son who could be spared from the estate for two full days could carry eight silver groats in his pocket, haggle with the blacksmiths, and return before Friday dusk. Half the tenants along the river could have recited his itinerary without pausing in their work, and not a single soul would give it a second thought afterward.

He departed on Tuesday morning rather than Monday—a small, deliberate variation that ruffled no schedules, yet ensured that anyone waiting along the highway on the customary market day had spent twenty-four hours watching empty mist.

He rode out with three distinct versions of his journey prepared, because an errand boy with only one explanation is an amateur reciting a script.

The first version he gave to his mother, and it was filled with the hinge, the chain, and the precise measurements of the granary frame.

The second he shared with his brother Brennan over oats in the stable, and it was filled with the hinge, the chain, and a great deal of fabricated adolescent interest in a carter's daughter who sold ribbon at the Thornwall fair. Brennan had laughed, punched his shoulder, and told him not to let their father see him blushing over red silk.

The third version he told to no living person, because it was the reality: he was entering Baron Osric's municipal seat for forty-eight hours with an empty ledger, an open purse, and an entirely malleable reputation.

*I have never done this before,* Casian remarked silently as his grey gelding trotted past the boundary stones.

"Done what?" Argius inquired in the quiet of his thoughts.

*Walked into an enemy's market simply to be perceived.*

"The young lady from Harven made an astute observation when you rode with her to Fenwick," Argius murmured. "She noticed that you have a habit of answering the question that was not asked. I must remind you, before you ride beneath Osric's portcullis, that this is the first occasion you have had to construct a public identity from nothing. Be exceedingly cautious regarding what you pretend to be."

*Why?*

"Because a mask is not merely a coat of paint," Argius said. "It is an architecture you inhabit. You may wake one morning to find the door has locked from the inside."

---

Thornwall market was three times the size of Harrowmere's, and it smelled of wet sea-coal, horse droppings, and fried lard.

Casian spent twenty minutes at an ironmonger's stall near the north gate, haggling with clumsy, earnest hesitation over a heavy iron hinge. He allowed the smith to bully him, fumbled his pennies twice in the sawdust, and ended by paying three groats more than the iron was worth. The smith was delighted with the transaction and shouted a crude jest to his neighbor about country squires who sent schoolboys to do a tradesman's buying. Casian tucked the greasy hinge beneath his woolen cloak with a sheepish, flustered grin, exactly as a humiliated lad of sixteen ought to do.

It took him twelve minutes to spot the boy who had been assigned to trail him.

Casian did not turn his head or quicken his stride. The tail was completely transparent: a lad of fourteen wearing a serviceable frieze coat that was two sizes too large across the shoulders, loitering beside the horse-troughs without a mount of his own, and staring with excessive concentration at the clouds whenever Casian paused.

Casian led him on an artless circle through the stalls. At the baker's cart, Casian bought a mutton pastry, found it undercooked, and ate it anyway with awkward perseverance. Near the fountain, he gave an entire silver penny to an old blind beggar woman whose knees were wrapped in sacking.

*The penny was the necessary touch,* Casian thought, turning toward the High Street. *A miserly lad is an ambitious lad calculating his margins. A boy who hands a full penny to a roadside beggar because he cannot bear the sight of bare knees in November is a soft boy with a tender conscience.*

"Your mother would endorse that sentiment entirely," Argius noted. "It also happens to be effective, which makes your calculation rather chilling."

*They will have me entered in their ledger as soft by noon.*

"Soft and harmless are not identical," Argius warned. "A harmless man is ignored. A soft man is targeted as an instrument."

*Exactly,* Casian replied. *A man who believes his neighbor is weak stops watching for a trap. He begins looking for a lever to bend him, and while he searches for the lever, his flank is completely exposed.*

---

The commercial house of Master Rennick occupied the corner of the market square opposite the guildhall.

It was an imposing two-story building of dressed limestone with leaded glass casements, three busy clerks scribing at elevated desks, and a polished oak sign displaying a white swan above crossed quills. Yet as Casian strolled past the yard with his hinge under his arm, his gaze drifted to the rear timber lean-to.

The heavy oak door was secured by a new, iron-strapped sliding bolt on the exterior.

*Why bolt a storage shed from the outside with heavy iron?* Casian mused.

"Because one is storing livestock that seeks to escape," Argius said, "or because one is housing guests who are not permitted to walk the streets in daylight."

Casian lingered by a cooper's shop, listening to the gossip of two carters sharing a flagon of small beer. Within ten minutes, the carters confirmed the suspicion: Master Rennick had received a party of rough-spoken men from the high cut three nights prior, and four young northern lads were being lodged in the warehouse loft until the river barges were ready for freight.

The pieces aligned with cold clarity. The eleven villagers taken from Lowmere had not been an isolated haul. Hask Veyr's hill-men were raiding the border settlements to supply laborers and captives, and Rennick was serving as the broker who laundered both the stolen timber and the displaced souls into the southern coastal trade.

Across the square, another phenomenon caught Casian's eye.

Before the arched door of Rennick's private counting-house stood a line of nine men. They were not indigent vagrants; two wore the respectable beaver hats of master weavers, and another had arrived on a stout cob with silver buckles on his harness.

Casian approached an elderly peddler arranging iron nails upon a wool blanket.

"A brisk trade for the scrivener today, master," Casian said, pointing toward the line with an innocent smile.

The peddler spat into the kennel. "Trade? That is not trade, lad. That is Rennick's winter charity. He is lending coin against next summer's wool clips."

"A generous neighbor, then?"

"Generous as a frost in May," the peddler muttered, glancing sideways before lowering his voice. "He lends forty marks on a parchment note, and when Michaelmas arrives, the interest has eaten the flock, the fleece, and the barn. Three weavers from the lower cut signed those notes last autumn, and today they are weaving Rennick's yarn on Rennick's looms for three pence a week. The man is swallowing the town."

*He is using the nine-mark levy to buy his neighbors' livelihood,* Casian thought.

"A classical consolidation," Argius observed. "He extracts silver from the river mills under the guise of an illegal surcharge, then re-lends that very coin to the town's craftsmen at usurious rates. Within three seasons, he owns the entire trade corridor from the hills to the port."

---

At eleven o'clock, Casian walked into the taproom of the Bell Tavern, carrying the greasy hinge wrapped in a bit of sacking.

Harn Rill was sitting in the corner by the water-butt, nursing a mug of bitter ale with an expression of stoic, uneasy defiance. On the bench beside him rested a flat parcel wrapped in oilcloth and bound with hemp string.

"You took your time," Harn grunted as Casian approached the bench.

"I had to overpay an ironmonger to keep the watchers contented," Casian replied in an undertone. "Take the parcel into the stable yard. A transaction conducted in an open yard looks like an honest carrier delivering freight. A transaction in a dark tavern looks like a plot."

They stepped through the rear door into the wet courtyard, where two draymen were greasing their wagon axles. Harn handed over the oilcloth package, which contained three sealed sample dockets of Thornwall wool-receipts obtained from his mother's relatives.

Casian slipped four silver marks into Harn's calloused palm.

Harn stared at the coins, his jaw tightening. "This is four times the carriage rate from Bradeley to the gate. My mother surrendered her papers so our name would be cleared when the storm breaks. I asked for no silver."

"You are a carrier on the river, Harn," Casian said smoothly, keeping his hands loosely at his belt. "A tradesman who works for nothing is an unnatural spectacle. If your neighbors see you hauling parcels for House Vale without charging a fee, they will wonder what secret bargain you have struck. When they see you accept good silver, they will know you are simply an industrious man doing his job. I am not rewarding your virtue; I am protecting your reputation."

Harn weighed the marks in his palm, closed his fist with a decisive snap, and tucked them into his leather vest.

"There is an auditor from the ducal court lodged at Harrowmere," Harn said, leaning closer. "A young noblewoman with four lances at her back. She was at my mother's cottage on Wednesday, asking about the carriage surcharge."

"I know," Casian said. "She was courteous, I trust?"

"Sharp as an adze," Harn replied. "She reads accounts like an upland clerk. If she comes to Thornwall, Master Rennick will find his ledgers turned inside out before sundown."

"Let us ensure the ground is prepared for her arrival," Casian said.

---

By midafternoon, the market began to thin as the November drizzle intensified.

Casian returned to the public room of the Bell, ordering a plate of salt beef and a flagon of small beer. Near the central stone hearth sat two of Thornwall's most prominent burgesses: Master Garrow, the town's principal wool factor, and Master Hallett, who owned the copper-foundry near the river gate. Both men were drinking spiced wine, their brows furrowed over an open sheaf of invoices.

"I tell you, Hallett, the arrangement is becoming precarious," Garrow was muttering, tapping his finger against a wine cup. "The northern lances were supposed to maintain order along the high track, not carry off whole hamlets in broad daylight. If Duke Varyn's commissioners audit the county books and trace those transit drafts back to our guild, we will lose our municipal charter."

"Baron Osric assures us the matter is entirely contained," Hallett replied, though his tone lacked conviction. "The Count of Harven has never looked past his own moat in twenty years."

"The Count has not looked, but his daughter has," Garrow retorted. "She was at Harrowmere three days ago. She has already impounded the toll receipts from the eastern turnpike. If she discovers that our guilds have been financing Hask's supply trains through Rennick's books, we will be tried for treason before the king's bench."

Casian took his mug, rose from his stool, and drifted casually toward the hearth, warming his boots by the coals with an air of vacant simplicity.

"Pardon, gentle masters," Casian said, ducking his head with an awkward, apologetic bow. "I could not help overhearing your talk of the northern road. My father sent me from Vale to purchase chain, and he warned me to avoid the high turnpike because of the great warlord in the hills."

Garrow glanced at him with mild irritation. "Your father gave you sensible advice, lad. Run along and mind your iron."

"Aye, sir," Casian said, lingering by the hearthstone. "Though our parish reeve says the warlord is quite ruined now. The reeve says the duke's men have already seized the high river crossing and that any merchant who holds northern drafts will find them worthless by the new year. My brother Gareth says only a fool would accept a Thornwall paper this winter, because when a baron falls, his creditors inherit nothing but the debt."

Hallett stiffened, exchanging a sharp, startled glance with Garrow.

"Who said the high crossing was seized?" Hallett demanded, his voice dropping into a tense whisper.

"Everyone on the turnpike, sir," Casian answered, widening his eyes in apparent wonder. "They say four wagon-trains have disappeared without a trace since Michaelmas, and not a single bushel of northern grain has reached the market. If you have silver out upon those hills, my father says you had best call it home before the duke's riders seal the valley."

Casian bowed again, mumbled an apology for interrupting his betters, and shuffled back to his corner table with his beer.

Across the hearth, Garrow pushed his wine cup away, his face pale beneath his merchant's cap.

"Did you hear that?" Garrow hissed to Hallett. "The valley tenants already know the supply trains are collapsing. If the Vale peasants know it, Harven's court knows it. I am not putting another mark of silver into Rennick's northern pool. I will call in my notes tomorrow morning and let Osric fight his own war."

"I will do the same," Hallett agreed grimly. "I would rather forfeit the seasonal profit than hang from the guildhall gallows for abetting a border rebellion."

Casian watched the two men pack their papers and depart through the rain, their cloaks drawn tight against the damp.

*Two pillars removed from Rennick's foundation,* Casian noted silently.

"Remarkably economical," Argius murmured. "You planted an unverified rumor in the ears of two nervous creditors, and within twelve hours Rennick will face a liquidity crisis. You did not wield a single blade, yet you have amputated his commercial support."

---

Just before dusk, the heavy oak door of the tavern swung open, admitting a blast of cold wind and a tall, broad-shouldered man wearing a travel-stained leather brigandine.

Tolan entered the room with an unhurried, measured stride.

He was thirty-seven years old, his dark hair streaked with iron grey, his eyes carrying the quiet, alert detachment of a veteran mercenary who had outlived three commanders through sheer caution. He bore no clan badges, yet his heavy broadsword hung straight at his hip, and his boots were encrusted with the grey clay found only along the high northern ridges.

He took a stool near the wall, positioned with his back to the timber partition so that both doors remained within his line of sight, and signaled for bread and small beer.

Within minutes, a third captain from Rennick's circle approached his bench.

"Tolan," the newcomer muttered, pulling up a stool. "Master Rennick wants to know why the supply wagons from the upper cut were four days late. Hask is demanding seventy sacks of barley before the first snowfall, and the teamsters refuse to harness without additional hazard pay."

Tolan chewed his bread slowly, swallowed, and washed it down with beer before answering.

"Tell Rennick the wagons were late because the road does not exist," Tolan said, his voice flat, steady, and entirely devoid of heat.

"What do you mean, does not exist?"

"I mean what I said," Tolan replied, resting his scarred forearms upon the timber table. "The crossing at the willow ford was dry gravel when I rode it in August. When the wagons arrived last week, the causeway was twenty feet under black water, the timber revetments were gone, and four drivers walked back to camp with a story about a mist that swallowed their lead mule. I went down into the gorge myself. There were no tracks, no fallen trees, and no sign of an ambush. The road simply vanished."

"That is peasant superstition," the captain scoffed. "Hask will not accept ghost stories in place of winter grain."

"Then let Hask go down into the mist and haul the grain on his own shoulders," Tolan said coldly. "I have served as a mercenary captain since I was fifteen years old. I have fought for seven different lords between here and the coast. When an enemy puts fifty spearmen in an alder grove, I know how to flank them. When an enemy puts an archer on a ridge, I know how to bring him down. But when a river valley begins swallowing wagon-trains without leaving a corpse or a bloodstain, an intelligent man stops riding into the trees."

He drew a small, leather-bound notebook from his vest and tapped it with his thumb.

"I am keeping an accounting of every cart, mule, and man that crosses that border," Tolan continued. "Rennick can threaten to withhold my retaining fee if he likes. But I am not leading thirty horsemen into that mist until I know what is waiting on the other side."

Casian sat twelve feet away, his head lowered over his empty bowl, listening to every syllable.

*He understands,* Casian thought. *He does not possess our names, but he has perceived the design.*

"He is the dangerous one," Argius whispered. "The other captains are arrogant butchers who believe every obstacle can be solved with an axe. Tolan is an auditor who happens to wear a sword. He will not charge into your traps; he will observe them from the perimeter, compile his evidence, and wait for you to overextend yourself."

*Then we must ensure he survives to carry that warning north,* Casian replied. *A frightened captain who retreats is far more useful than a dead captain who must be replaced.*

---

At four o'clock, Casian stepped outside to the tavern yard, where the fourteen-year-old spy was shivering in his oversized frieze coat beneath the eaves.

Casian walked straight toward him, holding a steaming earthenware mug of mulled cider.

The boy flinched, his eyes darting toward the alleyway as if preparing to bolt into the rain.

"Here," Casian said, thrusting the mug into the boy's cold, red hands. "Drink this before your fingers turn blue. You have followed me through five lanes of mud today, and you look as though you have not seen a hearth fire since sunrise."

The boy stared at the mug, then looked up into Casian's face with a mixture of terror and utter confusion.

"I... I am not following anyone, young master," the lad stammered.

"Of course you are not," Casian said pleasantly, clapping the boy gently on his soaked shoulder. "You are merely an enthusiast of country hinges who happens to admire my taste in ironmongery. I will not ask who gave you that sixpence, and you would be an idiot to tell me. But when you report to Master Rennick tonight, tell him the Vale boy spent his afternoon weeping over an overpriced hinge and drinking small beer at the Bell. It will save you a thrashing, and it will keep your employer in an amiable temper."

The boy stood frozen for several seconds, his chest heaving, before taking a desperate, scalding gulp of the spiced cider.

"Thank you, sir," the boy whispered, his voice trembling. "Master Rennick... he said you were a cunning devil who stole an entire water-mill with a scrap of parchment."

Casian laughed aloud—a clear, infectious, boyish laugh that carried across the wet stones.

"Do I look like a cunning devil to you?"

The boy looked at Casian's mud-splattered wool cloak, his crooked cap, and the greasy iron hinge tucked awkwardly under his elbow.

"No, sir," the boy admitted, a faint smile breaking through his grime. "You look like a squire who'd get cheated by a tinker."

"Keep that opinion to yourself, lad," Casian said with a conspiratorial wink. "It is the only thing protecting my allowance."

He turned and mounted his grey gelding, leaving the boy warming his hands against the clay mug in the gathering dusk.

---

Night had fallen completely by the time Casian reached the eastern boundary of Vale manor. The cold rain had turned to a light, freezing sleet that rattled against his wool hood.

At the lane turn, a lone lantern flickered beneath the bare boughs of an ancient oak.

Ansel Trask was waiting for him, wrapped in a heavy badger-skin cloak, leaning heavily upon an ash walking stick.

"You took your time," the old man rasped as Casian brought his horse to a halt. "Did you purchase your pretty iron?"

"I paid three groats too much for a granary hinge," Casian said, dismounting and leading the gelding by the bridle. "I frightened two wool factors out of Rennick's syndicate, I bought a mug of cider for the clerk's runner, and I sat twelve feet from Captain Tolan while he promised to keep his horsemen out of our valley."

Ansel grunted, lifting the lantern to inspect Casian's damp, impassive face.

"A tidy day's work for an errand boy," Ansel said. "Rennick's spies will report that you are an illiterate country soft-head, his creditors will begin demanding their coin at dawn, and his supply lines will freeze before Christmas."

"Then why do you look like a man who has just uncovered a grave?" Casian asked.

Ansel lowered the lantern, casting long, menacing shadows across the freezing mud.

"Because while you were playing the fool in Thornwall, something moved along the southern ditch," Ansel said, his voice flat and grave. "Three of my paid informants in the lower parishes vanished between noon and sundown. No bodies, no burning barns, no upland war-cries. Just an empty cottage and an unlatched gate near Ashgrove."

Casian stopped in his tracks, his fingers tightening on the leather reins.

"Hask did not send raiders," Casian said.

"No," Ansel answered quietly. "This was not a cattle raid, lad. This was a reconnaissance party looking for the boundary of your hand. Someone is probing the perimeter, and they are not striking at stone walls. They are striking at the men who carry the word."

Casian stood in the freezing dark, feeling the cold weight of the night settle upon his shoulders.

*The mask was successful,* Casian thought. *Now the work begins.*
