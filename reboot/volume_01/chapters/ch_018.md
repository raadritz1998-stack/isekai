The errand was real, which was the point.

His father needed a hinge for the granary door and a length of good chain and a woman in Thornwall who could put a mark on a seal, and a fourth son who could be spared for two days could carry the money and be back by Friday, and half the barony could have told you exactly what he went for and not one of them would have thought about it again afterwards.

He went on the Monday and he did not go alone. That was the other part of the errand and the more important one.

"Ansel's man will be at the Bell by eleven," he said, in the dairy, to Maeve and Corin. "He's a carrier called Harn and he's to be given nothing and told nothing, and he's to be seen with me twice, at eleven and at four."

"Seen by who?" said Corin.

"By whoever's paid to watch a boy from Vale."

"Are you watched?"

"Everybody's watched," said Casian, "and it's usually nobody and in a town like Thornwall it's a boy with a halfpenny a day and an interest in horses." He turned the map round. "I want to be seen doing a dull thing badly. A boy haggling over a hinge and being made a fool of. A boy meeting a carrier and taking delivery of a package and putting it inside his coat. A boy in the Bell at four o'clock asking where a man can buy a sheep."

Maeve Corran put down her cup.

"You're going to be seen buying a thing you don't buy."

"I'm going to be seen buying *everything*," said Casian. "I'm going to be seen all day being sixteen. And by Thursday, when a man asks whether the Vale boy is dangerous, six people in Thornwall will say no and they'll say it with a story."

---

---

The hinge was a lie in the sense that his father did need one, and the chain was a lie in the sense that he did not, and the seal-cutter was a woman called Bette who was seventy and took two days over a mark and was not a lie at all.

He went on the Tuesday instead of the Monday, which was deliberate and which nobody noticed, and he went with the story prepared in three versions, because a boy with one story is a boy with a story.

The first version he told his mother and it had the hinge and the chain and the woman who cuts seals in it.

The second he told Brennan, and it had the hinge, the chain and a great deal of interest in a girl at the fair.

The third he told nobody at all, and it was the true one, and it was: that he was going to be in Thornwall for two days with a purse and a face, and that for two days he could be anybody, and that the only question was who.

*I've never done this before,* he said, on the road.

"Done what?"

*Been somewhere as a choice.*

"The girl on the cut said a thing to you in October," said Argius. "She said that you answer the question you were not asked. I would like to point out, before the gates of Thornwall, that in two days you are going to be in a town where nobody knows you, and that this is the first opportunity you have ever had to lie about what you are, and that you should be extremely careful what you lie about."

*Why?*

"Because a lie is not a thing you say," said Argius. "It is a thing you find out you would like to be."

---

Thornwall market was better than Harrowmere'sThornwall market was better than Harrowmere's and he haggled badly over a hinge for twenty minutes and paid more than he should.

The Rennick shop was on the corner of the market street and Casian walked past it four times in the first hour, which was a deliberate thing and a mistake, because by the fourth time he had learned what he needed and it was worse than he had expected.

The shop had three apprentices, a new lock, and a book in the window with a swan on it, which was not a book at all but a card set up to show that Rennick of Thornwall dealt in everything and that everything was in order.

The lean-to at the back had a door with a good bolt on the outside of it.

*Why does a merchant's store have a bolt that close from the road?*

"Because he is holding something he does not want taken," said Argius, "or because he has recently had a man come up from a valley to ask about a thing."

*Which?*

"Ask a person, not me."

So he asked the woman who sold pies, and she said the merchants in this town had all got careful in the autumn because of the levy, and then he asked a boy who held horses, and the boy said Master Rennick had a lad from the north living in the loft since the end of September and he didn't come out.

*Nine men on a barn and a boy in a loft in Thornwall.*

"Yes."

*Osric's people are taking delivery.*

"It would appear so. Which means the eleven at Lowmere were never the whole of it."

Casian stood in the market with a piece of pie in his hand and understood, for the first time, that the thing had a shape he had not seen, and that he had come to Thornwall to be seen and had found something instead.

It took him eleven minutes to find the boy who was watching him.It took him eleven minutes to find the boy who was watching him. He did not look at him. It was entirely obvious from the way the boy moved — a lad of about fourteen with a good coat that did not fit and an interest in horses and no horse — and by eleven o'clock the boy had seen Casian be cheated by a hinge-seller and had seen him give a penny to a beggar, and had gone and reported both.

*The penny was the clever one,* he thought, walking to the Bell. *A stingy boy is a boy with a plan. A boy who gives a penny to a beggar because he can't bear not to is a boy with a mother.*

"Your mother would be delighted with that reasoning," said Argius. "It is also true, which is why it worked."

*They'll have me down as soft by four.*

"Soft and harmless are not the same. You are aiming at harmless."

*No. I'm aiming at soft. Harmless is what a careful man looks at and dismisses. Soft is what a careful man looks at and puts in the ledger as a thing to be used, and a man who thinks a person is soft stops watching him for the thing he's actually doing.*

There was a pause.

"That is a much better thought than the last one," said Argius.

---

The carrier Harn was waiting at the Bell with a package the size of a shoe and a face that said he had been told not to ask questions and had several.

"You'll want this," he said.

"I will."

"And I'd like to say a thing."

"Say it in the yard and not in the room," said Casian, and they went out into the yard where a man could be seen by anybody, and Harn said it.

"My mother gave up a paper out of a box and my family is in this now," he said. "I'm to be your carrier and I'm to carry the eastern traffic and be a man of no particular interest. And there's to be no money until it's done."

"There's money now," said Casian, and put four marks into his hand where anyone could see it, and Harn looked at it and then at him.

"This is too much."

"It's a carrier's fee," said Casian. "It's not for the paper and it's not for the risk and it's not for the box. A carrier who isn't properly paid has to explain to his family why he's doing a thing for nothing, and a family that doesn't understand a thing is a family that says a name in the wrong kitchen." He put his hands in his pockets. "That's the whole of why. I'm not being generous. I'm being tidy."

Harn looked at the money, and then folded it up very carefully and put it away, and said: "There's a man asking about a levy at Thornwall. Not one of ours. A foreigner in a good coat, been in the market four days asking about carriage and paying for drinks."

"Thank you," said Casian. "Tell me if he asks what it means."

"He's already asked what it means. He's asked eleven people."

"Then tell me what you told him."

"I said," said Harn, slowly, "that I'm a carrier on the cut and nine marks a quarter is nine marks a quarter and it's the same for every man who works the water, and that if he wants to know what a paper is for he should ask the man who signed it."

*He is very good,* thought Casian.

"He is very good," said Argius, "and he learned it from his mother last week, and that is the thing you should be frightened of, because it means this county is full of people who will be excellent at this the moment they are asked to be."

---

He walked the town in the afternoon with the hinge under his arm, and it was the hour of the day when a county tells you what it is, and Thornwall told him.

It was a fat town with a good road and four money-changers and a well that a baron had paid for and complained about. There were men on the corners doing nothing with their hands, which there had not been in the spring. There was a cart yard with six carts in it and none of them going anywhere. There was a woman selling bread at the price it had been in August, which meant she had bought her flour in June and knew it.

And there was a thing he had not expected and did not like: a queue.

It was outside a house at the end of the market street, and it was eleven men long, and they were not poor men — one of them had a good coat and two had horses.

"What's that?" he said, to the man selling nails.

"That's the factor. Rennick's factor." The man did not look up. "He's lending."

"Lending what?"

"Lending," said the nail-seller, "and if you're from out of town I'll say it plainly. There's a man in that house who'll lend you forty marks against your winter on a paper, and there's about a hundred men in this county who've discovered they need it, and I have not worked out what that adds up to and I've been thinking about it since September."

Casian stood with the hinge under his arm and looked at the queue for a long moment.

*He's not just paying the valley. He's bought the town.*

"He has bought the town," said Argius, "and he is doing it with the levy, and the levy is nine marks a quarter out of eleven mills in a county that is now queueing in a street to borrow its own money back at interest. That is the trick, and it is a very old trick, and the man doing it has been careful for thirty years and is now at the point where careful is not enough and he must be quick."

*How quick?*

"By the spring," said Argius. "He is buying everything he can before the spring, because in the spring there will be a Count's man in this county and he will not be able to do it any more."

At eleven a package went inside his coat in the yard of the Bell where it could be seen, and at noon he ate a pie in the market and was cheated over that as well, and at two he was at the ironmonger's and at four he was back at the Bell buying a drink for the boy who was watching him.

That was the part he had planned and it was the part that went differently.

"Here," he said. "You've followed me all day, and you're fourpence down on it, and I'd rather buy you a drink than have you go home and be told off."

The boy went white and then red and stood absolutely still.

"I'll not tell anybody you knew," said Casian. "I don't want to know who pays you and you'd be a fool to say. I'd just like you to be all right about it."

And the boy, who was fourteen and had been given a halfpenny a day and a coat that didn't fit, sat down and drank the drink and did not say anything for a while and then said: "He's not a bad man. He's got a daughter my age."

"Who has?"

"Master Rennick."

*There it is,* said Casian.

"There it is," Argius agreed, "and you may not use it, because a boy who gives up his employer for a drink is a boy nobody in this county will ever drink with again and you have just made him do it for the price of a cup. This is the first time you have used a person rather than hired him."

Casian sat with his hands round his own cup and did not feel good about it, and the feeling did not go away as the afternoon went on.

---

Tolan came in at the end of it and did not know who he was.

He came in because Tolan had come down out of the valley twice a week for four years and bought grain, and because there was a conversation in the Bell that he needed to be part of, and he took a table with two men and ordered and sat with his back to the wall, and that was that.

Casian was fourteen feet from him for half an hour and heard the whole of it.

"— told the man we're not buying," Tolan was saying. "Not at that price and not before the thaw."

"And the chieftain agrees?"

"The chieftain agrees to nothing in front of a room. But I had it out with him on the Sunday and the whole of it was this: we cannot buy, we cannot sell, we cannot move, and there's a man in this town who's been lending to everybody in the valley for two years and has now sent a man down to say what a raid on the Harrow road is worth."

The other man said something quiet.

"I don't care what it's worth. I care that it's in a room in this town and not in our valley." Tolan's voice had not gone up at all. "I've been in service since I was fifteen. There's a thing I know that I'd like somebody in this town to hear. I have run errands for eleven years, and the man who pays me has never once asked me to do a thing he wouldn't have been able to say in his own hall, and that was the whole of the arrangement, and it has been gone since September."

One of the men said something about Rennick.

"Rennick is a clerk," said Tolan. "He's a clerk with a daughter and a coat, and he'll be all right whatever happens, because a clerk is the only man in a thing like this who's all right whatever happens."

Casian sat with his cup and listened and did not move.

*Do you see it,* he said.

"I see it. He has not been converted and he has not been bought. He is a man watching a war be arranged over his head and doing the arithmetic."

*He's going to be the one who survives.*

"Yes," said Argius. "And he is going to remember the afternoon in the Bell, and in a year or two he is going to be telling it, and you should start thinking very carefully about what he is going to say."

---

---

He did the second errand at the close of the day, which was the one he had not told anybody about, and he did it badly on purpose.

He went back to Rennick's shop at dusk when the shutters were going up, and asked a question, and the question was foolish in a specific way: he asked whether the master did business with the Vale barony, and whether a boy might work for a merchant in this town rather than a garden.

The apprentice looked at him with contempt.

"Master Rennick's had the Vale business and lost it," he said. "It's the fourth son's own doing, everyone knows, and if that's your family then you've come to the wrong door and if it isn't you'd best not be a boy from Vale round here."

"Thank you," said Casian, and went out with his head down, and was very pleased with himself for about eleven seconds, and then stopped in the street and felt the floor go.

*I came here to be harmless.*

"Yes."

*And I have been the fourth son who cost him a mill, in front of three apprentices, and by tonight they'll be laughing about the boy who came to ask for work.*

"That is the perfect report," said Argius. "It is also the exact thing that will not survive contact with a clever man."

*Why not?*

"Because Rennick will not hear 'a boy came and asked for work,'" said Argius. "He will hear 'the boy who took my mill came to my door in the dark and asked me a question.' And a man hearing that does not conclude that you are harmless. He concludes that you are amusing yourself."

Casian stood in the street.

*That might not matter.*

"It might not. Or it will matter in a room in a hall in the spring, and you will be in that room, and a man will look at you and remember that you came to his shop and made a fool of him at his own counter for fun." A pause. "Which you did. And which you will do again. And which is the whole of the difference between a mask and a pleasure."

He came out at dusk and walked his horse through the edge of the town and past Rennick's shop, which was shut, and past a house with a good roof where a clerk lived with a daughter, and then he went home in the dark and did not enjoy the ride.

Ansel was waiting for him at the lane end with a lantern, which he had not expected.

"Well?"

"I've been about eight people's fool for a day," said Casian, "and I've had a drink with a boy who'd have been beaten for it, and I've sat fourteen feet from a man who is going to be the death of all this if I'm careless, and I've bought a hinge."

Ansel Trask, who was sixty and had done this for forty-three years, said: "And by the end of the month, six people in that town will have a story about how the Vale boy can't settle a price to save his life, and Rennick will have a report on his desk that says the boy is no danger at all, and men who think a thing is no danger do not watch it."

Yes."

"Then you've run a wasted errand very well," said Ansel, "and I'd like you to stop doing the pretty work and come and look at the ugly."

"What ugly?"

"Three of my eleven names have taken money since the levy went dry and none of them will tell me what for," said Ansel Trask. "Somebody in this county is hiring, and it isn't for a raid on a wall."

*He's right,* said Argius, quietly.

*I know,* said Casian, and followed the lantern up the lane.
