The thing Ansel had called ugly came at four in the morning on the eighth of November, and it came to the Vale granary, which was the one building in the barony nobody had thought about.

There were nine of them and they were not from the valley. That was the first thing, and Casian worked it out while they were still two miles out: they moved like men who had been told where a thing was and had not been shown. Nine men on the Harrow road at four in the morning with a cart, and no scout in front, and a lantern showing, which was the mark of a man who has been paid in advance.

*They're not after the granary.*

"You know what they're after," said Argius.

*Say it.*

"They are after a fire. The grain is worth less to them than the burning of it. Somebody has paid for the granary to be burned and the grain is the fee the men take for themselves."

*Then it's Osric, and he's decided.*

"It is Osric, and he has decided, and he has decided in the way he does everything: a thing that is not traced to him and that frightens a county into being reasonable."

---

He was in the garden, because he was always in the garden, and he had four seconds to decide and he used all four.

The easy thing was to take all nine where they stood, on the road, in the dark, in the time it took to think about it. Nine men and a cart and no noise and in the morning a cart with nothing in it and a story the drivers would tell for the rest of their lives.

He did not do it, and afterwards he could not have said why with any precision.

Some of it was the granary. Some of it was that a cart standing in a road with nine dead men around it is not a story about a mist, it is a hole in a county, and there were going to be questions, and questions were a thing he had decided in October to be finished with.

Some of it — and this was the part he looked at, later, and could not get comfortable with — was that he had spent a week being a boy who could not settle a price, and some part of him wanted a thing to be *witnessed*.

"Put them in the lane," he said.

"Which lane?"

"The Ashgrove lane, by the byre wall. It's got a wall on both sides and the far end turns. There's no roof and no grain and nothing on fire."

"You are going to let them see something."

*I'm going to let them see that the lane is where they are,* said Casian, *and then I'm going to let them find out what's in it. I want one of them to go home.*

---

He sent the Current through the barony and it took no time at all and it made no noise.

Corin was awake in the guardroom with his boots on because Corin had not slept with his boots off since September. Jory was at the west gate. The two men on the granary door were asleep.

*Corin.*

Corin Ahl sat up on the bench as though somebody had said his name.

"Get up. Get the four at the west gate. Go to the Ashgrove lane by the byre wall and go quietly and do not go into the lane." Casian's voice, in his head, at four in the morning, in the guardroom of a barony eleven miles from anything. "Nine men are going to be in that lane in about a quarter of an hour. They will not be there when you arrive. Bring a lantern and do not be the first man round the corner."

Corin did not ask who was speaking. He had been told to do a thing that used to be impossible by a boy of sixteen, and had stopped asking in October, and this was, he would say afterwards, the night he stopped being surprised about any of it.

---

It happened in the lane.

The nine men came up off the Harrow road and round the byre wall in the dark, and the lane was where they had been told the granary was, and it took them a moment to understand that the lane went the wrong way.

The first three went in and did not come back. The fourth went in and made a noise. The fifth to eighth went in together, shouting, with a torch.

And then there was a silence in the lane that the men still out on the road could see, because they had a lantern, and because a silence with men in it is a thing a lantern can show you.

The ninth man ran.

The Vale party arrived six minutes later and found a lane, two byre walls, a torch on the ground still burning, and no one at all.

---

That was the plan. It had worked, and it had worked with no sound at all, and Casian Vale sat on the bench in the garden and felt exactly nothing for a moment and then felt the thing he was going to feel, which was that he had been right about the lane and right about the story and wrong about everything that mattered.

Because the fifth, sixth, seventh and eighth had gone in shouting, and one of them had a pitchfork, and one of them had a torch, and one of them — before any of it — had been in the byre.

---

---

The call came at half past four from Jory at the west gate, and it came the way calls came at Vale, which was that a man ran.

Corin was there in six minutes with four men and a lantern, and the first thing he did was exactly what he had been told, which was not to be the first man round the corner.

He stood at the corner of the byre wall with the lantern behind him, held low, and listened to a lane for the length of a slow count of fifty.

Nothing.

Then he went round it, and saw the torch on the ground, and the pitchfork, and the bodies, and stopped.

"Sweet Christ," said the man behind him.

"Get the baron," said Corin. "And get Aldous."

"Aldous will be coming anyway," said the man, "there's one of ours in the lane and he's breathing."

And Corin Ahl, who had been an armsman for thirty-five years and had never in his life had a night go this well, felt the whole of his stomach drop, because he had heard the shouting from the road before the silence and he had heard a voice he knew in it, and he understood what he was going to find at the end of the lane before he found it.

Aldous woke because the dog went, and because Aldous had been waking at things for thirty years.

He was fifty and he was the surgeon-apothecary at Vale and had been for thirty of those years, and his house was the last house on the Ashgrove lane, on the north side, with the byre beside it, because a surgeon's house needs a byre for the horses of people who arrive at night.

He went out with his bag and his stick and no light, because a man going toward a noise does not carry a light, and he found the boy on the ground at the end of the lane, the one who had fallen, and he knelt down and turned him over and said something soothing, and the man behind him put a knife into his back below the shoulder at a scale that a man who does that once and then runs will do it.

Aldous Vale lived another eleven hours.

---

The rest of it was the longest night of Casian's life and it took place almost entirely in one room in a house three hundred yards from a garden.

He was there before the men got him back. That was not a comfort. He was there, standing in the surgery with his hands at his sides, while Nessa — twenty-one, with her sleeves tied back and her mouth set — cut the shirt away and got the bleeding under control, and while Aldous, who was grey and calm and had done this with other people's bodies for thirty years, lay on his own table and told her what to do.

"It's the lung," he said. "You'll not fix the lung."

"I'll fix what I can fix."

"You'll fix what I can fix," said Aldous. "Press there. Harder. Now — when the man came in with the shoulder, the one from the lane, how is he?"

"He'll keep his arm."

"Good." Aldous shut his eyes. "Good. That's a good night, then."

He was still himself at two in the morning and he asked for his wife and was told she was asleep and said, "Leave her. Leave her till it's light." At three he was quiet for a long time and Nessa put her hand on his chest and looked at Casian and did not say anything.

At five the men brought in the boy from the lane, who was seventeen and who had been lying in the cold for an hour, and he lived.

Aldous did not.

---

Casian Vale had stopped a raid at four in the morning without a sound and had saved a granary and a cart and two horses and a boy of seventeen, and there was a man dead at three hundred yards because in the ninety seconds before he arrived on that lane, a knife had gone into the back of a fifty-year-old man in the dark.

Nessa came out at six and found him standing in the yard and did not say anything for a while, and then said the thing he was not expecting.

"I want you to know what I'm going to tell people."

"What?"

"That he was hurt in a lane and I couldn't fix it, and that he was the best man in this county with a knife in his hands and I learned everything I know from him in four years, and that there is now nobody in these marches who can cut a man open and I am twenty-one." She wiped her hands on her apron and did it twice. "And that's the truth, and there's no lie in it, and I want you to see that there's no lie in it before you go anywhere near his wife."

"You'll not tell her the rest."

"His wife can put a thing together from four men and a hat," said Nessa, "and she will, and she'll do it in about a year, and when she does I'd like the rest of it to be a thing she heard from me and not from a neighbour."

That was when Casian Vale understood that he had, without noticing, hired the most formidable person in the barony, and that she was twenty-one, and that he could not have paid her enough and had not tried.

He went outside.

It was getting light. The lane had a torch on the ground in it, burnt out, and a pitchfork, and a boy's blood on the cobble, and beyond the byre wall the first grey of it was coming up over the east.

*I could have taken all nine of them on the road at four o'clock. Before the lane. Before any of it.*

"Yes."

*Ninety seconds.*

"Yes."

*Tell me what the mistake was.*

"You made the story more important than the ninety seconds," said Argius. "You wanted a witness. You told me in the dairy in October that you were going to let a move happen so that every concealed friend would come into the light, and then a move happened and it came to a granary with no walls, and you decided to make it a lesson instead of a thing you had stopped."

*That's not the mistake. The mistake is I got it wrong that nobody would be in a lane.*

"No," said Argius. "That is the mistake you are allowed to have. The other one is the mistake you are not."

---

Corin found him in the lane at half past six.

"My lord asks for you."

"Corin."

"I'm not going to say a thing," said Corin Ahl. "I've been with the man's wife since five o'clock and she's sat at that table with a cup she's not drinking and she's asked me twice if anyone could have done anything, and both times I've said the same thing, which is no." He stopped. "And both times I've been lying, and I'll live with that, and I'd like you to know that I know it."

"Nine men came to burn a granary."

"Nine men came to burn a granary," Corin said, "and nine men are dead in a lane and nothing was burnt, and I've been an armsman thirty-five years and I've never had a night where the losing side did better. I noticed it, is all. I noticed it and I've stopped asking what it means."

The ninth man got home.

Casian knew he would. That had been the point and it had worked and there was no satisfaction in it whatsoever: nine men had come, one went home, and by the end of the week the valley would have a story about a lane with no roof and no way out.

He would hear, later, what the man said. It would be repeated in the valley for years, and it would be the reason that for the whole of the following winter not a single man would agree to go out against a Vale holding without being ordered by name.

What the ninth man said was: "There wasn't a soul in it. We went round the wall and the lane was in front of us and the lane was the whole of the world, and there wasn't a soul in it, and I turned round to say so and there wasn't anybody behind me either."

Casian stood in the lane with his hands at his sides.

"There was a man," he said. "On the road at four o'clock. He was going to be in that lane no matter what I did, because the lane was the plan and the plan was mine."

"Aye."

"He was fifty."

"Aye."

"He'd have had a bad back, and he'd have been frightened of a raid, and it wouldn't have mattered at all, because nobody in that party was going to touch Ashgrove village and nobody would have been hurt in a lane." Casian's voice was steady and he hated how steady it was. "I made a lesson and it cost an old man."

Corin Ahl, who was thirty-five and had a wife and two daughters, considered.

"Then don't make lessons," he said. "That's the whole of it. Don't make a lesson out of a thing that can be stopped, and when you have to choose between a thing being seen and a thing not happening, choose the second one." He put his hat on. "I've been in a yard for thirty-five years, and the men whose names are still spoke of are the ones who won't make a lesson out of you."

---

---

He went to the surgery at eight and Nessa had him laid out on the table with his own clean cloth over him and his instruments put away in the box, which she had done at some point in the night and which was the thing that undid Casian, in the end, more than any of it.

"Why did you put them away?"

"Because they're mine now," said Nessa, "and a man's tools don't lie about on a table where he died."

She was twenty-one and had been awake for four hours on her own with a dead man and had done every single thing correctly.

"Nessa."

"Don't." She was not crying. "I'd like a thing from you and then I'd like you to go. I want the surgery kept open and I want a second person trained and I want it paid for, and I don't want it paid for out of pity, and I'll not have a penny of it that isn't accounted for in a book where I can see it."

"You'll have it."

"Then that'll do," said Nessa, and went and opened the shutters, because it was morning.

Maren met him in the passage at seven, dressed, with her hands folded, and she had been to the surgery and come back.

"Nessa's laid him out. She's done it very well and she's done it alone because she asked to."

"Mam."

"Your father's in the hall and he's asked for his breakfast and I've given it to him, because a man who doesn't eat in this house in November gets ill in December and I've four to keep alive."

"Mam."

"Casian." Maren Vale stood in the passage of her own house and said it very quietly. "I asked you in August to do it so that I was never sat in a kitchen being told about it by a man with a hat in his hands."

"I know."

"And I have been up since four o'clock and I have heard it from four men with hats in their hands." She put her hand flat against the wall. "I'd like you to look at me and tell me the truth of this and then I'd like us both to go and be told about it by nobody ever again."

He looked at his mother.

"Four men came to the granary and I stopped them in a lane," he said. "That's what comes after this. But the truth of it is that a man came at four o'clock and he was going to be taken out of that lane and I made him wait because I wanted him to be seen, and I got it wrong, and Aldous went out into a lane he had no business being in because a thing was happening next to his house, and I do not know that I could have stopped that and I'll never know it now."

Maren Vale stood there.

"That's the truth," she said.

"That's the truth."

"Then we'll not speak of it again and you'll not carry it round this house in front of the children." She straightened. "Elna's asked what happened and I've told her Aldous was very ill and has died, and that's what she'll be told, and in a year I'll tell her the rest because she's twelve and she's got a memory like a trap and she'll find out anyway and I'd rather she found out from me."

"Yes, mam."

"She's in the kitchen."

Casian went into the kitchen.

His sister was sitting at the table with a bowl in front of her and her hands in her lap, and she had been crying at some point and had stopped, and when she saw him she said the thing he would carry for a very long time.

"It was the men in the lane," Elna said. "Wasn't it. Nobody else could have."

And he looked at his sister, who was twelve, and had counted the eleven at the table in the spring and told him about a grey coat, and he did not lie to her.

"Elna. I need you to say the thing you said in the spring again."

"I told you," she said. "A man with a grey coat and a horse too good for him."

"He'll have asked about other houses too. Other villages. No — " He stopped. "I'll not make you work. Not today. Go and finish your bowl and I'll be in later."

He went out into the garden and stood in it for a while with the light coming up over the wall, and the frost on the roses, and a man in the house behind him being laid out by a girl of twenty-one who had asked to do it alone.
