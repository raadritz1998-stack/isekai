They buried Master Aldous on the eleventh of November under a cold, relentless drizzle.

Four hundred people attended the graveside behind the chapel—an astonishing assembly for a barony whose official parish roster numbered barely one hundred and forty souls. Freeholders, carters, upland shepherds, and tenant farmers had trudged through eleven miles of freezing mire to stand in the wet grass while the parish priest muttered Latin over the damp earth.

Casian stood near the back wall beside his father and brothers, watching the crowd file past the open trench. There were faces he recognized from childhood, tenants whose names Ansel had transcribed into his covert register in August, and even a carter turned off Vale land during the bitter frost of '09. Five months earlier, Casian would have seen only a gathering of drab, rain-soaked peasants; now, with the ledgers burned into his memory, every face represented a debt, a lease, a grievance, or a quiet obligation.

*Four hundred people,* Casian thought, watching the rain run down his sleeve. *Three hundred of them owe no legal homage to this hall.*

"That is the reality you have been slow to acknowledge," Argius observed in the quiet of his mind.

*I know.*

"You have spent three months learning the geometry of this valley," Argius murmured. "You have cataloged every ditch, millrace, and sheep-cote. You have yet to name what the enterprise is actually for."

*I know that as well.*

"Then state it aloud to those who must carry the burden," Argius urged. "And state it today, because this is the single hour when no one will ask why your eyes are cold."

---

He summoned the council for Sunday the fourteenth, meeting in the old dairy beneath the shadow of the cider press.

Eight people crowded around the rough timber table. Maeve Corran arrived with her flour-dusted account books tucked under her woolen shawl. Corin Ahl sat near the door with his broadsword unbuckled, his arms folded across his leather brigandine. Ansel Trask had brought his embroidered velvet cushion and an ink-horn. Vesna Orrow had walked down from the high northern crags in hobnailed boots that had been resoled twice with pigskin. Watt, the taciturn carter from the crossroads, sat upon an upturned firkin, declaring that an independent drayman paying nine marks a quarter into an illegal levy possessed an indisputable right to hear what the gentlemen intended.

And Nessa had come, taking a stool beside Maeve. She wore her coarse apothecary's apron, her dark hair bound tightly in linen, her hands bearing the faint yellow stains of sulfur and willow-salve.

Casian did not offer an inspiring speech. He stood before the map of the eastern marches and opened with a single question.

"How many people dwell within the eastern marches?"

The room fell quiet. The rain dripped rhythmically from the eaves outside.

"Four thousand," Casian said, answering his own challenge. "In the hundred square miles encompassing this house, the river, and the northern ridges. Ansel holds two hundred names in his private records. Maeve grinds meal for four hundred hearths along the water. Corin knows every veteran capable of holding an ash lance between here and the border cut. Yet across the eleven miles between Vale Manor and the burned church at Lowmere, there are six independent villages, three stone fords, and thirty outlying farms. And until this week, there was nobody guarding any of them except five men in a dairy and a girl with an upland bow."

"Where is this argument heading, lad?" Maeve asked, setting her spectacles upon the table.

"It is heading to an admission," Casian replied evenly, placing his palms flat upon the oak boards. "On the eighth of November, an honorable man was slain at Ashgrove, and the responsibility rests entirely upon my shoulders. It does not belong to Baron Osric, it does not belong to Hask Veyr, and it does not belong to the mercenary who held the blade."

A heavy, suffocating silence gripped the chamber.

"Nine raiders marched toward our granary," Casian continued, his tone cold and steady. "I possessed the means to dismantle all nine upon the highway at four in the morning without raising an alarm. Instead, I channeled them into the stone lane by the byre because I desired one survivor to ride home and spread a tale of terror. I assumed the lane would be empty. I was mistaken in a manner that an attentive commander would have foreseen had he bothered to watch the houses instead of admiring his own design."

Nobody moved. Vesna stared at the timber planks; Ansel traced the edge of his parchment with an ink-stained thumb.

"This is the truth I have been reluctant to confront," Casian said. "There is no conclusion to this war if it remains a private contest of skill. If I dismantle a causeway at a river crossing or erase an armed squad upon a ridge, the next incursion simply descends upon an unprotected village three miles away. I am a single person. I can move with extraordinary speed, and I have indulged a foolish pride in how swiftly I could intervene."

He raised his head, meeting each gaze in turn.

"I have treated this undertaking as an errand I perform in the dark," he said. "It cannot remain an errand. It must become an unbroken architecture that exists everywhere at all hours. Not one boy on a road. Not six men with spears behind a stone wall. We require a presence in every parish—someone who recognizes where to dispatch a message, a watcher at every bridge to count the freight, a ledger in every mill, and an honest carter who can answer an inquiry without trembling before a bailiff."

Maeve Corran leaned forward, resting her elbows upon the table.

"Then name it plainly, Casian. What are you building?"

"I am building an order where there is no corner of these marches where we are not present," Casian answered. "That is the purpose. Not to slaughter every raider who crosses the border—any fool with fifty lances can spill blood in a ditch. The objective is to ensure that the instant an hostile force gathers in the northern hills, their destination, their provisions, and their backers are known before their boots ever touch our soil."

"That is an immense ambition," Ansel murmured, shaking his head. "It borders on empire."

"It is not ambition," Casian retorted. "It is the bare minimum required to prevent our neighbors from being butchered in their beds."

Ansel Trask sat back, adjusting his velvet cushion with deliberate care.

"Let us understand the mechanism," the old merchant said, his gravelly voice filling the room. "You desire an administration with no village that cannot dispatch a runner, and no tenant who does not know where the runner goes. And you refuse to enforce it with naked steel, because armed garrisons frighten honest farmers."

"State the remainder of it, Ansel."

"It will require coin," Ansel continued. "Coin demands books, and books demand literate agents in thirty parishes who can record a transaction without panicking when a baron's bailiff enters the taproom. There are perhaps nine men and women in these marches who possess both the pen and the nerve for that trade. I know four of them. Two are seated in this dairy."

"Then that is the work of the coming winter," Casian said.

---

The practical obstacle lay in the funding, an issue Casian had dissected across two sleepless nights.

"There is another reality we must confront," Casian said, drawing a small, leather-bound tally-book from his tunic. "What we have accomplished since August has been financed through my private allowance, a loan against my horse, Ansel's discreet reserves, and the cash reserves of Maeve's mill. In total, we have expended nearly eleven hundred silver marks."

Vesna's eyes widened. "Eleven hundred marks?"

"Eleven hundred marks," Casian confirmed. "It paid for thirty mortgages, four freight bribes, winter feed for forty horses, and the release of eleven captives. At this moment, exactly twenty-four marks remain in the dairy chest. Within four months, our private reserves will be entirely exhausted."

"Then we have until March," Maeve said grimly.

"We have until the thaw," Casian agreed. "Which brings me to the proposal Ansel will detest, and which he must hear before he raises his objections.

"There is a water-mill at Vale that pays heavy carriage tolls. There are six freight drays operating between Harrowmere and the river cut. There is an honest, profitable commercial business to be established in this valley—in broad daylight, under a registered charter, with an open ledger. It can generate the silver required to maintain every watcher, courier, and storehouse along the march, and nobody will ever question where the coin originates because it will appear as the legitimate profit of hauling rye, wool, and timber."

Ansel Trask placed his cup upon the table with ominous slowness.

"A freight enterprise bearing a Vale boy's name," Ansel said. "Open to the inspection of every merchant in Thornwall."

"Precisely."

"Which means Master Rennick can examine our waybills, calculate our tonnage, and observe every bushel that moves through our yards."

"He may examine every line," Casian replied. "That is the core of the strategy. Currently, every one of us is compensated from an unlisted purse. That appears clever, but it is an amateur's illusion. An invisible purse attracts suspicion the moment an agent buys a new cloak or purchases an extra horse. An open commercial firm that pays regular wages and records its tithes in the county rolls is utterly uninteresting. Men of power never look twice at an enterprise that appears tedious, respectable, and modestly profitable."

"And when the neighboring barons ask why a fourth son of House Vale is managing a fleet of grain wagons?" Maeve inquired.

"They will assume a penniless fourth son is seeking a tradesman's living because he has no inheritance," Casian said with a dry smile. "They will dismiss me as a harmless clerk, and they will sleep soundly."

The discussion continued for three grueling hours, yet it proceeded with remarkable harmony because each participant possessed a distinct, necessary objective.

Nessa spoke for the surgery. She stated with unyielding composure that she was twenty-one, capable of setting fractures, treating wound-rot, and stitching arterial lacerations, but she refused to remain the solitary healer in an endangered province. She demanded an annual allocation of fifty marks to train three apprentice girls from the river hamlets, buy medical supplies from Harven, and maintain an emergency dispensary at Fenwick.

Corin Ahl requested four mounted outriders. When informed the funds could support only two riders and a dispatch runner, he accepted the limitation without complaint: "Then station them away from Vale. An armsman sitting in our courtyard is merely an ornament. Place one at the Fenwick palisade, one at the willow cut, and station the lad at the Ashgrove crossroad with fresh mounts."

Maeve undertook to inspect the tallies of six water-mills along the river, observing that three millers would cooperate for silver, two required diplomatic flattery, and the sixth was a stubborn fool who would resist until his water-wheel broke in the spring.

Vesna claimed jurisdiction over the high northern cut. "The high path belongs to me," she said, her fingers resting near her knife-hilt. "I discovered four hidden trails through the scree this autumn. I will walk all nineteen miles of the ridge before the heavy snows fall, and by midwinter no scout will cross that divide without my mark upon his trail."

Watt agreed to oversee the freight drayage, provided he was granted two apprentice drivers to handle the heavy baggage wagons.

Then Nessa altered the scope of the gathering with a single observation.

"You have assembled eight reliable people, a chest of ledgers, and an admirable design," she said, looking around the whitewashed walls. "Yet you possess no public threshold. There are four thousand inhabitants in this valley, and not a single freeholder will ride up to Vale Manor to report a suspicious movement on the turnpike. A tenant who enters this gate is marked as the baron's partisan, and most farmers would rather lose a sheep to a northern raider than alienate their neighbors by taking a side."

Casian leaned back against the stone wall, absorbing the point.

"We require an independent sanctuary," he acknowledged.

"You require a roof that bears no coat of arms," Nessa said. "A place where a carter can drink small beer, speak with an apothecary, and mention an unusual troop of horse without feeling he has signed a treasonous warrant."

Maeve Corran wiped her spectacles with the hem of her apron.

"There are several houses along the river road where every traveler halts as a matter of necessity," Maeve said quietly. "And the most natural of them is a water-mill."

---

Long after the lanterns were extinguished and the council had dispersed into the cold night, Casian remained alone in the dairy.

*Argius. What is the current accounting?*

"Eighteen souls," Argius responded from the dark. "You have formally bound Nessa, Watt, and the carters into your design. You have excluded the dead."

*And the total strength of the valley?*

"The valley cannot be reduced to a single ledger entry," Argius said. "Four thousand people dwell within these hills. Your father, your mother, and your brothers remain convinced they are weathering a conventional border dispute through patient endurance."

Casian sat with his palms resting on the rough pine table.

*If I had maintained my focus upon the stone lane at four in the morning, Aldous would be sleeping in his bed.*

"He would," Argius confirmed.

*Then what is the operational rule? Not an abstract philosophical maxim. What is the standard I must apply when the night is dark and blades are drawn?*

A protracted pause stretched through the empty dairy.

"You do not construct an object lesson out of an engagement that can be terminated immediately," Argius replied. "That is Corin Ahl's principle. It is an infinitely wiser rule than your theatrical stratagems, and you would do well to remember that you received your most profound tactical lesson from a thirty-five-year-old armsman who has never read a military treatise."

*It is a necessary constraint for an ambush,* Casian agreed. *It does not resolve how one protects four thousand souls across a hundred square miles.*

"No," Argius said. "However, it establishes the correct boundary. You cannot maintain an omniscient watch over an entire province. Therefore, the structure must be engineered to operate independently of your personal gaze. That is not humility, Casian; it is basic mechanics. You have delayed that realization since midsummer because you are sixteen and found immense satisfaction in your solitary supremacy."

---

Before entering the manor house, Casian walked the familiar perimeter of the walled garden.

The damp earth smelled of decaying leaves and wet loam. The fourteen raised beds were cleared for winter, their dark soil turned beneath coats of straw mulch. The irrigation sluice rested silently in its stone groove. Along the eastern masonry wall, the climbing roses were pruned back to bare, thorned canes, resting in dormant anticipation of the spring.

Beside the central pathway stood the young walnut tree, barely four feet in height, its slender trunk wrapped in protective willow wicker.

Casian knelt, resting his bare hand against the thin, smooth bark—a stem no thicker than two fingers.

*I planted you in May,* he reflected, gazing at the bare branches outlined against the misty stars. *Before the northern riders burned Lowmere, before the stone causeway was swallowed by the flood, and before a gentle old physician perished in a dirt lane three hundred paces from this wall.*

"You did," Argius said.

*I expected to remain here to harvest its fruit.*

"You will remain," Argius said softly. "That is not a consolation; it is an unalterable reality. It is the very circumstance that enables your design, and it is the burden that will make the coming decades agonizingly heavy. Stop attempting to persuade yourself that you have not perceived the cost."

Casian rose and walked through the postern gate into the manor hall.

A single tallow candle burned upon the high table, where Baron Edric sat alone, reviewing a parchment dispatch bearing a broken seal of red wax.

"A courier arrived from Harven while you were out," Edric said, looking up with weary, bloodshot eyes. "Lady Seraine's initial dispatch has reached her father's desk. The report contains specific references to the Thornwall accounts, and the Count of Harven has commanded me to attend his court following the spring thaw."

Casian paused near the hearth. "That is favorable news, father."

"It is considerably more promising than five unread petitions gathering dust in an archive," Edric acknowledged, setting the parchment down. "Yet it is a result that would never have occurred had a sixteen-year-old boy not placed an accounting roll into the hands of an inquisitive noblewoman. Therefore, hear me clearly, Casian."

The baron leaned forward, his weathered hands clasped over the table.

"I do not comprehend what you are executing in the shadows of this estate, and I do not intend to demand an explanation. I have considered the matter since August, and each time I approach the truth, I conclude that I would rather retain a fortified roof, full granaries, and my youngest son seated at my supper board."

His gaze bore into Casian with startling intensity.

"Whatever power or organization you are assembling beyond these walls, it is an enterprise for which ordinary men will become indebted to you. And common folk do not discharge that species of obligation with silver coin. The day will arrive when four hundred families in these marches cannot bake a loaf, harvest a field, or settle an inheritance without your consent. On that morning, you will become the most formidable authority in the eastern province, and it will have nothing to do with magic or martial prowess."

The baron took up his brass candlestick and stood.

"You will be dangerous because you will be indispensable," Edric Vale said quietly. "And against that species of dominion, no stone wall has ever been built."

He turned and climbed the narrow stone staircase to the bedchambers above.

Casian remained in the shadowy hall, listening to his father's receding footsteps.

"Your father possesses little cunning," Argius observed from the dark.

*None at all,* Casian agreed.

"Yet he is an exceptionally honorable man, and he has just articulated the trajectory of your next decade from an oak chair, using five sentences and raising neither hand nor voice."

*Yes.*

"Do you intend to heed his warning?"

Casian walked to the narrow casement window, looking out into the cold blackness of the valley.

*No,* Casian whispered. *I intend to accelerate the pace.*

He stood before the glass as the midnight wind whistled through the timber eaves. Out in the vast, shadowed basin, four thousand people slept in their beds—eleven sheltered at Fenwick, forty-one bound to an extortionate levy, a desperate warlord brooding in the northern crags, and a ruthless factor counting silver in Thornwall.

*Argius. When the work is accomplished. When a courier stands in every hamlet, an accurate ledger rests in every mill, and no family on this border must wait upon a courtier in Harven to secure their roof.*

"Yes?"

*What will remain for me to do?*

A protracted, profound stillness settled over the room—the stillness of two minds gazing into a horizon devoid of boundaries.

"That," Argius said quietly, "is the single inquiry you have posed to which I genuinely yearn to know the answer."

Casian extinguished the candle and retired into the quiet dark of his home.
