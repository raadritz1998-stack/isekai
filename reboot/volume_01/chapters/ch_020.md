They buried Aldous on the eleventh in the rain, and four hundred people came, which in a barony of a hundred and forty meant that people had come from eleven miles away.

Casian stood at the back with his family and watched them come. There were men there whose names he knew and men there whose names he had got from Ansel in August, and one of them was the man turned off Vale land in the bad winter, which was a thing he would not have known about in July and had known about for three months, and it made the whole of the crowd a different thing to look at.

*Four hundred people,* he thought. *Three hundred of them aren't ours.*

"Which is the thing you have been slow about," said Argius.

*I know.*

"You have spent since August learning a county. You have not once asked what it is for."

*I know.*

"Then say it to somebody who can answer," said Argius, "and do it today, because today is the one day nobody will ask you why you look like that."

---

He called them for the Sunday, which was the fourteenth, and he called them to the dairy, and there were seven.

Maeve came because she came. Corin came because he came. Ansel came with his own cushion. Vesna came down off the upland in boots that had been mended twice. Watt came, which surprised everybody, and said that a man of fifty-one had a right to know a thing he was nine marks a quarter into. And Nessa came, which surprised everybody more, and which Casian had asked for himself.

He did not begin with a speech. He began with a question, because he had spent two days working out what to ask.

"How many people are in the eastern marches?"

Nobody answered, because it was not a question anybody could answer.

"Four thousand," said Casian. "In the hundred square miles around this house. Ansel's got two hundred in his book and Maeve's got four hundred through the mill and Corin could name you every man between here and the cut. And in the eleven miles between the Vale wall and Lowmere there are six villages and a mill and a ford, and there is nobody watching any of them except us, and we're five people and a girl and a man with a dog."

"Where's this going?" said Maeve.

"Nowhere good," said Casian. "I'm going to say a thing and I'd like it written down, and I want one of you to say it back to me at the end, because I've worked out this week that I'm very good at not hearing a thing when it comes out of my own mouth."

He put both hands flat on the table.

"On the eighth of November a man died at Ashgrove and it was my fault, and it was not the fault of Hask Veyr or Osric or the man with the knife." He said it evenly. "Nine men came to a granary and I could have stopped them on the road at four in the morning. I let them into a lane because I wanted one of them to go home with a story. I thought nobody would be in the lane, and I was wrong in a way that I could have been right about if I'd looked."

Nobody said anything.

"Now this is the thing I've been slow about," said Casian. "There is no end of this. If I take a causeway out of a river or a barn off a hillside, the next thing comes to a different village, and I am one person and I have been very pleased with myself since August about how fast I am."

He looked round the table.

"I have been thinking of this as a thing I do," he said. "It isn't. It's a thing that has to be somewhere all the time. Not a man on a road. Not nine men on a road. Every village with somebody in it who knows who to send to, and every road with somebody counting, and every mill with a book, and every carrier asked a question he can answer without being afraid."

There was a silence of a completely different kind.

"Then say what it is," said Maeve Corran.

"It's a thing that has nowhere in this county I'm not," said Casian Vale. "That's what it's for. Not to kill anybody. Anyone can kill anybody. The whole of what I'm building is that when a thing starts in the east, it is known before it is anywhere near a house."

"That's a large claim," said Ansel.

"It's not a claim. It's a description of a thing that doesn't exist yet and that I've been pretending does."

He turned and waited.

Ansel Trask, sixty years old, who had spent forty-three of them arranging other men's affairs, said it back.

"You want a county with no corner in it that can't send a man and no man in it who doesn't know who to send to," he said, "and you don't want to pay for it with a sword, because a sword is a thing a man can see."

"Say the rest of it."

"And it'll want money and money will want a book and a book will want somebody in every village who can write a line and not be frightened of it," said Ansel, "and there are perhaps nine people in these marches who can do that, and I know four of them, and two of them are in this room."

"Then that's the shape of the next year," said Casian.

---

Before the argument there was the money, which was unavoidable and which he had spent two nights on.

"There's a thing nobody's said," said Casian, "so I'll say it and then we'll do the arithmetic properly. What we've been doing since August has been paid for out of a purse and a loan and a grandmother and a miller's savings, and it has cost, all told, something close to eleven hundred marks."

Vesna put her cup down.

"Eleven hundred."

"Eleven hundred, and it's gone, and there's twenty in the dairy chest and a promise to Brennan that I've not paid and a horse I've no right to sell."

"Then we've four months," said Maeve.

"We've four months and then nothing," said Casian. "So the second thing I'm going to say tonight is the one I've been putting off since September, and Ansel will hate it and I'd like him to hear it before he says so.

"There's a mill at Vale that pays a carrier's fee. There's a wagon that drives up and down the Ashgrove road and takes a cargo twice a quarter. There's a business to be done in this county, honestly, in daylight, with a name on it, and it would pay for all of this and nobody would ever know where the money came from because the money would come from carrying other people's grain."

Ansel Trask put down his cup very slowly.

"A carrier's business with a boy's name on it," he said. "Which every man in Thornwall can see."

"Yes."

"Which means Rennick can see how much comes in and how much goes out."

"Yes."

"Which means," said Ansel, "that in a year there'll be a man in a counting house who knows more about your comings and goings than your own mother does, and he'll be a man you pay."

Casian nodded.

"That's the whole of the objection," he said. "Now I'll say the answer. Every one of us is currently paid out of my purse, which means every one of us can be looked at and nobody can see what we're in. That's not safety. That's a thing that only works while it's small." He put his hands on the table. "I want a business that pays wages in daylight and appears in a book in Harrowmere and is dull, because dull is the only thing that men who are looking never look at twice."

"And when someone asks why a fourth son of a barony is running grain?" said Maeve.

"They'll ask what else a fourth son does," said Casian, "and the answer is nothing, and everyone will be relieved."

They argued for three hours and it went much better than the last argument, and the reason was that everybody in the room had a thing they wanted and it was not the same thing.

Nessa wanted the surgery kept open and said, plainly, that she was twenty-one and could set a bone and sew a wound and had watched a man die of a knife in the lung and would like there to be a second person in this county who could do any of it. She said it without any drama at all and the room went quiet.

Corin wanted six riders and was told he could have two and a boy, and accepted it, and then said the thing that mattered: "Then they'll not be at Vale. A man at Vale is a man at a gate. I'd like a man at Fenwick and a man at the cut and a boy at Ashgrove, and I'd like them to be people who live there."

Maeve wanted the mill books of eleven mills and thought she could get six by spring and said that four of them would want to be paid and one of them would want to be flattered and one of them would tell her nothing for a year and then she would have her.

Ansel wanted the eleven names protected and wanted to be allowed to buy two of them, and was allowed, and did not say which two.

Vesna wanted the cut and got it. "I want it written down that the cut is mine," she said. "Not the upland. The cut. There's four gates on it that no one made last year and I'll walk all nineteen miles of it before spring and I'll know it by then."

Watt wanted to not be the only man on a road and got two boys, one of them the carrier Harn's cousin, and said it was more than he'd asked for and less than he wanted.

And Nessa, at the end, said the thing that changed the meeting.

"You've a room and a book and seven people and no door," she said. "There's four hundred people in this county and not one of them will come to a house with a Vale on the gate and tell it a thing, because a man who talks to the Vale is a man who has taken a side and there's a lot of men in these marches who don't want to take a side, and some of them are the ones we need most."

Casian sat back.

"Then we'll need a door that isn't the Vale," he said.

"You'll need a house we didn't build," said Nessa, "and I'll not be the one who finds it, because I'm twenty-one and I set a bone in front of a man who'd have been arrested for it if he'd been rich."

"Who, then?"

Maeve Corran cleared her throat.

"There's houses in this county," she said, "that every man will walk into, and one of them is a mill."

---

Afterwards, when they had gone, he sat in the dairy a long time.

*Argius. What's the number?*

"Sixteen. You have added Watt and the girl and you have not added the dead."

*And the county?*

"The county does not have a number and will not for a year. There are four thousand people in it and you have a brother and a mother and a father who do not know they are in it."

Casian sat with his hands round a cup that had gone cold.

*I keep thinking if I'd been looking at the lane I'd have seen him.*

"You would have."

*Then what's the rule? Not a rule. What's the thing I actually do at four in the morning?*

Argius took a while.

"You do not make a lesson of a thing you can stop," it said. "That is the rule and it is Corin's and it is a better rule than yours, and you should notice that you have been given your first good rule by a thirty-five-year-old armsman who has never been anywhere."

*That's not enough. That's a rule about a lane. It's not a rule about a county.*

"No," said Argius. "But it is the correct shape of the rule about a county, and you will have to arrive at the rest of it yourself, because I cannot see the county either. I can see what you look at. I have never once been able to see what you do not."

Casian set the cup down.

*Everybody keeps telling me that,* he said. *Everybody in this county keeps telling me I'm the only one who can see, and I have spent three months believing it, and a man died because I wasn't looking at a lane.*

"Which is precisely why the thing you said tonight is the right thing," said Argius. "You cannot be everywhere. So the thing must be built so that it does not need you to be. That is not a philosophy and it is not humility. It is arithmetic, and the arithmetic is very simple, and you have been putting it off since August because you are sixteen and you are enjoying yourself."

---

---

He walked the boundary of the garden before he went in, which was a thing he had done since he was nine and which he had stopped doing at some point in September and started again on the ninth of November.

Fourteen beds. The channel with its four leather gates. The glasshouse. The east wall with the roses along it, cut back hard, doing nothing until spring.

And the walnut, four feet tall and ridiculous, doing nothing until he was fifty-six.

He stood in front of it for a while.

*I planted you in the spring,* he said. *Before any of it. Before Lowmere and before the ford and before a man died in a lane three hundred yards from where you're standing.*

"You did."

*I was going to be here for all of it.*

"You are," said Argius. "That is not a comfort and I am not offering it as one. It is simply true, and it is the thing that makes the rest of it possible, and it is also the thing that will make the rest of it very hard, and you should stop pretending to yourself that you have not noticed."

Casian put his hand flat on the trunk of the tree, which was the width of two fingers.

*He'd have been fifty-one in the spring.*

"Yes."

*I keep doing the arithmetic. Every time it comes out the same and I keep doing it.*

"That," said Argius, "is the only part of you I have any real confidence in."

He went out through the stubble to the house and his father was in the hall with a lamp and a paper, as he had been every night for a fortnight.

"I've had the Count's man," said Edric. "The Varyn girl's report has gone south and it has Thornwall in it, and the Count has asked to see me in the spring."

Casian stopped.

"That's good, isn't it?"

"It's a great deal better than a fifth letter to Harven." Edric put the paper down. "It's also a thing that wouldn't have happened without a boy of sixteen putting a piece of paper into the hands of a woman who reads. So I'd like to say a thing and then I'd like to go to bed.

"I don't know what you are. I'm not going to ask. I've been thinking about it since August and every time I get close to it I decide I'd rather have the wall standing and the grain in the barn and the boy at my table." He looked up. "But I'll say this, and you'll hear it. Whatever it is you're building out there, it'll be a thing that men owe you for, and men don't pay a debt like that with money. So the day is coming when four hundred people in these marches can't do a thing without you, and on that day you will be the most dangerous thing in the east and it will not be because of what you can do."

He picked his lamp up.

"You'll be dangerous because you'll be *needed*," said Edric Vale, "and there's no wall in the world for that."

He went up.

Casian stood in the hall for a long time.

"He is not a clever man," said Argius.

*No.*

"He is a very good one, and he has just seen the whole of the next four years from a chair in a hall with a lamp, and he has said it in four sentences without raising his voice."

*Yes.*

"Are you going to take the advice?"

Casian went to the window and looked out at the dark of his own county.

*No,* he said. *I'm going to do it faster.*

He stood at the window a moment longer with the lamp behind him and the dark of a county in front of him, fourteen miles wide and four thousand people in it, and eleven of them asleep at Fenwick in a barn, and forty-one paying a levy, and one man in the eastern hills with a hundred and six and no bread, and one in Thornwall with a queue outside his door.

*Argius. When this is all done. When there's a man in every village and a book in every mill and nobody in these marches has to wait for a letter to Harven to be safe.*

"Yes."

*What will there be for me to do?*

There was a long silence, and it was not the silence of a thing thinking of an answer.

"That," said Argius, "is the first question you have ever asked me that I would like to know the answer to as well."

Casian turned the lamp down and went up to bed in his father's house.
