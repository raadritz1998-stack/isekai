The hall at Thornwall was a good room and Hask had been in it once, four years ago, and had been given the chair by the fire and the second-best wine and had been asked three good questions.

He came down off the cut on the nineteenth of November with Tolan and eight men and they came at night and by the drove, which was a thing no man who held a valley should have to do to visit a neighbour eleven miles off, and he was aware of that the whole way and did not say so.

Osric met him in the yard himself, which was courtesy, and did not embrace him, which was arithmetic.

"My lord."

"Chieftain." Osric's hand was dry and his grip was brief. "You'll want food and you'll not want to talk until you've had it. There's a man come down from Harrowmere who talks too much and I've put him in the small room with a bird."

"What kind of bird?"

"A heron," said Osric. "It belongs to the Count. He carries it about. I have not asked why."

---

There were five of them at the table and the table had been laid for four, which Hask noticed, and which meant Osric had intended to keep something out of the room and had changed his mind.

Osric at the head. Weyl the steward at the corner with a box of papers and a face like a wall. Rennick, who was cheerful and had a new coat. Tolan, standing, because Tolan did not sit at other men's tables. And Hask.

And a boy came in and out with the jug, who Hask did not look at, because a boy with a jug is furniture.

"Now," said Osric, when the plates were down. "Rennick. Tell the chieftain what you told me."

Rennick was cheerful. That was the man's whole trouble and it was why he was a clerk: a cheerful man says a thing plainly and hears it after.

"The levy's finished," he said. "Not stopped. *Finished.* There's two of my eleven closed their books and won't say why, and one of them has been talking to a woman off the Vale mill who's been asking about the price of carriage for a fortnight, and I've had a man watching her and she's been to three carriers and a paper-house." He drank. "The levy's dead. There's no buying in this county after Christmas."

Hask put his cup down.

"How much have we got?"

"Four hundred marks in the box and forty-one men who've stopped paying." Rennick shrugged. "It'll keep a hundred men to the thaw. Not through a second winter."

"Then say the other thing," said Osric.

Rennick glanced at him.

"Say the other thing," said Osric again, "or I'll say it, and you'll not like the way I say it."

"Then here's the other thing," said Rennick, "and I'd like it noticed that I said it in front of a man who's been in this county eleven years and knows it better than me. There's a report gone south. The Varyn girl wrote it in October and it went to the Count and the Count has read it and the Count has asked the Baron of Vale to come to Harven in the spring."

Nobody moved.

"To Harven," said Hask.

"To Harven, in the spring, to explain his letters and his raids and his county, and there's a duke's daughter who's been six days in the records at Harrowmere and has been to see him twice." Rennick turned his cup round. "My lord. I'll say it to your face and then I'll be quiet. There's a report in the south with Thornwall in it and there's a man from Vale going to Harven to be believed, and I have been in trade eleven years and I know what that is. That's not a levy that's stopped. That's a reckoning that's started."

---

The room was quiet for a moment, and it was Osric who filled it, and he did it without heat, which was the thing about him.

"The report has three things in it," he said. "I have a copy, because a man in Harrowmere owed me a favour from '09. It says the county has been paid out of a levy that cannot be traced. It says there are eleven recognisances lodged by one clerk for one patron and the patron's name is nowhere. And it says the raids are a symptom and the money is the disease." He looked up. "It does not name me. It names a *pattern*."

"Then it's nothing," said Hask.

"It is worse than nothing. If she had named me I could answer it — I could go to Harven and open my books and be a careful man accused by a girl who does not know a road from a river." Osric put both hands flat on the table. "She has named a pattern, and the Count will read it and will not know what to do with it, and a Count who does not know what to do with a thing sends a man with a writ to find out. And a man with a writ in the eastern marches in the spring will find the valley, and will find a hundred and six men in it with no title, and will come back and tell the Count about *that*."

"Then we take the eastern strip before the spring," said Rennick, "and we're a fat county with a paper and a title and nobody's interested."

"How?"

"By the deed. The four parcels the Vales have held for four generations against a claim that isn't written down anywhere. A man in the Exchequer at Harven owes me four hundred marks and can file a finding, and I can make the other two quiet, and by the spring there is a suit before the Count in which the Vales must produce a paper they do not have." Rennick shrugged. "It'll take two years. We don't have two years."

"No," said Osric.

"No," said Rennick, "so I'll say the thing I came here to say and then you can have me out of the room.

"It's not the deed. It's the girl's report. The report says the money is the disease, and she is right, and she cannot prove it, and in the spring she will go to Harven with a pattern and no name. Unless somebody gives her a name."

Hask looked at him.

"Give her the valley," said Rennick.

---

The boy came round with the jug.

He was a boy of sixteen in a servant's coat with his sleeves too long, and he filled Hask's cup and Weyl's cup and went and stood at the back by the sideboard where a boy with a jug stood, and nobody looked at him.

Tolan was standing by the window with his arms folded and looked at him for about four seconds and then looked away.

"Say that again," said Hask.

"Give her the valley," said Rennick. "Not you. *The valley.* There's a hundred and six men in the upper Veyr with no title and no grain and a burned village and eleven people taken out of a barn, and if the Count wants a thing to put in a report, there it is, and it is not Thornwall. It's a warlord in the hills that the Baron of Thornwall has been warning the Count about for two years." He drank. "I'd have the letters written by March and dated in '16."

"Forged."

"Would you like to hear the alternative?"

"Go on."

"The alternative is the Church," said Rennick, "and I've saved it for last because it's the one that costs."

Osric did not move.

"There's a Warden at Harrowmere, named Selwyn, who has spent eleven years writing to the Synod about the eastern marches. He believes there is a thing out here. Not a man — a *thing*. An old horror in the hills that the people of this county have been buying off with stones for a hundred years, and he has four folders of evidence and no bishop will read them because there has never been a single death he could put a name to." Rennick turned his hands over. "And then in August a holding burned and two people died, and in September a causeway came out of a river, and in November eight men went into a lane at Ashgrove and none of them came out, and all of that is in a report by a duke's daughter that is going to the Count in the spring."

There was a long silence.

"Say the rest of it," said Osric.

"Give it to him," said Rennick. "Not a warlord. Not a levy. Give the Warden a report that says the eastern marches are harbouring the thing he's been looking for for eleven years, and let him do what he does. He'll come east with a writ and a fire and he'll look in the wrong places and be told the wrong names *very courteously*, and at the end of it there'll be a burned chapel and a scandal in the Church, and the Count will not be looking at Thornwall in the spring because he'll be looking at a heresy."

"And the Vales?"

"The Vales will be a small barony that was unlucky," said Rennick, "and by the autumn they'll be a small barony that is finished, and nobody will have touched them. That's the whole of the beauty of it. You don't burn a man's house. You have a Church burn his name."

---

Hask had been in rooms like this four times in his life and had never said much in any of them.

He said: "It's a good plan and I'll not be part of it."

"Say why," said Osric.

"Because it's the plan of a man who has never had a thing done to him." Hask put his cup down. "There's a causeway gone out of the Veyr in September and it didn't go by weather. There's nine men went into a lane at Ashgrove and the one who came back has told it the same way four times and he is not a liar. There's eleven people out of a barn above the ford with four men dead and nobody heard a thing. I have had a hundred and six men in a valley for eleven years and I have never once had a thing happen that I could not put a man's name to, and since August there have been four."

The room was very quiet.

"And your captains laughed at you," said Osric.

"My captains laughed at me," said Hask, "and I wrote it down anyway, and I would rather be laughed at and be right than be the man who gave a report to a Church and found out afterwards what he had given it to."

"Then what would you do?"

"I'd find out what it is before I did anything at all," said Hask, "and if I could not find out what it is, I would not put my hand on a thing I did not understand in the hope that it was on my side."

Osric sat back.

"That is the answer of a man with a hundred and six men and a valley," he said. "I have a title and a road and eleven parcels and thirty years of being careful, and I have spent four years buying silence in this county and it has run out, and I am not going to be the man who did nothing and lost the road. We'll do both. We'll do the deed in the spring, and we'll do the letters in the spring, and we'll give the Warden his report in the spring, and if one of them lands, I am safe, and if two of them land, I am a Count's friend."

"And if all three land?"

"Then it will have been a busy spring," said Osric, "and I will have been seen to have helped a Warden and to have raised a suit and to have been honest throughout, and there is not a court in Varenthos that can hang a man for that."

---

It was at that point that Osric asked the question.

He asked it of the room, in the way a man asks a thing he has been turning over for a month.

"Tell me this, then. Both of you. If there is a thing in these marches — and I will allow it, because the two of you have between you more experience of the east than any man in the capital — what does it want? A man who can take a causeway out of a river and eight men out of a lane and leave a barn untouched. What is he after?"

"It wants the Vales protected," said Rennick. "It's been sitting on that barony since August."

"It wants the road," said Tolan. "Everything that's happened has been about a road."

"It has killed nobody who wasn't carrying a weapon," said Hask, "and it took eleven villagers out of a barn and did not take a penny of the grain that was in the same valley. I've thought about that for a month and I cannot make it a man who wants a road."

Osric looked at the three of them.

"Then it is a thing that wants what a thing wants and cannot be reasoned with," he said, "and I would still like to know."

And a boy at the back by the sideboard, with a jug in his hands and his sleeves too long, said:

"To be left alone."

---

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Osric turned his head. It took him a second to find the boy, which was itself a thing, because there were only five men at that table and the boy was six feet behind the chair.

"Say that again."

"To be left alone, my lord," said the boy. "You asked what it wants. That's what a thing wants that has taken a causeway out of a river and eight men out of a lane and hasn't burned a barn or taken a coin. It doesn't want the road and it doesn't want the Vales. It's not doing any of it because it wants something. It's doing it because somebody keeps coming."

Osric stared at him.

Then he laughed — a short, dry, entirely genuine sound, and he reached into his coat and took out a coin and held it up.

"Six men at this table with eleven years of the east between them," he said, "and it's the jug that has the sense. What's your name, boy?"

"Casian, my lord."

"Casian." Osric turned the coin over and put it in the boy's hand and closed his fingers on it. "You'll do well if you keep listening. Now go and tell them in the kitchen we'll want the fire built up and something hot at eleven, and then you can go to your bed."

"Thank you, my lord."

The boy went out and shut the door.

---

"That," said Osric, "is what I have instead of an answer. A jug of wine and a boy with his sleeves too long."

"Send the boy away," said Tolan.

Osric looked at him.

"Send him away from this house tonight," said Tolan. "Give him a mark and send him home and have him watched for a month."

There was a silence of a completely different quality.

"Tolan," said Osric, patiently. "The boy has been in this kitchen since he was nine. Weyl hired his mother."

"No," said Tolan.

"Say that again?"

"No, he has not." Tolan had not moved from the window and his voice had not gone up. "I have been in this house four times in four years. I know the faces in that kitchen because I eat in it and I sleep in the stable and a man who does that learns a house. There is a cook and two girls and a man with one arm and a boy of twelve who carries the wood. There is no jug. There has never been a jug."

Weyl looked up from his box.

"There's a boy from the town," he said. "He came up with the wine. The cellarer sent him."

"Which cellarer?"

"The— " Weyl stopped.

"Which cellarer," said Tolan, "and where is he, and when did you last see him?"

The room was quiet.

"I want the yard sealed," said Osric. He said it without any change in his voice at all, and it was the first time that evening that anybody in that room had heard him frightened. "I want the wine counted and the cellar opened and every man in this house in the yard in ten minutes, and I want the boy found."

They found nothing.

The wine was where it should have been and had been signed for by a carter who had signed for it at four o'clock in the afternoon. The carter was a man from Harrowmere who had a good name and had carried for the house for nine years, and he had taken four marks off a boy in the market that morning to let him ride up with the barrels, and he could not describe the boy, and he was so frightened that Weyl had to give him a cup of something before he could speak at all.

There had been no boy in the kitchen. There had been no boy in the yard. There had been no boy on the road, and the gate-man had let in a cart and a carter and had not looked at the back of it, because a gate-man looks at a cart and a carter.

At midnight Osric stood in the yard in his coat with the whole household in front of him and said, "Nobody leaves this house tonight and nobody leaves this town tomorrow," and then went in, and did not sleep.

---

At two in the morning Hask found Tolan in the stable with a saddle over the wall.

"You'll not ride tonight."

"No."

"Then what are you doing?"

"Checking my horse," said Tolan. "I do it every night."

Hask stood in the stable doorway for a while.

"He answered a question," Hask said at last. "That's all. He answered a question and then he took a coin and thanked you for it and went out and shut the door, and there were five men in that room and we none of us looked at him until he spoke."

"He said 'To be left alone,'" said Tolan.

"Yes."

"Which is not what it wants," said Tolan. "It's what it wants *from us*. They're different things and one of them is a complaint and the other is a warning, and he gave us the warning and my lord gave him a coin."

Hask was quiet.

"You'll not say that to him."

"I said it to him twenty minutes ago and he told me the boy had been in the kitchen since he was nine, and I told him there was no jug, and he believed me for about four seconds and then he remembered he is a baron and I am a man with a horse." Tolan put the saddle back on its peg. "It's not the boy, Hask."

"What?"

"It's what the boy was carrying. He came up with the wine and he poured for four men at a table deciding to give a hundred and six men to a Church, and he did it with his sleeves too long and his eyes down, and he heard the whole of it." Tolan turned round. "We're going to be given to the Church. All of us. Not the Vales. *Us.* We're the thing that gets handed over, and the man who's going to hand us over said it in front of a boy with a jug, and the boy is going to be extremely interested in that."

Hask stood in the doorway.

"You've been saying that since September," he said. "The thing that's coming."

"I've been saying it since August."

"And now?"

"Now I've seen its hand," said Tolan, "and it's a boy of sixteen with too-long sleeves, and nobody in that room believes me, and I have started keeping a horse ready at night, and I'd like you to write down that I asked you to seal the yard in time."

"I'll write it down," said Hask.

"Thank you," said Tolan, and meant it, and neither of them said anything else, and they stood in a stable in Thornwall in the middle of the night with the whole of the next year already decided in a room neither of them was in.
