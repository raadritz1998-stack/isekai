The great hall at Thornwall was a formidable chamber of dark timber and dressed stone, and Hask Veyr had entered it only once before—four years earlier, when he had been granted the high-backed chair beside the hearth, poured the second-best vintage of southern red, and asked three penetrating questions regarding the northern passes.

He rode down from the high cut on the evening of the nineteenth of November, accompanied by Tolan and an escort of eight mounted spearmen. They traveled by night along the sunken drovers' tracks—an undignified detour for a chieftain commanding eighty lances in the upper valley, and the humiliation grated upon him with every mile of muddy scrub.

Baron Osric met them in the courtyard in his own person. It was a formal display of courtesy, yet he offered no embrace—a cold omission that signaled precise political calculation.

"Chieftain," Osric said, his dry palm gripping Hask's wrist for a brief second before releasing it. "You will require meat and hot wine before we open the rolls. I have an auditor from Harrowmere lodged in the west tower who talks incessantly, but I have placed him in the solar with a captive marsh bird to occupy his leisure."

"What species of bird?"

"A grey heron," Osric replied flatly. "It was dispatched as a gift from the ducal court. It stands upon one leg and snaps at flies. I have not inquired into the symbolism."

---

Five men assembled in the private council chamber behind the high dais. The table had been laid for four—an arrangement Hask noted immediately, indicating that Osric had initially intended to exclude someone from the conference and had reversed his judgment at the eleventh hour.

Baron Osric occupied the head of the oak board, wrapped in a heavy mantle of marten fur. Weyl, his chief steward, sat at the corner with an iron-banded chest of parchment rolls, his countenance as unyielding as a limestone cliff. Rennick, the merchant factor, sat opposite him, remarkably cheerful in a freshly tailored tunic of green broadcloth. Tolan remained standing near the arched window embrasure with his arms folded across his leather brigandine, refusing to sit at an alien table. Hask took the high-backed chair opposite Osric.

A servant boy in a coarse brown tunic with sleeves hanging past his wrists moved quietly between the sideboard and the table, carrying a heavy earthenware flagon. Hask barely glanced at him; in a marcher keep, a boy pouring wine was simply part of the room's furniture.

"Now," Osric said, signaling the steward to close the iron latch. "Rennick. Inform the chieftain of the news you brought from the market."

Rennick possessed an irrepressible cheerfulness—the defining flaw of an ambitious tradesman who enjoyed reciting bad news simply because it proved his grasp of the figures.

"The carriage levy has collapsed," Rennick stated, leaning forward and tapping the table. "Not paused, mind you. Terminated. Two of my primary millers in the lower valley closed their accounts on Friday and refused to offer an explanation. One of them had spent two weeks conferring with a widow who manages the Vale water-mill—a woman who has been systematically cross-referencing carriage waybills across four parishes. The flow of coin has dried up. There will be no unlisted silver moving through this town after the midwinter feast."

Hask set his silver cup down with a sharp thud.

"What remains in the reserve?"

"Four hundred marks in the strongbox," Rennick answered with a casual shrug. "And forty-one contributors who have ceased to pay. That will feed eighty horses and their riders until the February thaw. It will not sustain your camp through a second winter."

"Tell him the second development," Osric commanded, his voice cold and devoid of inflection.

Rennick hesitated, his smile faltering slightly under the baron's stare.

"Speak it plainly," Osric repeated. "Or I will recite it, and you will find my phrasing considerably less agreeable."

"Here is the second reality," Rennick conceded, glancing at Tolan by the window. "A formal petition has been dispatched to Harven. Lady Seraine Varyn composed the dispatch in late October following her audit at Harrowmere. The Count has reviewed her findings, and he has formally summoned Baron Edric Vale to present his testimony at the spring assize."

A dead, heavy silence enveloped the room.

"Edric Vale has been summoned to the ducal court?" Hask demanded, his dark brows knitting in fury.

"To the ducal court in April," Rennick said. "To present his boundary records, his parish tallies, and his accounting of the border raids. A duke's daughter spent six days inspecting the county archives and visited Vale Manor twice. My lord, I will state it to your face: there is a file in Harven that mentions Thornwall's transit accounts, and an honest marcher baron has been invited south to be believed. In twenty years of trade, I have learned the smell of a reckoning. This is not a temporary embarrassment; it is the beginning of an imperial inquest."

---

Osric sat motionless, his fingers linked over his marten-fur collar. When he spoke, his voice remained entirely level.

"The young lady's report contains three specific assertions," Osric said, drawing a folded leaf from Weyl's chest. "I procured a transcription through an associate in the Harrowmere registry. She asserts that the river parishes are being systematically drained by an unchartered levy. She asserts that eleven recognisances have been registered by a single clerk on behalf of an anonymous creditor. And she asserts that the northern raids are a deliberate symptom financed by unlisted silver."

He raised his eyes to meet Hask's glare.

"She does not name me in the text. She identifies a pattern."

"Then she possesses no proof," Hask growled.

"A pattern is vastly more dangerous than an accusation," Osric countered, leaning forward over the table. "Had she named me directly, I could travel to Harven with my account books, profess righteous indignation, and present myself as an upright border lord slandered by an inexperienced girl. But by naming a pattern, she invites the Count to dispatch an armed inquiry. When royal commissioners arrive in these marches with search warrants, they will explore the high cut. They will find eighty mounted lancers camped in the Veyr gorge without royal commission, and they will present their report to the crown."

"Then we strike first," Rennick suggested eagerly. "We seize the eastern strip before the ground thaws. The Vale estate holds four parcels under ancient usage without written parchment. I have an assessor in the Harven exchequer who owes me four hundred marks; he can register a prior title. By the time the spring court convenes, House Vale will be entangled in a property dispute that will take three years to resolve."

"We do not possess three years," Osric said.

"Then hear the third alternative," Rennick said, lowering his voice until it barely carried past the table. "The lady's report claims the money is the disease, but she cannot prove who holds the purse. She will arrive at Harven with a suspicion and no culprit. Unless we provide her with a culprit."

Hask stared at him. "Whom do you propose to deliver?"

"We deliver the valley," Rennick said.

---

The servant boy stepped around the corner of the sideboard, carrying the flagon of dark wine.

He was sixteen, with unruly brown hair and sleeves that fell over his knuckles, moving with the quiet, self-effacing caution of a scullery lad terrified of spilling a drop upon a noble mantle. He replenished Hask's cup, moved behind Weyl to fill the steward's goblet, and returned to his station against the timber wainscoting.

Tolan, watching from the window, fixed his dark eyes upon the boy for four long seconds before turning back to the table.

"Explain what you mean by delivering the valley," Hask said, his voice laced with suspicion.

"There are eighty armed riders in the upper gorge with no legal title to the land," Rennick said, gesturing with his ringed fingers. "A burned hamlet at Lowmere. Eleven captives removed from an upland holding. If the Count requires a monstrous perpetrator to satisfy his court, there is the target. It is not Thornwall. It is an outlaw chieftain whom the Baron of Thornwall has been attempting to contain for two years at great personal expense. We compose the dispatches immediately and date them back to the autumn of '16."

"A manufactured correspondence," Hask hissed, his hand dropping toward his broadsword.

"Consider the alternative before you lose your temper," Rennick replied smoothly. "The alternative involves the Church."

Osric did not stir. "Proceed, Rennick."

"There is an ecclesiastical Warden stationed at Harrowmere named Selwyn," Rennick said, his tone dropping to a conspiratorial murmur. "He has spent eleven years composing petitions to the Synod regarding the eastern border. He is convinced that an ancient corruption haunts these hills—not a reiver with a lance, but an occult malice that the local peasantry have appeased for generations. The bishops ignore him because he has never produced a corpse or a named heretic."

The merchant spread his hands in an expansive gesture.

"In August, a holding burns and two cottagers perish. In September, a stone causeway vanishes beneath a sudden river surge. In November, nine armed men enter a lane at Ashgrove and only one returns, babbling of a living mist. Give Warden Selwyn a dossier that confirms his theory. Convince him that the Vale family has harbored an unnatural pact to preserve their fields. The Synod will dispatch inquisitors with writs, flames, and an ecclesiastical ban."

"And the consequence?" Osric asked.

"The Church will conduct the slaughter for us," Rennick said with cold triumph. "The Count will not investigate Thornwall's ledgers while the bishops are burning heretics in the river parishes. House Vale will be dismantled by canon law, their land forfeit to the nearest solvent neighbor, and not a single lance from Thornwall will have struck a blow."

---

Hask Veyr sat in silence for a full minute, staring into his dark wine.

"It is an ingenious scheme," Hask said at last. "And I refuse to participate."

"Provide your reason," Osric demanded.

"Because it is the stratagem of a clerk who has never faced an unseen enemy in the dark," Hask answered, his rough voice cutting through the warmth of the room. "A stone causeway disappeared from the willow ford in September, and it was not washed away by rain. Nine experienced fighters entered an Ashgrove lane two weeks ago, and the single survivor has recited the identical account four times without contradiction: the ground swallowed them. Eleven captives walked out of my guarded holding with four sentries dead and not a dog barked."

He slammed his fist onto the table, rattling the pewter plates.

"I have commanded men in these mountains for eleven years. I have never encountered an enemy I could not locate with scouts and bleed with an axe. Since midsummer, four impossible catastrophes have struck this border. You wish to summon Church inquisitors into this valley? You will invite a wildfire into your own barn and discover too late that the fire has teeth."

"Then what is your counsel?" Osric asked, his eyes narrowing.

"Discover what is actually operating in this valley before you take another step," Hask stated flatly. "And if you cannot locate it, do not lay hands upon a power you do not comprehend in the foolish hope that it will serve your convenience."

Osric leaned back into his marten furs.

"That is the perspective of a mountain fighter with eighty horses," the baron remarked dismissively. "I govern an estate, a municipal highway, and thirty years of accumulated prestige. I have expended substantial treasure to secure this march, and I will not permit an anonymous rumor to strip my inheritance. We will initiate the legal suit against the Vale titles, we will prepare the manufactured correspondence, and we will place the evidence before Warden Selwyn. If one maneuver succeeds, I remain protected. If all three land, I am celebrated as the savior of the province."

---

The chamber grew still as the candles guttered in the draft.

Osric rubbed his temples, staring at the empty plates before turning to the room with the weary curiosity of a man wrestling with an intractable riddle.

"Answer me this, then," the baron murmured. "Both of you. If there is an unseen intelligence guiding this border—and I will grant the possibility, given what occurred at the ford—what does it desire? An adversary who can erase a stone road and eliminate eight fighters without burning a single sheaf of barley. What is his objective?"

"He seeks to protect House Vale," Rennick declared promptly.

"He seeks dominion over the river road," Tolan stated from the window embrasure.

"He has slain nobody who was not bearing a weapon against an innocent hearth," Hask observed, shaking his head. "He liberated eleven peasants and left forty bushels of grain untouched in the same yard. That is not the conduct of a bandit seeking tolls."

Osric sighed, spreading his hands.

"Then he is an irrational phantom pursuing an incomprehensible purpose," the baron concluded. "And I would give twenty marks to understand his design."

From the shadows behind the sideboard, the servant boy holding the earthenware flagon spoke into the silence:

"To be left alone."

---

The words hung in the warm air like a drawn blade.

Osric turned his head sharply, taking several seconds to locate the speaker. The boy stood six paces behind the high chair, his head bowed respectfully, his hands resting lightly upon the glazed clay of the jug.

"Repeat what you said," Osric commanded.

"To be left alone, my lord," the boy replied, his tone polite, unhurried, and entirely devoid of fear. "You inquired after his objective. That is what an adversary seeks who dismantles a river causeway and traps eight men in a stone lane without burning a barn or stealing a silver penny. He does not desire the turnpike, and he does not desire House Vale. He is executing none of this out of ambition. He is doing it because strangers keep intruding upon his ground."

Osric stared at the lad in utter astonishment.

Then the baron burst into a dry, genuine bark of laughter. He reached into his velvet pouch, retrieved a bright silver groat, and held it aloft.

"Four veteran counselors debating border strategy for two hours," Osric chuckled, "and it is the scullery boy with the wine flagon who articulates the plain truth. What is your name, lad?"

"Casian, my lord."

"Casian." Osric flipped the silver coin through the air; the boy caught it cleanly and slipped it into his sleeve with an awkward bow. "You have a sharp ear, Casian. Run down to the buttery, instruct them to stoke the scullery hearth, and then seek your pallet."

"Thank you, my lord," the boy said, backing through the door and closing the heavy oak panel behind him.

---

Osric shook his head, smiling down at his wine. "That is the wisdom of the common folk. A jug of southern red and a boy with sleeves past his knuckles."

"Arrest that boy," Tolan said.

The room froze.

Osric looked toward the window, his smile vanishing. "Tolan, what nonsense is this?"

"Arrest him immediately," Tolan repeated, his voice cold and flat as an iron ingot. "Seal the outer gates, muster the guard, and bring that boy back into this room under heavy irons."

"The lad has worked in this household since he was nine years old," Osric said with mild irritation. "Weyl engaged his mother for the dairy."

"No, he did not," Tolan replied, stepping away from the wall. "I have visited this keep four times in four years. I sleep in your stable and take my bread in your kitchen. An experienced scout memorizes every face under a roof where he rests his head. Your scullery employs a cook, two kitchen wenches, a one-armed carter, and a twelve-year-old boy who splits ash wood. There is no lad of sixteen with that face. There has never been such a boy in this house."

Weyl scrambled through his parchment rolls, his hands suddenly trembling.

"He... he arrived with the morning wine-dray," the steward stammered, his sallow face draining of color. "The carter brought him up from the town cellar."

"Which carter?" Tolan demanded, stepping directly in front of the table. "And where is the carter at this moment?"

Osric rose from his chair, his marten mantle falling open.

"Seal the courtyard!" the baron roared, the veneer of urbanity shattering into raw panic. "Rouse the guard! Search every cellar, loft, and stable! Find that boy!"

The alarm horn sounded across the inner ward within three minutes. Torches flared through the freezing drizzle; armored men searched the stables, the brewhouse, and the granaries.

They found nothing.

The wine barrels were accounted for, delivered by an honest drayman from Harrowmere who had carried freight for Thornwall for nine years. The drayman had accepted three groats from a pleasant youth in the town market to let him ride upon the tailboard, and the carter was now weeping in the guardroom, terrified that he would be hanged for treason.

The boy had vanished into the wet November night as completely as a drop of water falling into a well.

---

At two in the morning, Hask Veyr walked into the manor stable, where Tolan was tightening the girth on his dark mare by the light of a horn lantern.

"You will not clear the town gate tonight," Hask said, leaning against the timber partition. "Osric has posted thirty men on the perimeter."

"I am aware," Tolan answered without pausing in his work.

"Then why saddle the beast?"

"A prudent man keeps his mount prepared when the roof begins to creak," Tolan said.

Hask stood in the doorway, listening to the horses shifting in their stalls.

"He answered an idle question," Hask remarked slowly. "He stated a truth, accepted a silver coin, and departed through an unbolted postern. There were four seasoned men at that table, and none of us perceived anything extraordinary until he spoke."

"He said 'To be left alone,'" Tolan murmured, checking the stirrup leather.

"Yes."

"That was not an explanation of what the power wants," Tolan said, turning to face his chieftain. "It was an explicit warning directed at the four men in that chamber. And my lord gave him a silver groat for his courtesy."

Hask folded his arms, his rough features troubled. "You believe the boy was the power itself?"

"I know what the boy was carrying," Tolan answered grimly. "He stood six paces from our chairs while Rennick proposed delivering your eighty lances to the Synod as heretical sacrifices. He heard every maneuver, every forged letter, and every legal snare Osric intends to lay in the spring. He did not come to spy upon our provisions, Hask. He came to inspect the men who are preparing to die."

Tolan hung his lantern upon the timber peg and looked into the dark rafters.

"I have warned you since August that an impossible adversary was operating along this border," Tolan continued quietly. "Tonight, I looked him in the face. Nobody in that hall believes me, and by dawn Osric will convince himself the boy was merely an opportunistic thief. But I am keeping my horse saddled every night through the winter, and when the snow melts, I advise you to look very carefully at the road behind you."

Hask said nothing. He turned and walked out into the misty courtyard, leaving the mercenary captain alone with his saddled horse in the cold stillness of the night.
