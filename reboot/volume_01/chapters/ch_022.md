Tolan had been a soldier since he was fifteen and had kept a written account of things since he was twenty-two, which is not the same as being clever and is the reason he was still alive.

The account was three books. The first was a book of the things he had been told to do and had done, with the dates, in case a man ever said he had not done them. The second was a book of things that should not have been so. The third was new, and had started on the nineteenth of November at about eleven o'clock in the evening, and had one line in it.

He did not write in the third book that night. He sat in the stable until it began to get light, and then he went and stood in the yard and looked at the wall and did the thing he had been trained to do, which is to consider the ground.

---

What he had was this.

A causeway, gone out of a river in September, with two carts upright and the horses in a meadow and every trace off the road.

A wagon with nothing on it going down the Ashgrove road eleven times, and a fire square on a drover's track sited by a man who knew what he was doing.

Eleven people out of a barn above the ford, with four men dead on the shoulder and no sound at all, and the girl in the barn saying *Maren Vale sent us*.

Eight men into a lane at Ashgrove with a torch on the ground and no one in the lane, and the one who came back saying *there wasn't a soul in it.*

And a boy at a sideboard with too-long sleeves who had answered a question that no man at that table could answer, and had been given a coin for it, and had thanked a baron for his own money.

Tolan took these out and laid them in a row, the way you lay out a map, and looked for the line.

The line was not the Vales. That was the thing he kept arriving at and rejecting. A barony with four sons and a wall and eleven men and a name that men said without laughing — that was not a thing that could do any of it, and he had spent three months making the fit and it would not fit.

The line was not a hundred men either. A hundred men cannot take a causeway out of a river.

*It's one thing,* he thought, standing in the yard at dawn. *It's been one thing since August and it's been on the same road the whole time.*

---

He did the other thing in the afternoon, which was to go back to the barn.

It was two hours from the hall, above the ford on the west side, and the water was low and the channel where the causeway had been was a channel of stone and there was ice in the shallows. He had been there in September. He went again because he had a book and a book wants a second reading.

He walked it for four hours.

And this time he found the thing he had missed, because in September he had been looking at the water and this time he was looking at the road.

The road on the west bank ran up from the crossing and turned north toward the cut and there was a place a quarter mile along it where the bank was soft and the verge had been trampled by horses — not a cart, horses, six or eight of them, standing, for long enough to leave a mark.

He had looked at that in September and had thought: *a drover's camp.*

In November he crouched over it and looked at it properly and thought: *eight horses, standing, in the dark, on the night, and nobody dismounted.*

There was no fire. There was nothing at all in the middle of it, no gear, no pot, no blanket — because he had looked for a camp in September and had found a fire square four miles off and had made it fit.

He stood up.

"They weren't a camp," he said, out loud. "They were a party that stopped here and waited and then went up to that barn, and they came down off the cut and they went back up the cut, and not one of them left a fire."

He walked the verge for another half mile and found two things. One was a boot-heel in the soft ground that was a woman's boot-heel and not a man's. The other was a strip of linen, six inches long, folded and tied, the way a thing is tied over a wound.

He put the linen in his coat.

*That's the girl off the uplands,* he thought. *The one who walks in the dark. Because a man would have ridden and she walked and a man in boots does not leave a heel like that.*

---

---

There was a thing he did that afternoon which he did not write down and did not tell anybody about and thought about for the rest of his life.

He rode to Fenwick.

Not to the gate — to the mill on the lower field, where a woman was stacking hurdles, and he sat on his horse forty feet off and looked at her until she noticed him, and then he got down and walked over and took his hat off.

"My name is Tolan and I'm a captain in the upper Veyr."

The woman was about thirty and had a bar of iron in her hand and did not put it down.

"I know the name."

"I'm not here about anything," said Tolan. "I'd like to ask you one question and then I'll go, and if you'd rather I went now I'll go now."

"Ask it."

"Has anybody come to this barony since October and asked you for a thing?" He said it carefully. "Not a miller. Not a carrier. Anybody. A boy, a woman, a man with a slate. Anybody who asked a question that wasn't about sheep."

The woman regarded him for a while.

"Why?"

"Because I'm trying to work out whether the thing that happened to eleven people in a barn is a thing that happens in one place or a thing that happens everywhere," said Tolan, "and I have no way of finding out except by asking, and I'd rather ask a woman with an iron bar in her hand than a man in a hall."

She put the bar down.

"There's a girl off the upland comes through this time of year with a pack," she said. "She's come four times since the harvest and she never used to come at all." She straightened. "And there's a man from the mill at Vale who came in October about a levy and was polite about it and left when I said I'd nothing to say."

"Thank you."

"You'll not be back."

"No," said Tolan. "I'll not be back. But there'll be others, and if a girl off the upland comes again, tell her a captain in the upper Veyr sat on a horse in your lower field in November and asked after her, and that he took his hat off first."

He rode away and did not look back, and he knew, absolutely, that he had just done a thing that could not be undone, and that he had done it deliberately, and that the reason he had done it was that he was not willing to be a man who only wrote things down.

He did not go to the hall. He went down to the ford and sat on the wall of the mill there and worked out what he was going to do, and it took him an hour and it was the hardest hour of his life.

He was a soldier. That was the whole of him and it had been since he was fifteen. He had served three men and had been loyal to all three and had never once, in twenty years, been disloyal in a way that anybody could have named. When a thing was wrong he said it in the room. When he had said it and been overruled he did the thing he was told, in the way it was supposed to be done, and if it went wrong he wrote it down.

And that had worked for twenty years and he had no idea what to do now, because the thing that was wrong was not a plan. It was the shape of the world.

*If I tell them there's one thing behind all of it,* he thought, *they'll laugh, and then in the spring they'll hand a hundred of us to a Church and it'll not matter.*

*If I tell them there's a boy in the Vale household, they'll ride down there with forty men and burn it, and I have met exactly one person in this county who could do the things in my first book, and he is not a boy of sixteen with too-long sleeves, and if I am wrong about that then eleven people who were in a barn are dead for nothing and the man who took them out will come for us in a way that none of us will see.*

*And if I say nothing at all, then I am a man with a book who says nothing, and there is no version of that in which I come out of the spring with my name.*

He sat on the mill wall until the light went.

---

Then he did the thing he had actually decided an hour earlier and had been walking round, which was to write it all down and put it somewhere.

Because a soldier who says a thing in a room and is laughed at has said it and it is gone. A soldier who wrote it down and put the paper where it can be found has said it in a way that a room cannot laugh at.

He went back to the hall and up to the room they had given him and he took the third book and he wrote for two hours.

*A hundred and six men in the upper Veyr. No title. The levy through Thornwall is dead and there will be no buying after Christmas.*

*The Ford. The causeway went out on the second of September. Two carts upright, horses sound, traces off the road. The weather did not do it.*

*The barn. Eleven taken on the twentieth of October. Eleven out on the thirty-first. Four men dead on the shoulder. No sound. Nobody in that valley heard anything at all. The people were told: Maren Vale sent us.*

*The lane at Ashgrove. Eighth of November. Nine went in. Eight did not come out. No sound. The granary was not burned.*

*The council at Thornwall, nineteenth of November. Osric of Thornwall proposes: (1) a deed against the eastern strip in the spring, (2) forged letters dated in '16 giving the valley to the Count, (3) a report to Warden Selwyn at Harrowmere that the eastern marches harbour the old thing, so that the Church does the work. I said we should find out what it is first. I was overruled.*

*A boy came up to the hall with the wine. He poured for the table and heard all of it. He answered the question about what the thing wants. He said: to be left alone. My lord gave him a coin and sent him to bed. There is no boy in that kitchen. The carter was paid four marks to let him ride up.*

*Conclusion. There is one thing in the eastern marches and it has been there since August and it is not a warlord. Everything that has been done to us is a reply. And in the spring we are going to be given to the Church by the man who pays us.*

He read it back.

Then he wrote the last line, which he knew was the real reason he had been writing for two hours, and it said:

*If this is read by anybody who was not in that room, then I am dead or gone, and I would like it known that I asked for twenty men on that barn in October, and for the yard to be sealed on the nineteenth, and that both times I was told no by men who were certain.*

---

---

He gave the book to Ferrin.

That was the other thing, and it was not sentiment, and he was rather proud of how little sentiment was in it. Ferrin was a headman. A headman is a man in a village who holds a thing that other men do not, and he had been in a barn for four days and had come out of it with his granddaughter and had not once, in the telling, said anything about the four men on the shoulder.

Tolan rode to Lowmere in the third week and found him rebuilding a barn with nine men and a boy of fifteen with a foot wrapped in cloth, and took him aside, and gave him the second book — the one with the fire square and the ford and the lane in it — tied in oilcloth.

"I'm not asking you to do anything with it," he said. "I'm asking you to keep it. If nothing happens in a year, burn it. If a thing happens — if a Warden comes through this county with a writ, or if a baron from the south comes asking about a barn, or if a man in a grey coat ever comes to this village — give it to him."

Ferrin turned the package over.

"Why me?"

"Because you were in the barn and I was not," said Tolan. "Everything in that book is a thing I worked out from the outside. You've the inside of one of them and it's the only one anybody's got."

"I'm a farmer."

"You're a man who sat in a dark room with ten other people for four days and worked out that whoever came for you would not be the Count," said Tolan. "That's better than anything in the book."

He did not leave that night. He went down to supper and sat with the household and ate, and Osric came in and was courteous to everybody, and Weyl read out a list of the next day's business, and it was an entirely ordinary evening in a barony with a good road.

And at the end of it, Osric said: "Tolan. A word."

They went out into the passage.

"I've been thinking about what you said," said Osric.

"My lord."

"You believe there is one thing in these marches."

"I do."

"Then do me the courtesy of assuming I am not a fool," said Osric, "and answer me honestly. If there is one thing, and if it has been replying to us since August and not the other way round — what would you have me do differently?"

Tolan considered the question for a long time, because it was an honest one and he had not expected it.

"I'd have you not give it a name," he said. "You're going to hand a Warden a report in the spring. The moment you do that, the thing in the marches stops being something we don't understand and becomes something the Church is hunting, and the Church will hunt it in this county with fires and it will not find it, and it will burn eleven wrong houses a year for four years." He met Osric's eye. "I'd have you go to Harven yourself in the spring and tell the Count the truth. That there's a hundred and six men in the upper Veyr and a man in this hall that paid them, and that you'd like to be allowed to put it right. It'd cost you the strip and your name and half your standing, and in ten years you'd be a baron with a road and a clean house."

Osric looked at him.

"You'd have me confess."

"I'd have you be the man who found a warlord in the hills and dealt with him," said Tolan. "It's the same story. It's just the version where you're in it."

Osric was quiet for a while.

"That," he said at last, "is a great deal better advice than I have had from anybody with a title, and I am not going to take it."

"I know."

"Tell me you know why."

"Because it costs you the strip and your name and half your standing," said Tolan, "and a man of fifty-five who has been careful for thirty years will not spend thirty years in one spring. And because you think a thing that has killed eight men quietly might be handled by a Church that burns people loudly." He put his hands behind his back. "And because you are not a wicked man, my lord, and that is the whole difficulty. A wicked man would have done this in '16 and been finished. You have been careful and you have been fair and you are going to do a very great deal of harm, and there is no room in your head for the possibility that it is the carefulness that got you here."

Osric stood in the passage of his own house.

"You are the best man in my service," he said.

"I know."

"And you are going to leave."

"I'm going to be here in the spring," said Tolan, "and I'm going to do what I'm told while it's a thing a soldier can do. And on the day it stops being that, I'll be gone, and I'll not be quiet about why."

"That's a threat."

"It's a forecast," said Tolan. "I'm not a man who threatens. I'm a man who writes things down."

---

He went up to his room and put the third book in the lining of his saddlebag, which was where he kept the other two, and then he went down to the stable and checked his horse, which he did every night, and which was the only lie he told in the whole of that autumn.

The horse was not a horse he had been given. It was a horse he had bought in September with his own money, from a man in Harrowmere, and it was a good one, and it was stabled at a farm a mile from the hall and not at the hall at all.

He had bought it in the week after the barn.

He had told himself at the time that a soldier keeps a spare horse. He had known then that it was not true.
