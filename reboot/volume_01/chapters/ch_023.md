The carrying business opened on the first of December and it was called Corran and Son, which was a lie in two directions at once and Maeve had insisted on it.

"A woman trading under her own name in this county is a woman men will ask about," she said. "A woman trading under her dead husband's name is a business. And the *Son* will make every man in Harrowmere assume there is a man somewhere doing the work, and they will not ask where he is, and I will be the one they deal with and I will be dull about it."

It took eleven days and four marks and a licence, and at the end of it there was a slate on the mill wall and a cart with a name on the side of it and a list of eleven carriers on the cut who would take a load for a fee.

The first month it moved two hundred bushels and made nine marks.

"That's a bad business," said Harn, who was a carrier and knew.

"It's a dull business," said Maeve. "Dull's the point. A bad business is a thing men pity. A good business is a thing men watch."

---

Corin's part was the roads.

That was the thing he had wanted since October and it took him four weeks and it cost almost nothing and it was, he thought, the best work he had ever done in his life.

Here is what he did. He took a map of the eastern marches and he marked every place a man could put a cart or a fire and not be seen from a village: the byre walls, the old sheep folds, the three fords, the two bridges, the eleven barns that stood empty between September and May. Then he marked every place on the Ashgrove road and the drove and the cut where a body of men would have to stop.

Then he went out and visited them, one at a time, on foot, with a boy carrying a bag of tools.

He did not build anything. He did not post anybody. He went to a sheep fold above the Ashgrove road and he repaired the wall of it — two days' work, done properly, so that it was a good fold again, and if a man asked he would have said that a good fold is a good fold.

And in the wall of it he left a place where a man could lie down out of the wind and see the road for a quarter of a mile without being seen.

"Master," said the boy, the fourth time.

"Ask it."

"What's the hole for?"

"It's for sheep," said Corin.

"There's no sheep up here."

"There will be," said Corin Ahl. "In about a year. And when there are, there'll be a boy whose job it is to sit in it in the rain, and he'll be warmer than he would have been, and he'll thank nobody and I'll not be there."

The boy thought about this for a mile.

"Are you making the whole county into a thing?" he said.

"No," said Corin. "I'm making eleven places in it worth being. The county is not my business."

---

---

The eleven places took four weeks and cost nine marks and a boy's wages, and on the twenty-first of December the last of them was done.

It was a byre above the Harrow road, on Vale land, with a stone wall on the north and a gap in it the width of a cart, and Corin had been watching that byre since September because it was the only roof between the ridge and the ford.

"Who owns it?" he asked the tenant.

"Nobody. It's the baron's and it's empty since the wet."

"Then it's mine," said Corin, "and I'll have a floor in it and a good door and a place in the wall where a man can stand."

"Stand for what?"

"Stand for a long time," said Corin Ahl, "and don't ask, and if anybody asks you, say the Vale has put a floor in the byre because a floor in a byre is a sensible thing and there's no man alive who'll argue with that."

The tenant, who was nineteen and had a wife and no particular opinion about byres, said that it was a sensible thing and went back to his pigs.

It was, Corin thought, the finest piece of work he had ever done and nobody would ever know it existed, and there was, he found, a particular kind of satisfaction in that which he had not known was available to him.

*Twenty men on a battlefield can see a wall go up,* he thought. *One man alone gets to see a hole in a hedge and know what it's for.*

---

Kael came on the ninth of December.Kael came on the ninth of December.

He was twenty-two and he was the best scout in the upland and he had been used for four years by men who did not know what to do with him, and Corin had been watching him since September.

"Your master won't like it," said Corin, in the yard.

"He's not my master. He's my uncle and he's got two hundred sheep and he'll not notice I've gone till lambing." Kael put his bag down. "I've been on the cut four times this autumn for a girl of nineteen and I know the ground better than anyone alive except her, and I've had exactly one interesting month in four years, and I'd like another one."

"You'll not be told much."

"I know. The girl told me."

"She told you what?"

"She told me," said Kael, "that she was told she'd be told nothing and that she was told the whole of it inside a fortnight because she asked in a way that made a man think." He looked at Corin steadily. "So I'll ask. What am I?"

The yard was quiet.

"You're the second man on the Ashgrove road," said Corin, "and you'll ride it at night and you'll learn every fold and every byre and every man's dog between here and the cut, and you'll be paid eleven marks a quarter, and in a year you'll know this county better than any man in it including me."

"And if a thing comes?"

"Then you'll send a boy to Watt at Ashgrove and you'll ride to me and you'll not do anything clever."

"And if I can help a thing that's happening as it happens?"

"Then you'll use your judgment," said Corin, "and I'll tell you now that judgment is the word a man uses when he's decided to do something and would like it not to be his fault."

Kael picked his bag back up.

"I'll take the words and the eleven marks," he said, "and I'd like to start tonight."

Vesna was on the cut for eleven days in the second half of December and came back on the twenty-eighth with a boot she had mended herself and a thing she had not expected.

"I walked all nineteen miles," she said, in the dairy, with her hands round a cup. "All of it. Both sides of the wall, twice, and I've drawn it and I'll have a copy made so that there's one that isn't in my head.

"And I found eleven of those gates. Not four. Eleven. In a line, along the top, all of them above the line where a man would put a road, and all of them built in the same way, and the oldest one is old enough that the posts have gone soft in the middle and the newest one is — " She stopped.

"Say it."

"The newest one has been mended," said Vesna. "Not by me. Not this year. There's a post at the sixth gate that's been replaced and the wood in it is not the wood in the others, and there's a stone kicked up at the foot of it that's been put back."

The dairy was quiet.

"How long ago?"

"Two years," said Vesna. "Maybe three. Not more."

Casian sat back.

*Argius. The Fenwick stone was moved two or three seasons ago. There were oxen that would not pass a stone in the spring.*

"Say the thing you are thinking," said Argius.

*Somebody is maintaining the old crossings and moving the old stones, and it is not me, and it is not Hask, and it is not the Church.*

"Yes."

*And whoever it is has been at it for at least three years and nobody in this county has noticed, and I have noticed because a girl from the upland walked nineteen miles in December.*

"Yes." A pause. "That is the second thing in this volume that is not yours and is not the plot. The first was a stone in a field. This is a man walking a wall."

*Should I follow it?*

"No," said Argius. "You should write it down and put it in the wall with the rest, and you should leave it alone with the entire strength of your considerable will, because the moment you go looking for it you will find it, and whatever you find will take a year, and in the spring a Church is going to burn your family's name."

Casian came to the mill in the second week of December to sign a paper, which was the dullest hour of his life and the most satisfying.

There was a book. It was a good book, kept in Maeve's square hand, and it said what a business says: a load of barley from Fenwick to Harrowmere, three marks; a load of hurdles from the upland to Vale, four; a carrying fee from the cut to the cut, eleven. It was dull and it was true and the totals were, at the end of four weeks, nine marks.

"Nine marks," said Casian.

"Nine marks," said Maeve, "and it cost you eleven to make it, and there's a hundred men in this county who'd say you were a fool."

"I'd like to be a fool in a book."

"You'll be a fool in a book for a year," said Maeve, "and in the second year a man will ask who carries his grain and they'll say Corran and Son and he'll not ask again, and in the third year you'll be a man who carries grain and there'll be no story in it at all, and *that* is the thing we're making. Not the nine marks. The year three."

Casian turned the page.

"You've written the mill's levy in it."

"I've written everything in it," said Maeve. "That's the whole point of a book. A man keeps a secret book and it's found and it's a scandal. A man keeps a business book and writes his sins in the same page as his barley and there's a hundred books like it in a county and no man alive would read past the fourth page."

She took it back and put it on the shelf with the other seven.

"That's the last thing I'll teach you," said Maeve Corran.

"What's the first and I'll know what I've learned?"

"The first thing I ever said to you," said Maeve, "was that there's three sorts of people come into a mill. You've been the third sort for four months and you're now the second sort in a good coat, and there's no harm in it and I'd like you to know it's a downgrade."

They rode the road together that night, and Kael turned out to be better than Corin had thought, which was saying something.

He did the thing that only two men in the county could do, which Corin had seen once before and had not understood: he read a road as though it were a room. He did not look at where a man could hide. He looked at where a man would have to be, given a purpose.

"There," he said, at a place above the Harrowmere cut where a track came down to join the road. "That's not a gate and it's not a fold and it's not a way to anything. Somebody made that for crossing this road at speed and leaving it unseen, and they made it a long time ago."

Corin looked at it.

"How do you know it's old?"

"Because it's been used by sheep for forty years and it's still the shape of it," said Kael. "A man makes a thing for men and then sheep walk on it for forty years and it stays a thing for men. That's the whole of a scouting trade and it took me six years to learn."

*He's not guessing,* Corin thought. *He's never once guessed and he's the third person this autumn to say a thing about this county that I would not have seen.*

*The girl reads the ground. The boy reads a county. And I have been standing at a gate for thirty-five years.*

---

Casian came down to the guardroom on the fourteenth, which he did once a week now, with a slate of names and a purse that had got used to being thin.

"How many?"

"Twenty," said Corin. "Harn and his mother and Kael and two boys at Ashgrove and three carriers on the cut and eleven names Ansel bought, if you count them as ours, and I don't."

"Don't count them."

"Then nine, and I've been trying to make it eleven all month and it won't go." Corin rubbed his face. "There's a thing I want to say and it'll sound like a complaint and it's not.

"Since October I've had a roof over a fold and a boy on a road and a business with a name on it, and I have not been able to say one word about what any of it is for. Not to Jory. Not to my wife. Not to a man I've known since '06. And I've got good at it — I've got *too* good at it, and that's the thing I've come to say. A man who's too good at saying nothing starts doing things because they were in the slate and not because they matter, and I've watched that happen to better men than me in a yard thirty-five years."

Casian sat down on the bench.

"Say what you'd like."

"One meeting a quarter," said Corin. "You, me, the miller and the girl. Not a thing with a map on it. A room where a man can say a name and be told whether it's right."

"That's a great deal more dangerous than a slate."

"It's a great deal more dangerous than a slate," Corin agreed, "and I've been doing this since September and I'd rather be hung in a room where I've been told the truth than be useful in a room where I've been told a slate."

On the twentieth of December the eastern strip moved, and Corin heard about it from a carter before he heard about it from anybody official.

"There's a finding filed at Harven," said the carter, over a cup in the guardroom, "against four parcels on the strip, and it's filed by a man from the Exchequer who's never set foot in this county, and every man on the Harrow road is talking about it."

"Filed by who for who?"

"That's the thing," said the carter. "Nobody knows, and I've asked four men who'd know, and they all say the same, which is that it's a paper with no name on it and a fee paid in coin."

Corin went up to the house and told the baron, and Edric listened and then sat back and said, "How long?"

"Two years, my lord. A finding takes two years and it wants a hearing and a hearing wants a man to travel to Harven and stay there at his own cost."

"Then I'll go to Harven in the spring," said Edric Vale, "and I'll be there for the hearing and I'll be there for the Count and I'll be there in the same month as a duke's daughter's report, and there's three things arriving in one place in one season and I've been a baron for twenty-two years and I can tell you what that is." He looked up. "That's a man who has decided to be finished with me."

He reached for his pen.

"Fetch my eldest," he said. "And Corin — say nothing to the boy until I've spoken to Aldric. I'd like one of my sons to hear a thing from me before he works it out for himself."

Corin went out and did not say that the boy had known for eleven days, because the boy had told him so.

He went home at midnight and Ivet was up.

"There's a name for what you're doing now," she said. "The girls asked me at supper what you do at the barracks and I said you do the walls and I've been sitting here since and I've been ashamed of it."

Corin sat down heavily.

"It's the first true thing I've said to them in ten years," he said, "and I'd like to be able to tell them the real one."

"Then tell them a true thing that isn't the real one, and make it one they can keep." Ivet put a cup in front of him. "You've never once lied to me and I'd rather you didn't start. But our daughters are fourteen and eleven and there is a thing in this county that killed a man in a lane, and I've watched you all autumn getting quieter and happier at the same time, and I know what that is, because I watched my father do it in the bad winter."

"What did he do?"

"He was a shepherd and he carried a knife and he lived to be seventy," said Ivet, "and when it got bad he walked the line every night to make sure the gates were shut, and he came home and slept. That's a true thing and it's all of it and it's enough for two girls."

Corin looked into his cup.

"There's a thing I've been meaning to ask you since '12," he said.

"Ask it."

"Why did you say I was a big man in a small place?"

Ivet sat down opposite him.

"Because you are," she said, "and because when a woman marries a big man in a small place she has to say it once and make sure he hears it, and then she has to decide whether she'll live with what he does about it." She reached across and turned his cup round. "I said it in '12 and you didn't hear it and I decided to live with it, and I have been very happy for six years, and I'd not change the first of it."

"And now?"

"Now there's a girl at Fenwick with a split boot and a man in Thornwall who's going to give a Church a report in the spring," said Ivet, "and I've been listening at doors all November and I'd like you to notice I've not asked you a single question I shouldn't."

Corin looked at his wife for a long moment.

"Two promises," he said. "The first is that I'll tell you when it's dangerous, and not before, because you'll not spend a winter frightened of a thing that's three months away."

"And the second?"

"The second is that when it's bad I'll send a boy to this house before I send one to the baron," said Corin, "and you'll take the girls to Fenwick and you'll not wait to know why."
