The freight business commenced operations on the first of December under the registered trading title of *Corran and Son*—an arrangement that represented a deliberate deception in two directions at once, and one upon which Maeve had stubbornly insisted.

"A solitary widow trading under her own name in this march invites endless speculation," she had declared in the mill office. "A firm bearing her late husband's name is regarded as an established municipal fixture. And the word *Son* persuades every merchant in Harrowmere that some competent man is laboring in the yard, so they never inquire after him. I will conduct the transactions from this desk, and I will be unrelentingly tedious about every bushel."

The establishment required eleven days, four silver marks in registration dues, and a formal carrier's license issued by the peace clerk at Harrowmere. By the second week of the month, a painted wooden sign hung beside the millrace, a stout four-horse dray stood in the yard bearing the firm's brand, and eleven independent teamsters along the cut had agreed to haul freight for standard wages.

In its first four weeks of operation, the enterprise transported two hundred bushels of grain and yielded a modest profit of nine silver marks.

"That is a dismal margin for thirty days of mud," remarked Harn Rill, who understood freight carriage from the keel up.

"It is an inconspicuous margin," Maeve countered, dipping her goose quill. "Inconspicuous is the entire purpose. A failing enterprise invites pity; a lucrative enterprise attracts greedy eyes. A dull business paying its road tolls on Michaelmas is an enterprise nobody ever bothers to audit."

---

Corin Ahl's assignment was the terrain.

It was the specific responsibility he had sought since mid-October, requiring four weeks of arduous footwork, costing virtually nothing, and representing what he privately considered the finest craftsmanship of his life.

He began with an sheepskin map of the eastern marches, marking every feature where a cart, a picket of mounts, or an armed detachment could halt without being observed from a village church tower: ancient drystone sheepfolds, abandoned lime-kilns, three fords, two timber bridges, and eleven stone byres that stood empty between harvest and spring shearing. Then he identified every bottleneck along the Ashgrove turnpike, the high drove, and the willow cut where a marching column would be forced to deploy into single file.

He visited each location on foot, accompanied by an apprentice lad carrying a basket of mason's hammers, iron wedges, and lime mortar.

He erected no new fortifications; he posted no sentries. When he reached an overgrown sheepfold overlooking the sunken curve of the Ashgrove road, he spent two full days rebuilding the fallen limestone wall. He worked methodically, bedding the fieldstones in dry turf so that any passerby would see only an honest armorer repairing pasture boundaries on his lord's estate.

Yet in the northern angle of the wall, shielded beneath a heavy overhang of elder scrub, he left a low recess where a man could lie wrapped in a wool blanket, observe the turnpike for a quarter of a mile in both directions, and remain completely invisible from the ditch below.

"Master," the boy inquired on their fourth afternoon, wiping sweat from his forehead.

"Speak it, lad."

"What is the opening in the stones for?"

"It is for sheep," Corin replied, tapping a keystone into place with his wooden mallet.

"No sheep ever climbed into elder bushes, master."

"They will," Corin Ahl answered calmly. "In approximately twelve months. And when they do, some young carter's lad will have the assignment of sitting in that recess through the November drizzle. He will be considerably warmer than he would have been on the open bank, he will thank nobody for the shelter, and I will not be present to receive his gratitude."

The apprentice pondered the explanation for half a mile of their homeward walk.

"Are you turning the entire county into an armed trap?" the boy asked.

"No," Corin said. "I am preparing eleven specific positions where an honest man can stand without being cut down from behind. The rest of the province is not my concern."

---

The preparation of the eleven tactical positions occupied four weeks, expending nine marks of Casian's silver and the apprentice's weekly board. By the twenty-first of December, the final redoubt was completed.

It was an ancient field byre perched above the Harrow road on Vale land, possessing solid limestone walls to the north and a timber doorway the width of a hay-wain. Corin had monitored the structure since September, recognizing it as the sole weather-proof refuge between the rocky ridge and the river crossing.

"Who holds the tenancy?" he asked the young peasant tending swine near the hedge.

"Nobody, sir. It belongs to the baron, and it has stood vacant since the wet spring."

"Then it belongs to the guardroom," Corin told him. "I intend to lay an oak plank floor, hang a stout door with an interior draw-bar, and leave an embrasure in the masonry where a watcher can stand out of the wind."

"Stand for what purpose, master?"

"To stand for a very long interval without catching lung-rot," Corin said. "Keep your tongue quiet, and if anyone inquires, inform them that House Vale is improving its outbuildings because a dry floor preserves timber. No man in this valley will debate the wisdom of a dry floor."

The young swineherd nodded in incurious agreement and returned to his herd.

Corin stood in the shadow of the byre, running his rough palm over the fresh oak timbers. There was an intense, quiet gratification in the labor. Twenty knights on a tournament ground could witness an iron palisade being raised with blaring trumpets; a solitary armsman working in the drizzle possessed the deeper privilege of knowing that a gap in an elder hedge would determine the survival of a barony when the lances charged.

---

Kael arrived on the ninth of December.

He was twenty-two years old, the most perceptive tracker in the upland hills, and had spent four years performing thankless errands for pastoral kin who possessed no conception of his talent. Corin had observed him since the harvest.

"Your uncle will raise an outcry," Corin remarked, leaning against the timber rail in the manor yard.

"My uncle possesses two hundred mountain sheep and six sons," Kael replied, dropping his hemp pack into the dirt. "He will not notice my absence until the spring lambing. I have scouted the high cut four times this autumn for Vesna Orrow, and I understand this terrain better than any man living, save her. I have experienced exactly one purposeful month in four years, and I desire another."

"You will be told very little."

"I am aware," Kael said. "The girl warned me."

"What did she tell you?"

"She told me," Kael said with a faint, wry grin, "that you would promise to tell me nothing, and that within a fortnight I would understand the entire design simply because I observed where the grain wagons halted. What is my assignment?"

"You are the second scout on the Ashgrove highway," Corin informed him. "You will patrol the road by night. You will memorize every limestone fold, every field byre, and the bark of every tenant's sheep-dog between here and the willow cut. You will receive eleven marks a quarter from Master Casian's hand, and by next winter you will know this province better than any living soul, including myself."

"And when an enemy column appears?"

"You will dispatch a runner to Watt at the Ashgrove smithy, you will ride directly to my guardroom, and you will refrain from attempting any clever maneuvers."

"And if an opportunity arises to intervene while the blow is falling?"

"You will exercise your tactical judgment," Corin said sternly. "And I will remind you that *judgment* is the term a soldier employs when he has resolved upon a dangerous gamble and desires to avoid the gallows if it miscarries."

Kael slung his bag over his shoulder. "I accept the wage and the admonition," he said. "I ride tonight."

---

Vesna Orrow spent eleven days traversing the high cut during the latter half of December, returning to the old dairy on the twenty-eighth with an icy slush crusting her wool leggings and news that altered the perimeter.

"I walked all nineteen miles of the escarpment," she reported, cradling a mug of steaming cider between her chapped hands. "Both flanks of the boundary wall, twice over. I have committed every rock-fall to parchment so that a record exists beyond my memory.

"And I located eleven of the ancient gates along the crest. Not four, Casian. Eleven. Positioned in a regular chain along the summit, elevated far above the drovers' trails, and constructed according to an identical masonry technique. The oldest gates have rotted until the oak lintels are soft as cheese. Yet the sixth gate..." She paused, her dark eyes narrowing.

"What of the sixth gate, Vesna?" Casian asked.

"The sixth gate was repaired," she said, her voice dropping. "Not by my hand, and not this season. An oak post has been renewed within the past two winters, using timber felled elsewhere, and a displaced foundation stone at the threshold has been carefully re-mortared."

The room grew exceedingly still.

"How long ago?" Casian asked.

"Two seasons," Vesna answered. "Perhaps three. Certainly no longer."

Casian leaned back against the timber partition, his face impassive in the candlelight, though his eyes narrowed with a sudden, razor-sharp focus. Corin had watched men reckon numbers for eighteen years; this was the look a captain wore when he realized a third army had walked onto the field without drumming.

"Leave it alone," Casian said quietly, looking directly at Vesna.

"Leave it?" Vesna frowned, her mug suspended. "Casian, someone is maintaining the high wall. Someone with a stonemason's chisel who knows the old crossings."

"And that someone has been doing so for three years without troubling our sheep," Casian said, his voice flat with absolute discipline. "We do not chase ghosts in the heather while Thornwall is buying the roads under our boots. Mark the coordinate on your parchment, Vesna, and walk past it. The moment we start hunting mysteries in the high rocks, Osric catches our folk in the open."

Corin nodded in silent approval. It was the hardest thing for a young commander to learn: knowing which battle to ignore.

---

Casian visited the water-mill in the second week of December to examine the accounts—an hour he described as thoroughly monotonous and deeply comforting.

The ledger was immaculate, scribed in Maeve's dense, upright hand: five cartloads of barley transported from Fenwick to the Harrowmere wharf, three marks; two dray-loads of hurdles carried from the upland to Vale, four marks; carriage surcharges settled at the turnpike barrier, eleven marks. It was tedious, legal, and pedestrian. At the foot of the column, the net surplus amounted to precisely nine silver marks.

"Nine marks," Casian observed, turning the sheepskin page.

"Nine marks," Maeve confirmed, adjusting her steel-rimmed spectacles. "And it required eleven marks of expenditure to secure the trade. Half the merchants in Thornwall would declare you an incompetent novice."

"I am delighted to appear incompetent in a registered ledger."

"You will be regarded as a novice for twelve months," Maeve told him. "In the second year, the carters will refer to Corran and Son as a matter of habit and cease asking who owns the drays. By the third year, you will simply be the quiet lad who transports valley rye, and not a soul in this province will imagine there is a story behind the wagons. That is the architecture we are constructing. Not nine marks of silver today, but invisibility in year three."

She closed the ledger and returned it to the shelf.

"You have included the old surcharge entries?" Casian asked.

"Every penny," Maeve replied. "That is the supreme art of the craft. A foolish conspirator maintains a secret diary; when it is discovered, he is hanged. A wise administrator enters his illicit expenditures between bags of flour and horse-shoes. There are fifty ledgers like this across the county, and no auditor alive possesses the fortitude to read past the fifth page of oats."

---

That night, Corin Ahl rode the northern perimeter with Kael, discovering that the young upland scout possessed a rare, intuitive mastery of terrain.

Kael did not merely scan the hedges for concealed archers; he interpreted the roadway as a living mechanism.

"Observe the track there," Kael said, reining in his gelding where a sunken path converged upon the highway. "That is neither a sheep trail nor a drover's drove. Someone cut that passage forty years ago to allow four horses to cross the turnpike at speed and vanish into the alder swamps without leaving a trace upon the gravel."

Corin studied the gap in the brush. "How do you know it is forty years old?"

"Because sheep have trodden the ruts for four decades and the underlying geometry remains intact," Kael explained. "A soldier cuts a road for military transit, the livestock inherit the path, but the tactical line endures. It required six years on the moor for me to learn to see the difference."

Corin watched the young scout's profile in the moonlight.

*He does not guess,* Corin reflected. *He reads the earth with the certainty of a scholar. Vesna reads the winds, the boy reads the province, and I have stood at an iron gate for eighteen years believing an armorer's duty was simply to sharpen lances.*

---

On the fourteenth of December, Casian joined Corin in the guardroom, bringing a slate of names and an accounting of the seasonal treasury.

"What is our muster?" Casian asked, seating himself on the bench.

"Twenty men and women," Corin answered, checking his tallies. "Harn, his mother, Kael, two carters at Ashgrove, three teamsters on the cut, and the eleven tenants whose mortgages Ansel purchased—if you consider them reliable."

"Do not count the debtors as soldiers," Casian said.

"Then nine dependable souls," Corin said, rubbing his face with calloused hands. "And there is a matter I must speak aloud.

"Since October, I have fortified eleven field byres, engaged an upland scout, and backed a commercial freight firm, and I have been forbidden to speak a syllable of explanation to any human being. Not to Jory. Not to my wife. Not to armsmen I have drilled for eighteen years. I have become remarkably skilled at silence—frighteningly skilled. And when an old soldier grows too comfortable keeping secrets, he begins executing commands simply because they appear upon a slate, rather than because they preserve lives. I have watched that disease rot better men than myself."

Casian looked at him with profound respect. "What do you propose, Corin?"

"A quarterly conference," Corin insisted. "You, myself, Maeve, and the girl. No elaborate maps. Simply a secure room where an armsman can speak a name aloud and be told whether his judgment was true."

"A gathering of four principals is considerably more hazardous than an anonymous slate," Casian cautioned.

"It is hazardous," Corin agreed. "Yet I have served House Vale for eighteen years, Casian, and I would far rather face a hangman's rope having acted with full understanding than remain a blind instrument carrying out an unexamined slate."

Casian was silent for a long moment before nodding. "You will have your council at the spring equinox."

---

On the twentieth of December, Baron Osric's opening maneuver struck the valley, delivered by a traveling drayman before the official bailiffs could arrive.

"A legal finding has been lodged at Harven," the carter reported in the guardroom over small beer. "Directed against four parcels along the eastern strip of Vale land, filed by an exchequer assessor on behalf of an anonymous petitioner."

Corin climbed to the manor hall immediately to report the development to Baron Edric.

The old lord listened in silence, setting down his goose quill.

"What is the statutory timeline?" Edric asked.

"A formal hearing requires two years, my lord," Corin replied. "And it obliges the defendant to travel to the ducal court in Harven and maintain legal counsel at his own expense."

"Then I will ride to Harven at the spring assize," Edric Vale said with grim resolve. "I will answer the summons in the identical season that Lady Seraine's report reaches the council. Three separate assaults converging upon my estate in one spring. That is the hand of an adversary who has resolved to extinguish our lineage."

The baron reached for fresh parchment.

"Summon my eldest son," Edric commanded. "And Corin—say nothing to young Casian until I have conferred with Aldric. I wish my heir to hear this peril from my own lips before the household is alarmed."

Corin bowed and departed, refraining from mentioning that the fourth son had foreseen the maneuver eleven days prior.

---

Corin returned to his cottage at midnight to find his wife, Ivet, sitting beside the dying peat fire, mending an apron.

"There is a name for your night work now," she said quietly. "The girls inquired at supper why their father spends his nights patrolling empty ditches, and I told them you were inspecting the estate walls. I have sat here since dusk feeling the falsehood burn in my throat."

Corin took the stool opposite her, sighing heavily.

"It was the nearest truth I could offer them, Ivet," he said.

"Our daughters are fourteen and eleven," Ivet said, meeting his weary eyes. "A respected physician was slain in a dark lane last month, and I have watched you grow progressively quieter and strangely more purposeful with every week of the winter. I recognize that expression, Corin. I watched my father wear it during the great frost of '09."

"What did your father do?"

"He was an upland shepherd who carried an iron knife, and he lived to be seventy," Ivet answered softly. "When the wolves came down from the crags, he walked the stone line every night to verify that the hurdles were barred, and then he slept soundly. That was an honest duty, and it was sufficient to keep his children alive."

She placed her hand over his calloused fingers.

"You have never spoken a lie to me in eighteen years, and I will not permit you to start now. But I have ears, husband. I know there is an upland girl with a repaired boot at Fenwick, and a merchant in Thornwall preparing an indictment for the spring court. I have not asked a single question I ought not to ask."

Corin gripped her fingers, feeling the anchor of her steady presence.

"Two promises, then," he said. "The first is that I will inform you the moment our roof is in genuine danger, and not an hour before, so that you do not spend three months shivering over an anticipated storm."

"And the second?"

"The second," Corin said, "is that if the line fails, I will send a mounted rider to this hearth before I report to the baron's hall, and you will convey our daughters to the stone sanctuary at Fenwick without pausing to inquire why."

Ivet looked at him in the firelight, her jaw setting with quiet, unshakeable courage.

"That is an honest agreement," she said. "Now come to bed. The frost will be deep upon the road tomorrow."
