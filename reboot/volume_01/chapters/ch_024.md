Baron Osric of Thornwall was the most impeccably courteous host Seraine Varyn had ever encountered, and before the second course of soup was cleared on her first evening, she had identified two distinct falsehoods and possessed the evidence to prove neither.

He met her carriage at the outer portcullis in his own person—an honor marcher magnates rarely accorded a duke's daughter unless they sought an immediate concession. He assigned her the lord's guest solar in the south tower, ordered a fresh peat fire stoked in the hearth, and provided a soft-spoken maidservant to tend her gowns. He apologized twice for the modest accommodations of a border keep, doing so with the practiced modesty of an aristocrat who understood that his tapestries, silver flagons, and polished oak wainscoting were the finest in the province.

The contrast between Thornwall Keep and Vale Manor was striking in every masonry joint. At Vale, the timber roofs were low, the courtyard smelled of wet soil and horse manure, and Baron Edric's boots were caked with the honest mud of his drainage ditches. Thornwall was an austere fortress of dressed grey limestone, its corridors quiet and scrubbed, its high walls hung with heraldic banners depicting the stooping silver hawk of House Osric. Yet for all its luxury, there was an unnatural, disciplined chill in the corridors; the retainers spoke in hushed monosyllables, and not a single burst of kitchen laughter carried into the hall.

Osric inquired politely after her journey from the capital, listened to her replies with hands clasped over his marten-trimmed robe, and asked three discreet questions regarding southern grain prices.

"He is extraordinarily capable," Seraine noted in her private journal before changing for dinner. "He displays no warmth; he displays preparation."

---

The initial falsehood occurred while walking through the inner yard to the great hall.

"I understand you recently visited the Vale estate, Lady Varyn," Osric remarked, matching his stride to hers along the flagged colonnade. "How did you find Baron Edric? We have been neighboring peers for eleven years. I see him twice annually at the county assize, and I am always profoundly grateful for his fellowship."

"He appeared exceedingly weary, my lord," Seraine replied, watching the baron's profile.

"An honorable soldier carries the burden of his people," Osric said, holding open the heavy oak door for her. "I have urged him repeatedly over the past three winters to accept a detachment of my own lances to garrison his northern boundary. He refused every offer out of unyielding provincial pride. I respect his independence, of course, but it costs his tenants dearly when the mountain reivers ride."

It was an immaculate performance, and Seraine recognized the deception exclusively through the pitch of her internal ear.

The discordant hum resonated immediately: she had spent four days in Edric Vale's hall, listening to an aging, honest soldier recount his thirty-year struggle against debt and raiders. A desperate border lord overwhelmed by horse-thieves does not forget an offer of twenty armed lances from his nearest neighbor. Edric had never mentioned such a proposal because no such offer had ever been made.

*Either Osric invents generous gestures that never took place,* she deduced, *or Edric rejected him so decisively that Osric has refashioned the humiliation into an act of magnanimity. Either way, it is a monument constructed for my benefit, not a historical fact.*

---

The second falsehood surfaced before the pheasant soup was removed from the table.

There were eleven guests seated at the high board—municipal aldermen, the bailiff of the market, and several respectable burgesses. Osric had introduced each man by name, title, and lineage without a single error, demonstrating that he had rehearsed the roster during the afternoon.

"There is an inquiry I must put to you, Lady Varyn," Osric said as the servants cleared the pewter bowls. "And I beg you to inform me if it exceeds the bounds of propriety, in which case I will withdraw it without offense."

"Inquire as you wish, my lord."

"Your prospective report to the Count of Harven," the baron said mildly.

"The initial dispatch has already departed," Seraine answered, setting down her silver spoon.

"Then my intervention comes too late," Osric said with an easy, rueful smile. "Yet I will voice what I intended to request: whatever observations your document contains regarding this jurisdiction, I would appreciate the privilege of reviewing them before the Count convenes his assize. I should like the opportunity to offer a formal response."

Seraine studied him across the candlelight. "That is an extraordinary concession to ask of an independent commissioner."

"It is an unusual grievance to be judged upon an indictment one has never read," Osric countered gently. "I am informed your text delineates an economic pattern without naming a responsible perpetrator. I would far prefer to face a specific accusation in open court than remain shadowed by an ambiguous pattern."

The air between them chilled perceptibly.

"My lord," Seraine said, keeping her tone pleasant. "Who informed you that my dispatch outlined an economic pattern without naming an individual?"

Baron Osric did not blink. His smile remained as smooth as polished marble.

"The Count's senior clerk, when he dispatched the notification of your journey to my chancery."

*That is the second falsehood,* Seraine realized, feeling a cold prickle of alarm behind her eyes.

It was a brilliant lie because it carried an aura of bureaucratic likelihood—chancery clerks frequently sent advisory letters to provincial lords. Yet it was an absolute impossibility: Seraine had composed her private dispatch in her locked bedchamber at Harrowmere, sealed it with her personal signet, and committed it to a trusted rider bound directly for her father's solar in the capital. The Count of Harven had not received the parchment; it rested upon Duke Varyn's desk.

Baron Osric had not learned of the text through an official advisory. He maintained a corrupt informant within the ducal postal service, or he had purchased an illicit transcription from a courier.

"Naturally," Seraine said smoothly, dipping her fingers into the rosewater bowl. "I will bear your concern in mind."

"I ask nothing more," Osric replied. "And permit me to offer a practical safeguard. There have been disturbances along the high highway during the past fortnight. When you depart for Harrowmere, take two of my mounted retainers to reinforce your escort. A duke's daughter should not traverse this border with barely four lances."

"You are exceptionally considerate, my lord."

"I am simply an administrator who dislikes irregular casualties on his turnpike," Osric said.

---

On the following morning, Seraine walked down to the stables to inspect her party's mounts.

Twenty horses occupied the stone stalls of the inner court. Seraine had grown up around horseflesh and could distinguish a hunter from a dray-horse at fifty paces. Nineteen beasts were standard marcher coursers and heavy cobs belonging to the household guard.

The twentieth animal was a magnificent grey gelding standing in the far box, groomed until his coat shone like wet slate. He possessed the deep chest, clean hocks, and compact back characteristic of horses bred exclusively in the high northern hills.

She approached the stall, resting her palm against the timber rail.

"Whose mount is the grey?" she asked the stable lad.

The boy kept his gaze fixed upon the floorboards, shifting his weight in awkward silence—the unmistakable posture of a servant instructed to hold his tongue.

"That beast belongs to the upland chieftain," a voice interrupted. Weyl, the baron's chief steward, approached along the passage carrying an iron deed-box. "Chieftain Hask Veyr. He rode down from the high valley to settle his wool accounts. He is lodging in the solar if you wish an introduction."

"I should be glad of it," Seraine replied.

"He lacks courtly polish," Weyl observed. "He is an unlettered border fighter, yet you will find him a man of plain speech and more agreeable companionship than most of the scribes in this hall."

---

Hask Veyr was forty-four years old, with broad, weathered features, iron-grey hair cut blunt at the ears, and hands that bore the white scars of mountain warfare. He rose with stiff, military courtesy when Seraine entered the solar, waiting until she was seated before resuming his chair.

She questioned him for twenty minutes regarding the upper Veyr valley, and he answered each inquiry with direct, unadorned precision.

"We hold sixty hides of thin pasture along the northern gorge," Hask told her, resting his calloused forearms upon the oak table. "Forty lances can bar the mouth of that pass against two hundred foot-soldiers in summer. But when the November ice seals the crags, horses cannot eat rock. A chieftain who cannot purchase barley must lead his men down into the low country with torches, or watch his mounts die of colic before the thaw."

"And Baron Osric provides that barley?" she asked.

"Baron Osric provides silver drafts on the Thornwall market," Hask replied, his tone devoid of gratitude. "He purchases quiet upon his northern perimeter, and he ensures my riders look west toward the river parishes rather than east toward his own sheepfolds. It is not an alliance, Lady Varyn. It is a commercial contract between a man with a purse and a man with eighty hungry horses."

She watched him intently. Every statement he made was entirely factual; Seraine's internal pitch detected no deceit whatsoever. Yet every truth was laid down like a drystone boulder, designed to form an impenetrable wall around his business.

Finally, she leaned forward, abandoning administrative pleasantries.

"Chieftain Hask," she said softly. "In October, a hamlet called Lowmere was burned. Eleven inhabitants were carried north toward the ridge. Four days later, all eleven were delivered safely to Fenwick keep by an upland girl and an old carter, while four guards were found dead upon the heights without raising an alarm. What occurred upon that hill?"

Hask Veyr stared at her for several long seconds, his grey eyes unreadable.

"I will give you an honest answer, Lady Varyn," Hask said, his deep voice scraping like slate. "You should put that question to the authority that took those eleven people out of my camp. And you will discover that not a single tenant in this valley will whisper his name, because every soul between here and the coast is terrified of him and profoundly grateful to him at the same instant. That is a phenomenon I have never witnessed in twenty winters of campaigning, and I am forty-four years of age."

Seraine sat motionless in her chair.

"Why do you share this with me?"

"Because you will hear thirty manufactured explanations from courtiers with silver flagons," Hask replied, rising from his seat. "And I prefer you hear the reality from an enemy who has no reason to flatter your father. I return to the northern cut this afternoon, and I do not intend to spend the spring season in this county. When you draft your final dispatch, record that statement. It is the single unvarnished truth uttered in this keep all week."

He bowed stiffly and departed.

Seraine sat alone in the solar, listening to the silence. Her internal ear remained utterly clear.

*He spoke without omission,* she realized with a cold shudder. *He meant every syllable.*

---

That afternoon, Seraine toured the municipal streets of Thornwall accompanied only by her personal maid, declining the armed escort Osric had offered.

Thornwall was an affluent, tightly governed town. The market stalls were well constructed, the municipal well was paved in ashlar, and the gutters were clear of filth. Yet as she observed the populace, she noted the anomaly she had seen in her ledgers: eleven able-bodied craftsmen lingered near the guildhall steps with idle hands, watching the road with anxious, hollow eyes.

At the corner of the market lane, she discovered the queue.

Nine respectable men stood in line before the limestone doorway of Master Rennick's commercial house. One was an elderly freeholder wearing a frayed frieze cloak; another held the halter of an underfed pack-mule.

Seraine paused beside a cooper's shop, listening to the conversation of two farmers waiting in the cold drizzle.

"Three marks for the winter lease," the older man was saying, his voice cracked with despair. "My father paid nine groats to the Vale water-mill for twenty seasons and never faced an arrears. Now the bailiffs demand four silver marks at the barrier, and when I cannot pay the carriage fee, Rennick's clerk offers to advance the coin against next year's rye clip. If the frost holds until April, he will own my meadow, my harrow, and my roof."

"He already owns half the weavers along the ditch," the second tenant muttered bitterly. "We are borrowing our own bread back at three pence on the shilling."

Seraine walked back toward the keep, her mind racing as she assembled the arithmetic.

The pattern crystallized with devastating coherence. Silver was being extracted from the river parishes under the fictitious guise of the *sundries* carriage fee—seven hundred marks over two years. The tenants were systematically drained of currency, and when winter arrived, Master Rennick advanced them their own extracted silver as usurious loans secured against their title-deeds.

She entered her solar and wrote in her personal register:

*The tribute exits through the turnpike, and the debt returns through the counting-house. Baron Osric has engineered an artificial famine to subsidize a northern mercenary force, and the river freeholders are financing the very raiders who burn their holdings.*

---

At supper that evening, she was seated beside Master Rennick himself.

He was forty-five, jovial, wearing a stylish tunic of fine green wool, and he steered the conversation toward carriage tolls within ten minutes of taking his seat.

"Nine marks every quarter on the eastern transit," Rennick remarked with an easy laugh. "A fixed rate for two seasons, my lady. The carters complain of the expense, but an orderly turnpike requires continuous maintenance."

"What specific expenditure does the surcharge cover, Master Rennick?"

"Sundries, Lady Varyn," he replied, raising his goblet with genuine mirth. "Simply sundries. The endless friction of moving freight across mud."

He laughed—a hearty, untroubled sound devoid of malice or fear.

Seraine watched him, realizing that Rennick was an administrative cog who executed his extortion with bureaucratic detachment. He did not consider himself a traitor; he considered himself an industrious merchant managing profitable transactions.

---

Late that night, she explored the east range of the manor.

She had requested a chamber in the older eastern wing under the pretext of seeking quiet, and Osric had granted it with courteous reluctance. The corridor was deserted. At the far end of the gallery, the door to the private council chamber stood unlatched.

Seraine slipped inside.

A faint odor of peat smoke and stale wine hung in the air. The hearth fire was banked with grey ash—a fire that had burned until midnight.

Upon the long oak table rested five empty pewter goblets, an open ledger box, and several discarded quills. Near the sideboard stood a heavy earthenware flagon, and beside it sat an untouched clay cup.

Beside the ink-stand lay several discarded parchment scraps. Seraine lifted the top leaf, angling it toward the moonlight filtering through the high casement.

It was a rough draft of a legal brief challenging the Vale family's historic title to four arable parcels along the eastern strip. Beneath it lay a memorandum addressed to "The Reverend Synod at Harrowmere," containing repeated notations regarding Warden Selwyn and border heresies.

Seraine stood in the middle of the room, her heart pounding with cold clarity.

*A war council was held in this room within the past fortnight,* she realized. *Four principals seated at the board. A fifth man standing near the window. And a servant boy who carried the flagon and drank nothing.*

She recalled the gossip of the scullery maid that morning: an alarm had been raised two weeks earlier, the yard sealed, and an intruder pursued through the cellars.

*Someone was present in this room who heard every word,* she deduced. *A boy who carried wine and vanished into the night. Osric is preparing a threefold assault for the spring: a legal claim on the Vale parcels, manufactured letters surrendering the northern gorge to the Count, and an ecclesiastical inquisition through Warden Selwyn to burn the Vales as heretics.*

---

On the third morning, Baron Osric escorted her to the outer gateway, where her four retainers waited beside two mounted Thornwall armsmen.

The mist was lifting from the river, revealing the pale, cold horizon.

"Lady Varyn," Osric said, taking her hand with refined dignity. "You will return south to complete your report for the Count."

"I will, my lord."

"I make one final petition as an aging neighbor," he said, looking into her eyes. "If your dispatch contains allegations against my governance, record that I was never formally examined by your commission. State that I offered my complete cooperation, and that I was denied a hearing."

Seraine looked at the lord of Thornwall—a man who had spent thirty winters balancing ruthlessness against courtly propriety.

"I will record that you were not questioned, my lord," she said.

"Thank you," Osric replied with an elegant bow. "One final counsel: do not linger at Harrowmere. Ride south to Ostmark and present your findings to Duke Varyn before the Count convenes his assize. A father listens in a quiet room with one counselor; a Count listens in an open hall before twelve competing magnates. You will find the first room vastly more productive."

Seraine mounted her mare and turned down the turnpike without another word.

At the crossroads overlooking the Harrowmere cut, she reined in her horse.

Behind her lay Thornwall with its ledgers, its usurious counting-house, and its polished baron. To the west lay the quiet Vale estate, where an honest old soldier mended fences and a quiet fourth son weeded cabbages. Above them rose the grey northern crags, where eighty mercenaries waited for the thaw.

*My father instructed me to be certain a thing mattered before I spoke it aloud,* Seraine reflected, watching the mist drift over the empty turnpike.

*It matters,* she thought. *A storm is breaking across this border, and in the spring the entire province will catch fire.*

She gathered her reins and signaled her escort forward into the south road.
