Baron Osric of Thornwall was the most courteous man Seraine Varyn had ever been a guest of, and by the end of the second day she had counted four lies and could prove none of them.

He met her at the gate himself, which barons did not do for a duke's daughter unless they wanted something. He gave her the good room and the fire and a girl to attend her, and apologised for the house twice in a way that a man only does when the house is very good. He asked her four questions about the capital and listened to the answers with his hands folded, and one of the questions was about the price of carriage on the southern roads, which she thought about for a long time afterwards.

At supper on the first night there were eleven at the table and he introduced her to every one of them by name and by trade, and did it accurately, which meant he had spent the afternoon learning it.

"He's very good," she wrote that night. "He is not warm. He is *prepared*."

---

The first lie was in the yard.

"I understand you've been at the Vales," said Osric, walking her through to the hall. "How is Edric? He and I have been neighbours for eleven years and I see him twice a year and we are always glad of it."

"He's very tired," said Seraine.

"He would be. He takes the border hard." Osric opened a door for her. "I've said to him for years that he should let me lend him men when the north road is bad, and he won't have it, and I respect him for it and it costs him."

That was the first lie and she only knew it was one because of the note in her head, and the note was the flat one she had heard in the Baron of Vale's own hall: Edric had never once mentioned an offer of men in six conversations, and a man does not forget an offer of men.

*Either he's offering things he hasn't offered,* she thought, *or Edric refused him so thoroughly that he's decided to remember it as a courtesy.* She sat down. *Either way, it's a thing he wants me to believe about himself and not a thing that happened.*

---

The second lie was the report.

She had not told him she had written one. She had told four people in the county and three of them were at Vale, and she had told her father in a letter that had gone south on the eleventh.

"There is a thing I'd like to ask you," Osric said, at the end of supper, "and I'd like you to tell me if it's not my business, and I'll not ask twice."

"Ask."

"Your report. When it goes to the Count."

"It's gone."

"Then I'm too late," said Osric, "and I'll say what I would have said anyway, which is that whatever is in it, I'd like to read it before the Count does, and I'd like the chance to answer it."

Seraine put her cup down.

"That's an unusual thing to ask."

"It's an unusual thing to be asked to answer something I've not seen." He said it mildly. "I'm told there's a pattern in it and no name. I'd rather a name and be in the room than a pattern and be outside it."

She looked at him.

"My lord, who told you there's a pattern in it and no name?"

Osric did not so much as blink.

"The Count's man, when he wrote to tell me you were coming."

*That's the second lie,* she thought, and it was the more frightening one, because it was good. It was good because it was almost certainly true — a Count's man might well have written — and it was a lie because a Count's man writing to a baron about the contents of a report about that baron is a thing that would be in her father's file and her father's file had eleven letters in it and not one of them said a word about the eastern pattern.

"Of course," she said.

"And there's a third thing," said Osric, "which is not a request. There's been a disturbance in this county in November and I've had four men on the Harrow road since, and I'd like you to take two of them as far as Harrowmere when you go and not be on that road with four."

"You're very kind."

"I'm not kind," said Osric. "I'm a baron with a duke's daughter on my land in a bad month."

---

The third lie was in the yard on the morning of the second day, and it was not a thing he said at all. It was a horse.

There were twenty horses in the Thornwall yard and Seraine had grown up in a stable and could tell you the difference between a hunter and a hack at forty feet. Nineteen of them were the household's.

The twentieth was a grey gelding, standing in the far corner with a boy holding him, and he was a good horse — the best horse in that yard by a distance — and he had the deep chest and the short back of a beast bred in hill country and not on a good road.

She looked at him for a while.

"Whose is the grey?"

The boy holding him said nothing and looked at the ground, which is what a stable boy does when the answer is not his to give.

"That's the chieftain's," said Weyl, coming past with a box. "He's been down about the levy. He's in the small solar if you'd care to meet him."

"I would," said Seraine.

"He's not a baron," said Weyl. "He's a man with a valley. He'll be courteous to you and you'll find him better company than most of the men in this hall."

Hask Veyr was fifty-four and had a broad, tired, careful face, and he stood when she came in and sat when she sat and answered every question she asked him with a straight answer that was not the answer.

She asked him four things and he gave her four true things that were each shaped exactly like a wall.

She asked what he did in the upper Veyr valley. He said he held land there. She asked how much. He said a great deal, and named eleven farms, and could not have been lying because she checked two of them afterwards.

She asked whether he had ever had trouble with the Count's officers. He said no, and that he paid his dues to Harven in the autumn through a steward and had done for six years, and that she could ask the steward.

She asked what he thought of the eastern marches. He said they were a hard country and always had been and that a man who wanted a quiet life should go south.

And then she asked, because she had promised her father she would be certain before she said a thing and had decided that morning to be certain of nothing and ask anyway:

"Chieftain. In August a holding on the Ashgrove road was burned and two people died. In October a village called Lowmere was burned and eleven people were taken out of a barn four days later by somebody nobody can describe. What do you think happened?"

Hask Veyr looked at her for a long moment.

"I think," he said, "that you should ask the man who took the eleven out of the barn, and that nobody in this county is going to give you his name, because everybody in this county is grateful to him and frightened of him at the same time, and that is a thing I have never in my life seen before and I am fifty-four."

Seraine Varyn sat very still.

"Why are you telling me that?"

"Because you'll hear it from somebody," said Hask, "and it will be somebody with a reason, and I would rather you heard it from a man with none." He stood up. "I'm going back up the cut this afternoon and I'll not be in this county in the spring. If you write a report, put that in it. It's the only true thing I've said all morning and I'd like it to be of some use to somebody who isn't me."

He went out.

Seraine sat in the small solar with her hands in her lap and heard, very clearly, the flat note that means a man has told you a true thing and it is not the thing that matters — and then understood, with a small cold shock, that the flat note was not there.

*He meant all of it.*

---

---

There was a thing Seraine saw on the second afternoon and could not put down.

She walked the town with her own maid and no escort, which Osric had not expected and had not been able to prevent without being rude, and she did what she had done at Harrowmere: she read the place.

Thornwall was fat and well run and had a good well and men who were doing nothing with their hands. She counted eleven of them in an hour and made a note.

And then she saw the queue.

It was outside a house at the end of the market street and it was nine men long, and she stopped and looked at it for a while, and her maid said, "That's the factor's, my lady, for lending," in the tone of a woman who had said it a hundred times, and Seraine said, "How long has it been there?"

"Since September."

She went home and did the arithmetic.

*Nine marks a quarter from forty-one men is three hundred and sixty-eight a year. There is a queue outside a lending house in a county that has had three good years. Either the money is leaving this county or a small number of men have been made very poor very quickly, and I have read the tallies at Harrowmere and neither is in them.*

She wrote: *The levy leaves and the debt comes back. Which means the same man runs both and it should be impossible to run both out of one house without a name on a paper — unless the name belongs to a baron, because a baron's name is on everything already and no clerk ever asks why.*

And underneath that, in a smaller hand, which was the way she wrote things she had not decided yet:

*If I am right about this, then the Baron of Thornwall has spent two years making a county poor in order to pay a warlord, and the poor men in the queue are paying for the raids that burned their own barns.*

---

Rennick she met at supper on the second night,Rennick she met at supper on the second night, and Rennick she did not like.

He was forty-five and cheerful and had a new coat, and he talked to her for a quarter of an hour about the price of carriage, which was the second time in two days that a man in this hall had brought the conversation round to carriage without being asked.

"Nine marks a quarter on the eastern traffic," he said. "It's been the same for two years and it'll be the same in two more, and there's not a carrier on the cut who'll thank you for asking about it."

"Nine marks a quarter on what basis?"

"On the sundries," said Rennick.

"Which are what?"

"Which are sundries," said Rennick, and laughed, and it was a good laugh and it was entirely genuine and there was nothing behind it at all, and Seraine sat at that table with her hands in her lap and understood that she had just been told the whole of it by a man who did not know he had told her anything.

*He doesn't know.*

She went upstairs and wrote three pages in her own hand, and the first line was: *It is not a secret. It is a great many people not being told a thing, and the men who are not told are content, and that is why the paper has a word like sundries on it.*

And the last line, which she wrote after sitting with the pen for a long time, was:

*Also: there is a grey hill horse in the yard of a barony on a good road and its master says he is a farmer and the boy who held it would not look at me when I asked whose it was.*

On the last morning, before the horses were brought, Osric walked her out to the gate himself and said the thing she had been waiting two days for.

"Lady Varyn. A courtesy, and then I'll let you go."

"My lord."

"You will write a report and it will go to the Count and it will say something about this county that I do not know." He stood with his hands behind his back in his own gateway. "I'd like to ask you a thing as a man and not as a baron. If there is a thing in that report that I could answer, and I have not been given the chance to answer it — will you put that in? Not my answer. The fact that I was not asked."

Seraine looked at him.

"Yes," she said. "I'll put that in."

"Thank you." He bowed, exactly correctly. "Then I'll say one thing more and it's the only gift I have. When you go south, do not stop at Harrowmere. Go on to Ostmark and see the Duke your father before the Count sees the report, and tell him what you've told me."

"Why?"

"Because the Count will read it in a room with eleven other men and a heron," said Osric, "and your father will read it in a room with one other person in it. And I have been in both of those rooms in my life, and I would rather have a thing read in the second."

He stepped back and she mounted and he did not say anything else at all, and she rode out of that gate with her four men and his two behind her and she thought about it for four miles.

*That was the most honest thing he said to me in three days,* she thought, *and the reason I can't trust it is that it was also the most useful thing he could possibly have said.*

*He'll make a very good old man and a very bad one and I have got two days and a queue and a grey horse and nothing else.*

She asked for the east range on the second night, which was not a thing a guest did, and Osric gave it to her with a courtesy that did not reach his eyes, and she went up the back stair and found what she had been expecting.

The east range was where a baron with a secret put a guest who asked questions. It was also where a baron with a council put the men he did not want in a room with the rest of the house.

The passage was empty. There was a fire in the small solar that had been banked and not let out, which meant a room used until eleven.

She went in.

There was a table with a green cloth and six chairs. There was a box of papers that had been taken away. There was wine in four cups and one cup on the sideboard, empty, that had not been drunk from at all, and a jug beside it.

She stood in the middle of that room for a while.

*Four men at that table,* she thought, *and a boy with the jug, and his cup was not used because a boy does not drink in the room. And the fire was built up at eleven, because I heard a servant say so, and I heard her say it because she said it to another girl in the passage below me and she was not told to be quiet.*

She went downstairs and wrote in her book:

*There was a council in this house in the last fortnight, of four men or five, sitting until eleven, and every man in the hall says there was not. And there is a boy who carried wine to it and no one will tell me his name and the cellarer they spoke of is not in the house.*

*I do not know what it means. I know that a man does not hold a secret council and then offer his guest two of his men to ride with her; he offers two of his men because he would like to know which road she takes.*

---

She left on the third morningShe left on the third morning with two of Osric's men and her own four, which she had decided to accept because refusing would have been a story.

At the crossroads above the Harrowmere cut she stopped.

Her own men stopped. Osric's two stopped, four lengths back.

"Why are we stopped, my lady?"

"Because I am looking at a road," said Seraine Varyn, "and I have been reading about this road for four years and I have now seen it, and I would like a moment."

It was a good road. It came up out of the low country and crossed at a ford and went east into hills that were grey and low and had nothing in them you could see.

*There's a hundred and six men in those hills,* she thought, *and a baron eleven miles behind me with a patience of thirty years, and a miller's widow with a book, and eleven people at Fenwick, and a boy at Vale who told me in one sentence who to go and see and was right.*

*And the only thing I can prove is that a levy has a name that means nothing.*

*My father asked me to be certain before I said a thing, and I have been in this county for a month and the certain things are all small and the big thing is a shape.*

She turned her horse.

"Ride on," she said. "We're going to Harrowmere and I'm going to write a letter to my father, and then I'm going to write to him again, because the first one will be brave and the second one will be true."
