The finding at Harven was a piece of paper, and Casian had spent a fortnight being angry about it before he understood that a piece of paper was the best news he had had all autumn.

He was in the dairy on the second of January with the finding in front of him, copied out by Ansel's clerk in a hand that was not Ansel's, and he read it four times.

*Four parcels on the eastern strip, held by the barony of Vale against a claim not entered. The complainant: a man of the Exchequer at Harven. The basis: reversion, the last holder in fee having died without heir in the year 1194.*

"Say it," said Ansel.

"It's a good paper."

"It's a good paper," Ansel agreed, "and I've read it nine times and I want to hear you say why."

"Because it doesn't say the Vales are wrong." Casian put his finger on the line. "It says the parcels were held against a claim that was never entered. That's true. My grandfather held four parcels on a claim he never wrote down, because in 1194 nobody in this county wrote anything down." He looked up. "It's not a lie. It's the truth used as a lever, and it took me a fortnight to stop being angry about it and start seeing that whoever wrote this had to admit a thing to say it."

"There's a man in Thornwall who has been paying for a very good clerk for eleven years," said Ansel.

"And that clerk has just told us exactly what we'd have to produce."

---

The rest of it took nine days and most of the money in the county and it was, Casian thought afterwards, the least glamorous thing he ever did and the one he was proudest of.

He did not buy the land. The land could not be bought, because the land was the thing being fought over, and any man who bought it in the middle of a finding would be a man entering the fight and would lose in two years at Harven.

He bought the *debts*.

Four parcels. Eleven tenants. Six of the eleven had borrowed in the last six years, and four of those six were behind, and one of them was behind by ninety marks to a money-changer in Harrowmere who had himself borrowed from a factor in Thornwall, and the chain went up and out and terminated, if you followed it far enough, in a counting house with no name on the door.

"He hasn't bought the parcels either," said Ansel. "He's done the same thing. He's bought the debts and he's going to foreclose in the spring and be the man holding four parcels when the finding comes up at Harven."

"Then he's going to be very annoyed," said Casian, "because I'm going to buy his debts."

"You can't afford his debts."

"No," Casian agreed. "But I can afford the six that aren't his, and if I'm the creditor on six of the eleven tenants on that strip then a foreclosure isn't a small thing any more. It's an eviction of six families in a county that's had two of them in a year and a half, and it's in every tally in Harrowmere, and it's in front of a Count in the spring."

There was a pause.

"You are going to let him foreclose," said Ansel slowly.

"I'm going to make it cost him the strip to do it," said Casian. "And I'm going to do it with money, in a room, in daylight, and nobody's going to die."

---

The money was the hard part and it was solved by two people.

The first was Maeve, who had been running Corran and Son for a month and had discovered something that made her angry.

"There's four hundred marks in this county doing nothing," she said, "sitting in the hands of men who've had three good years and don't trust a bank and won't put it in a field because a field can be rained on. I've had two of them ask me this fortnight whether the carrying business wants a partner."

"Does it?"

"It does not," said Maeve, "but the mill does. I'll take four hundred marks off four men at six and a half in the hundred and I'll put it in my book as a loan against the mill, and it'll look like a widow shoring up a business, which it is."

"And the rate?"

"Six and a half is what a miller pays," said Maeve. "You'll pay me five and I'll not be thanked for the difference."

The second was Brennan, who came to find him on the fourth of January and asked what his money had been for.

"It was for the mill at Vale."

"And the rest of it?"

"There isn't any rest of it."

"Casian." Brennan sat down on the end of the bench. "I'm the third son and I'm not clever about paper and I know exactly what I am. But I lent you four marks in the summer and I've watched this county change since September, and there's a miller who's been to this house eleven times at night and a girl off the upland who eats in our kitchen, and Da hasn't asked me anything because Da thinks I'm the one who talks."

"You are the one who talks."

"I'm the one who talks to people," said Brennan, "which is not the same thing and you know it. I can get a man in Harrowmere to tell me what he owes his brother in an hour and I've never once used it for anything because nobody ever asked me to." He looked up. "I've got two hundred marks. It's mine, it's not Da's, it's from my mother's brother and it's been in a chest for four years. I'd like to put it into a thing. Not to be told what. Into a thing."

Casian looked at his brother for a long moment.

"Brennan. If I take it, you can't have it back in a hurry."

"I know."

"And if I take it and it goes wrong, you'll be a third son of a small barony with nothing, and you'll be that for the rest of your life, and it'll be because you asked me a question in a kitchen."

"Then I'd rather that," said Brennan, "than be forty and be the man who talks."

---

They closed it on the eleventh of January in a room above a chandler's shop in Harrowmere, and it took four hours and eleven papers and a clerk with a good hand, and at the end of it Casian Vale was the creditor of six tenants on the eastern strip to the tune of four hundred and sixty marks, at nothing in the hundred, for as long as it took them to pay.

The clerk, who was a young man with a cold, said, "You'll want to enter these? Or hold them?"

"Hold them."

"They're not enforceable if you hold them and don't enter."

"I know," said Casian.

The clerk looked at him over his pen and then wrote out the receipts.

---

The other thing he did that week was the water, and it was the thing that made the whole of it hold.

There was a millrace on the eastern strip that came off the Harrow stream and ran along the boundary of the second and third parcels and turned the wheel of a mill that had not worked in nine years. It had been dug by somebody's grandfather and it had been maintained for sixty years and abandoned for nine, and the right to the water was the sort of thing that was written in a book at Harven in 1170 and had been argued about twice and never settled.

Casian bought the water right for eleven marks from a man who did not know what he had, and then he walked the race with Corin and a boy for two days and found that it wanted four hundred yards of ditching and a new head-gate.

"You could put a mill there," said Corin.

"I could put a mill there."

"That'd be a mill in a place where a mill hasn't worked in nine years, on a strip that four parcels are being fought over at Harven."

"Yes," said Casian. "It'd be a mill with a wheel turning on it in the spring, in front of a Count's man, and every man in that county would see the water going where it was supposed to go." He put his hand on the old head-gate and it moved under his fingers, which was a thing that had not happened to it in nine years. "A paper is a paper. A mill with water in it is a fact."

---

Brennan came with him to the strip on the last day, which was his right, and they walked it in the wet with the light going at four.

"Whose is it now?" said Brennan.

"Four parcels. Eleven tenants. Four of them don't know my name and two of them hate my father and one of them is a man who was turned off Vale land in the bad winter and remembers it." Casian put his hands in his pockets. "The mill and the race are mine. The debts are mine, but they're not entered, so nobody knows. The land is nobody's until the spring and then it'll be a widow's and an old man's and a boy's, because I'm going to assign three parcels to Calla of Fenwick and one to Ansel, and they'll hold them and nobody at Harven will be able to say a Vale ever did."

"Calla doesn't know this."

"Calla's going to be told on Thursday."

"And if she says no?"

"Then she says no," said Casian, "and I'll have to think of something else, and I won't be angry at her, because she's the only person in this county who's said a true thing to me without wanting anything and she's earned the right to say no to a boy in a wet field."

They walked on.

"You're going to give a barony to a widow and a moneylender," said Brennan.

"I'm going to give two parcels to a widow," said Casian, "and one to a man who has spent forty-three years being the person other men are frightened of and has never once in that time been on the side that won anything. And one to me, which I'll not put my name on either."

Brennan stopped walking.

"Casian. What are you actually doing?"

And Casian, standing in a wet field on the eastern strip with his brother, told him the truth, briefly, in the way he had learned to do it in October with Corin and in November with the dairy.

"I'm making it so that a man in Thornwall can't take a road with a paper," he said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. He's spent thirty years being careful with paper and I've spent four months learning that the way to answer paper is not to argue with it but to be standing on the ground when it arrives."

"That's not all of it," said Brennan.

"No."

"You're doing a great deal more than that and it's got nothing to do with a road."

Casian looked out at the dark coming down over four parcels he had spent his money on.

"I know," he said. "But I've been told twice this year that I answer the question nobody asked, and I'd like to get through one conversation without doing it."

---

There was a man called Hew Ord who had been turned off Vale land in the bad winter and had been living in a byre at the top of the strip since, with a wife and two children and a pig, and on the Tuesday Casian went up and stood at the byre door and told him the whole of it in nine sentences.

The nine sentences took a great deal of work. He had written them out on the Saturday and cut them down on the Sunday and had said them to Corin on the Monday and Corin had said, "That's still eleven."

The byre had a roof that was half thatch and half hurdle. The wife was inside with the children and the pig was inside because the pig was worth more than the roof.

"I've bought the debt on this ground," said Casian. "It's not my ground and it won't be my ground for two years and it may never be my ground. But the debt's mine and there's nothing in it that says you have to go."

Hew Ord looked at him for a long time.

"Whose man are you?"

"Nobody's, yet. I'm the Baron of Vale's fourth son."

"I know who you are," said Hew Ord. "You're the one that turned me off."

"No. That was my father, in the winter, on a levy I can't put a name to." Casian said it without any softening and Corin, standing back at the gate with his hat in his hands, watched the man's face and did not breathe. "I can't undo it. I can tell you that it won't happen again on this ground this year, and I can tell you why I'm doing it, and then you can tell me to go."

"Go on, then."

"There's a man in Thornwall who has spent thirty years learning how to take a thing with a paper. I'm learning faster. That's the whole of it. It's not to do with you."

Hew Ord spat, not at him, off to one side.

"That's the worst thing anybody's said to me all winter," he said. "I'd have liked it better if it was to do with me."

"It isn't. It's to do with him. You're the ground it's happening on."

The man stood in his byre door and thought about that.

"And if I stay?"

"You stay. Your children stay in the parish and your name stays in the book at the mill and it'll be a book where a name means a family and not a debt, and in three years, if it goes my way, you'll be a tenant of a house that has to ask you a question before it does a thing."

"And I'd be that because you did me a kindness."

"You'd be that because I needed a reason to be standing on this ground that a magistrate would believe," said Casian, "and you were the nearest."

There was a silence at the top of the eastern strip.

"Good," said Hew Ord. "Now I'll believe you. If you'd told me it was for my sake I'd have known you were a lord."

---

On the Wednesday the race was dug in front of witnesses, which was Corin's doing and Corin's idea and the best thing he did all year.

He did not dig it at night and he did not dig it quietly. He put a notice in the church door at Ashgrove on the Sunday, which was read out after the second lesson because the priest liked him, and the notice said that on the Wednesday the race above the mill would be cleared from the ford to the wheel and any man who had a grievance about the water was to come and say it standing on the bank.

Forty-one people came. That was more than came to the fair.

They dug the silt out in barrows and laid it along the top of the bank where it could be seen, and they worked from eight to four with a break at noon, and Corin had two men with poles pacing the line and Maeve stood on the bank with the ledger and a boy to write, and whenever a man asked a question she wrote the question down and read it back to him.

Three men came to say a grievance. One of them was a miller from downriver who said the race would take his water and Corin heard him out for a quarter of an hour in front of everybody and then had the poles moved four feet and the man was satisfied and told everybody so.

The second was a woman whose washing-place was below the ford and she was told the race would not touch it and she said she'd come and see in April.

The third was a Vale man on a hired horse who stood at the back and watched for two hours and then rode away, and Corin said afterward, "That'll be the steward, or the steward's man."

"He'll report it."

"He'll report it," said Corin Ahl, "and what he'll report is forty-one people standing on a bank watching a baron's son dig a ditch in daylight by a notice that was read out in church. I've been in this county all my life, master. There's no way to report that which doesn't make it bigger."
On the Thursday he went to Fenwick and asked Calla to hold two parcels of the eastern strip for him, and she listened to the whole of it, which took half an hour, and asked four questions, and then said the thing that made him certain he had been right about her.

"How long will I be holding it?"

"Until the finding is heard. Two years. Maybe three."

"And in three years, if it's settled in your favour, what do I have?"

"A mill that works, a race that's dredged, four parcels with eleven families on them who've been let alone, and a barony that's twice the size it was."

"And what do you have?"

"Nothing I can put my name to."

Calla of Fenwick looked at him.

"That's the first time in this whole winter you've said something that frightened me," she said, "and it's not because of what you're doing. It's because you've thought it through, and a boy of sixteen who has thought a thing through this far is not going to stop, and I have been running a barony on my own for six years and I know exactly what a person like that does to a county."

She put her cup down.

"I'll hold the two parcels," she said. "On three conditions. You tell me everything that's in your book — all of it, not the parts that make you look well. You put the mill in the name of the village and not in the name of any house. And when I ask you a question you don't want to answer, you say 'I'm not going to answer that' and not something clever."

"I'll not say that."

"You'll say it the first time," said Calla, "and then we'll find out what sort of man you are."

---

He rode home in the dark and Argius, who had been quiet for two days, said the thing he had been waiting for.

"Say the number."

*Twenty-seven. Twenty with Corin's count on the fourteenth, and Brennan, and Calla, and four tenants whose paper I now hold and who don't know it, and Nessa.*

"Say the other number."

*Eleven families on a strip that aren't mine.* He rode on a way. *And four thousand people in this county that I have spent five months making decisions for without once asking any of them.*

"Which is the thing that Calla of Fenwick has just noticed," said Argius, "and which is the reason she made you tell her everything in your book, and you should notice tonight that she is the first person in this county to ask you for something you would find difficult to give."

*She'll be sorry.*

"She'll be the most valuable thing you have," said Argius. "Do not tell her that. She would not like it and it would not be true in the way you mean it."
