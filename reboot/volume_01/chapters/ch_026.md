Elna Vale was twelve years old and had been waiting all winter for something to happen to her.

That was not a thing she would have said out loud. What she would have said, if anyone had asked — nobody did — was that everybody in that house had a thing now and she did not. Brennan had the fair and the road and he was always coming back from somewhere with news. Gareth had the wall and the men and the drills, and he'd got angrier since November and the anger had a shape to it, which was better than nothing. Aldric had the heirship and Da spoke to him in the evenings with the door shut. Nessa had the surgery and had been in a room with a dying man, which Elna thought about a great deal. Casian had the garden.

And Elna had a rake.

That was the whole of the winter, as far as she could see it: the rake and the lessons and being told to go inside when the men were talking.

*Nobody thinks a girl of twelve is a thing,* she thought, on the eleventh of February, walking down the lane above the mill. *They think a girl of twelve is a person who'll be a person later, and later isn't now, and now is when everything's happening.*

She was wrong about that by about four minutes.

---

The holding above the mill was called Bradey's and it had a walled yard and a byre and eleven sheep in it and a family called Ord, and Elna had been sent down with a basket because Maren wanted the loan of their churn and did not trust a boy to bring it back.

There were four things she noticed and she noticed them in the order a person notices things, which is not the order they matter in.

The first was a cart on the Harrow road at the top of the rise, standing, with nobody with it.

The second was that the Ord dog was not barking, and the Ord dog barked at everything, and it was the loudest dog in the county and it had once barked at Elna for four minutes while she stood in the yard with a basket.

The third was that the byre door was open.

And the fourth was a man coming round the corner of the wall who was not Ord and was not one of Ord's men and was wearing a coat she had seen before.

She stopped in the lane and did the thing she had been told to do in November, in the kitchen, by her mother, in a voice her mother had never used before: *if you are ever on a road and a thing is happening, do not run home. Run to the water and get behind a stone and count to two hundred.*

Elna Vale ran to the water.

---

There was a place she had known since she was six: a stone in the bank above the millrace where the water came round a bend and a person could get in behind it up to the knee and not be seen from the lane.

She got in up to the knee and counted.

She got to twenty and then the counting stopped being a thing she could do, because four men came down the lane at a run and two of them went past her and two of them stopped, and she could hear them breathing, and one of them said, "Where's the girl?"

And the other said, "There's no girl."

"There's a girl. I saw a girl."

"There's a basket in the road. A basket's not a girl."

"Then where's Ord's lot?"

"In the byre with a lad on the door and the old woman's in the house with her hands up and there's two of ours in the yard. It's going well. Come back and be useful."

They went.

Elna stayed behind the stone for a long time. She was aware that she was cold and that her feet had gone past cold and that she was shaking, and she was also aware, in a way that she would remember for the rest of her life, that she had not made a sound, and that this was not because she was brave; it was because she had been told what to do and had done it and her body had done the rest without asking her.

She got out of the water at the bend and went up the bank on the far side, which put the millrace between her and the lane, and she ran.

And halfway up the field she made the decision that she would still be thinking about at forty.

She did not go home.

Home was a quarter of a mile up the road in the direction the men had come from, and there were men on the road, and her mother was in the kitchen, and if she ran home and her mother came out and the men were on the road then her mother would be in the road. She had that in her head as clearly as she had ever had anything.

So she ran for the mill, because the mill had a bell.

---

Maeve Corran was in the mill with her sleeves tied back.

"There's nine or ten men at Bradey's," Elna said, in the doorway. "They've got Ord's lot in the byre. There's a cart standing on the road with nobody with it. Ring it."

Maeve did not ask a single question.

She went up the ladder to the bell — the old fire bell, which had hung in that mill for a hundred years and had been rung twice in Elna's lifetime — and she rang it, and stopped, and counted to ten, and rang it again.

"Now," she said, coming down. "How did you get here?"

"Along the race."

"Did they see you?"

"I don't think so. There was one that thought he did."

"Then you'll stay in this mill until your brother comes, and if I tell you to get in the grain cupboard you'll get in the grain cupboard and you'll do it without asking, because a cupboard with a girl of twelve in it is not a thing a man opens." Maeve looked at her. "You're bleeding."

"I'm not."

"You are. Your leg. Sit down on that sack and let me look at it and don't argue with me, you've been in a stream with a stone in it."

Elna sat down on the sack.

"Is Ord's baby in the house?"

"The baby's in the house," said Maeve. "And the men who have come to Bradey's are not men who hurt babies, or they'd be hurting them by now, and they've been there a quarter of an hour and there's a boy on a byre door, which is a thing a man does when he wants to hold people and not to hurt them. So the thing that's happening is that somebody is trying to take something and be quick about it, and the bell has just spoiled that for him."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because I'm forty and I've buried a husband and I keep a book," said Maeve Corran. "Now sit still."

---

Gareth was there in nine minutes with fourteen men and Jory and a cart, and he did the thing well, and the thing he did was not a charge.

He put six men on the road above and six on the road below and took two and went along the race himself, and the four at Bradey's came out of the yard and stopped, because there were men above them and men below them and a mill bell going, and one of them ran for the cart and got twenty feet before a Vale arrow went into the cart's side and he stopped running.

Nine of them. Seven taken. Two away over the field and into the willow, and Gareth did not follow them, and this was a thing his father praised him for afterwards and a thing he was bitter about for a month.

They found Elna in the mill with her leg bound and her hands round a cup and she said, before anybody asked, "I didn't run home. I ran to the water and then I ran here and I told the miller to ring the bell."

And Gareth Vale, who was twenty-one and had been angry since November, stood in the doorway and looked at his sister and then came over and crouched down and said, "You did exactly right," in a voice that was not right at all, and then went outside and stood in the yard for a while with his back to everybody.

---

Casian was at the far end of the garden when the bell rang, and he was in the dairy when they brought them in, and he did not go down to the mill.

That was the thing everybody noticed and nobody said.

He came out into the yard when they came up with the cart and the seven men tied in it, and he stood at the gate with his hands at his sides and looked at them one at a time — seven men, wet, one of them with a bloody face, one of them a man of about thirty with a good coat — and Elna, sitting on the tail of the cart with her leg bound up, saw her brother's face and did not recognise it.

It was not anger. She had seen Gareth angry that afternoon and it had been a loud thing with a great deal of walking about.

Casian's face was the face he wore when he was working out a sum.

He looked at the seven of them for what was probably four seconds and then he said, "Take them to the guardroom and don't let anyone talk to them tonight, and Gareth, put two men on the door who'll stay awake."

"They'll be talked to in the morning, then?"

"They'll be talked to in the morning," said Casian, "and not by me."

He came over to Elna and crouched down in front of her and looked at her leg and then at her face.

"You rang a bell," he said.

"I told the miller to ring it. I wasn't tall enough."

"No," said Casian. "You're not. That's a good reason." He straightened. "Mam's in the kitchen and she's been told you're all right and she'll be very angry with you in about an hour, and I'd like you to let her be angry and not say the clever thing, because she's been standing in that kitchen since the bell and she's got the right to be angry at somebody."

"Are you angry?"

"No."

"Gareth's angry."

"Gareth's angry because he's got fourteen men and a wall and he got there nine minutes late and it wasn't his fault. That's a different thing and it's the best thing about him." Casian put his hand briefly on top of her head. "I'm going to go and stand in the garden for a while. Tell Mam I'll eat at eight."

He went.

Elna watched him go and thought: *that's not a person who's finished.*

---

She was right.

What Elna Vale did not see, and what nobody in the barony saw, was that her brother spent the hour between five and six in the walled garden with his hands in his pockets, doing nothing at all in the way that a person does nothing when they are doing an enormous amount.

He did not go to the guardroom. He did not look at the seven men. He had looked at them once, in the yard, for four seconds, and in those four seconds he had gone through them the way a woman goes through a purse.

The man in the good coat was called Rennick's man and had been paid in Harrowmere six days ago. Four of the seven had signed a paper in November. Two of the seven had been at Lowmere. One of the two had been one of the four men at the barn above the ford, and had been on the shoulder when the fire was still burning and had come home and told the story about the mist.

And behind all seven of them, four days back, there was a room in Thornwall and a man in it who had decided that the Baron of Vale should have a bad February.

That was the sum. It took him four seconds in the yard and an hour in the garden to decide what to do about it.

---

At six Corin came out and stood at the gate of the garden and did not come in, because a man who has been admitted to the garden is a man who has been made something.

"Master."

"Corin."

"The baron's asking for you. He's in the hall with the eldest and he's got the seven in the guardroom and he's sent for the magistrate at Harrowmere."

"He has."

"He'll hang them," said Corin. "There's a law for it and it's a good law and it's the one thing this county has that a man can point at, and if he doesn't hang them he's a baron who had nine men take a village and did nothing, and every man in these marches will know it by the spring."

"I know."

"And you've been standing in a garden for an hour."

"Yes."

Corin Ahl came into the garden, which he had never done.

"Master. Tell me what you're going to do and I'll not argue, and if it's a thing that needs a man on a road at four in the morning I'd like it to be known that I've done it before and I'm not squeamish."

Casian turned round.

"They'll not be hanged," he said, "and they'll not be taken in the night. They'll go home."

Corin stared at him.

"Go home."

"All seven. Tomorrow. With the two that ran, and with the cart, and with a story." Casian put his hands back in his pockets. "In three days there'll be nine men in this county telling a story about the Vale and it will be the strangest story anybody has ever told in these marches, and by the end of the month it'll have been told four hundred times by people who weren't there, and there'll not be a man in the eastern hills who'll take a coin to come down and try it again."

"And the law?"

"The law is the thing my father has," said Casian. "Give me three days and the story will do more than the law can, and nobody will hang, and nobody will be able to say afterward that a baron did it, or why."

Corin stood in the garden in the dark.

"That's not a thing I understand," he said slowly, "and I've been following you since October and I've understood everything so far."

"That's because everything so far has been a thing a man could do," said Casian Vale. "This one isn't. Go and tell my father I'll come in at eight and he's to hold the magistrate until the morning."

---

Elna was in the passage when he came in, with her leg bound and her hair down, on her way up to bed and clearly not on her way up to bed.

"Casian."

"Elna."

"You're going to do something."

He stopped in the passage.

"I'm going to make sure nobody does this again this year," he said.

"That's what you said about the last one."

"Yes."

"It didn't work."

There was a silence in the passage of the Vale house, with a girl of twelve in it who had spent twenty minutes behind a stone in a stream that afternoon.

"No," said her brother. "It didn't. And I've been thinking about that all winter and I think the reason it didn't work is that the last time I made it so that nine men couldn't do a thing. This time I'm going to make it so that four hundred men won't."

---

By the fifth of March the story had been told four hundred times and there was no two versions of it that agreed, and every version was worse than the truth.

In one, the fourth son of Vale had a book in which was written the name of every man in the county, and a man whose name was in the book died in his bed within the year. In another, he had stood in the guardroom in the dark for four nights and on the fourth night he had taken a man's name out of his mouth and the man could not say it afterwards. In the worst version, told in Harrowmere by a carter who had never been east of the cut, the fourth son had made the nine men eat bread that had been baked over a fire he lit with his hand.

That last one Casian heard himself, in the yard at Vale, from a boy who had come up with a delivery and did not know who he was talking to. He listened to the whole of it and then went in and wrote it in the book, in the back, under the heading *What they say*, and under that: *Which is cheaper than what it costs to do the things they are talking about.*

And in Thornwall, on the ninth, Rennick heard the true version from his own man and did not laugh, and said to Osric, in the room with the four cups: "It's a trick. There's a trick in it and I'll find it."

Osric, who had not slept properly since the middle of February, said: "Find it in the next three weeks, then, because the Count hears the Vales at Harven in the spring and I would like to know what to say."
