Berrin had served as a mercenary captain for nine years and had gone unpaid since August, and the humiliation inflicted upon him at Bradey's holding in February had left him with an account he could not recite without being mocked by his peers—a disgrace far more corrosive than the seven riders his negligence had forfeit.

That failure gnawed at him through four sleepless nights in the high camp. He had placed a novice spearman upon the byre door, entered the yard himself, and remained inside the timber enclosure for twenty minutes without dispatching an outrider to observe the highway. He had known, in the vague, dismissive manner of an experienced fighter who neglects the terrain, that a water-mill equipped with an alarm bell stood barely half a mile down the river cut.

He had commanded lances for nearly a decade. An adolescent girl and an iron parish bell had shattered his reconnaissance.

---

The seven men who had been captured, along with the two who had fled into the willow marshes, returned to the northern camp on Friday afternoon, and their arrival confounded every veteran in the valley.

They ascended the sunken Ashgrove turnpike riding in the very dray they had commandeered for the raid, the splintered ash side still bearing the iron barb of a Vale broadhead. All nine men were present. None had been executed upon the manor gallows, none bore the purple bruising of a gaoler's baton, and every man retained his boots, his wool cloak, and his belt-knife.

Berrin intercepted them at the stone crossing, having spent three days calculating the cost of replacing their gear.

"What occurred in their hall?" Berrin demanded, seizing the lead horse's bridle.

"They released us," replied the man in the wool coat, a commercial factor named Skeat who took wages from Master Rennick and who appeared, on this grey afternoon, utterly drained of his customary urban swagger.

"They released you?"

"They unbarred the guardroom heavy door at daybreak," Skeat said, staring past Berrin toward the high crags. "They distributed coarse rye bread, returned our dray, and advised us to clear their parish boundary before Baron Edric concluded his morning prayers."

Berrin scanned the nine faces. Not a single man held his gaze.

"Why would they grant quarter without demanding a ransom?"

Skeat gave an explanation that Berrin would turn over in his thoughts for twenty winters—an account that would spread through every tavern between the Veyr gorge and the coastal marshes within a fortnight.

"They secured us in a stone cellar," Skeat murmured, his voice trembling faintly. "And on the initial night, a youth entered the chamber, held an iron lantern before our faces, and observed us for five minutes without uttering a syllable. On the second night, he returned at midnight and repeated the inspection. On the third night, he stood in the doorway for an hour while we sat upon the straw, staring through us as if we were chalk marks upon an account slate. And on the fourth dawn, he unbolted the timber latch and spoke one sentence: *Depart this valley, and inform your chieftain of what you witnessed.*"

"And what did you witness?" Berrin pressed.

"Nothing," Skeat whispered. "That is the terror of it. Nothing whatsoever. Four nights of absolute silence, an unarmed youth who observed us without malice or anger, and then an open gate."

---

Hask Veyr received the report in his timber hall, listening without uttering a word or smiling, and his grave silence caused half the garrison to take the matter with deadly seriousness while the younger fighters grumbled that their chieftain had lost his nerve.

Tolan stood in the corner of the hearth, his arms folded over his scarred brigandine, watching the nine returned mercenaries shuffle their boots in the rushes.

*That is not the mercy of an exhausted enemy,* Tolan reflected. *That is an adversary who understands precisely how to destroy the discipline of an armed troop.*

Because the incident did destroy them. It was a poison that no veteran captain could diagnose, yet every man in the camp felt its cold weight. Nine men had marched south with torches to burn an outbuilding and drive off valley sheep; they had returned with their hides intact, their knives sharp, and their stomachs filled with warm bread; and not a single one among them would agree to mount an expedition against House Vale again.

"What restrains you?" Hask demanded of the room, slamming his flat palm against the trestle board. "You stand before me whole. No bones are broken. No ear was cropped by the executioner. Nothing was taken from your gear."

An elderly spearman named Kester—a veteran of fifty-five winters who walked with an iron limp from the southern wars and rarely spoke—stepped forward from the bench.

"That is the core of the grievance, chieftain," Kester said, his gravelly voice silencing the hall. "Nothing was done to us. We rode south nine strong and we returned nine strong, without an honorable wound upon our bodies. Our neighbors will say we were dismissed because we presented no threat whatsoever, and none of us can produce a scar to prove otherwise. A soldier knows how to endure a defeat; he knows how to sit in irons, take his flogging, and swear retribution. But no man living knows how to be dismissed like a child sent home from a catechism lesson with a crust of bread in his fist."

---

The disaster that followed was entirely Berrin's design, and he would curse his own vanity until his dying day.

He conceded that they could not assault another fortified holding; the men would refuse the command. What Berrin proposed was a demonstration of strength—not an ambush, but a visible show of force conducted in broad daylight along the eastern highway, marching in full formation so that the Vale outposts would spend two weeks anticipating an assault while Hask's riders recovered their swagger.

Twenty-three mounted lances. Midday sun. Marching along the Ashgrove highway down to the willow cut and circling back across the high drove, observed by every sheep-tender on the ridges, burning nothing, and returning to camp before sunset.

Hask rejected the proposal twice, and on the third appeal he yielded through weary indifference—the most disastrous mistake he made all season.

---

They rode out on the twenty-fourth of February, twenty-three horsemen arrayed in disciplined files, their iron spear-points glinting beneath a pale winter sun.

For four miles, the expedition proceeded with textbook precision.

Berrin had deployed his outriders according to established marcher doctrine: two seasoned scouts half a mile in the van, two veterans guarding the rear, and the main body of nineteen lances trotting in double file between, visible to every watcher along the valley rim.

The van scout on the eastern flank was a young hunter named Nib, possessing sharp eyes and a light seat. He was the first to detect an anomaly.

Nib wheeled his gelding and galloped back along the turnpike—an undisciplined haste that an experienced scout avoided—hauling his foam-flecked mount onto its haunches beside Berrin.

"There are figures blocking the road," Nib gasped, his voice cracked with sudden dread.

"Where?"

"Ahead, captain. At the willow crossing." Nib's hands shook against his reins. "Six men standing across the turnpike. They are not retreating."

Berrin rose in his stirrups, shading his eyes against the pale glare.

The highway descended through a shallow limestone cutting, bent sharply toward the gravel ford, and climbed the opposite bank into the scrub. There, positioned squarely in the center of the road in broad daylight, six figures stood motionless.

They carried no weapons.

Berrin studied them through the thin air. He could discern no broadswords, no hunting bows, no iron caps, and no wooden targes. They stood shoulder to shoulder across the wagon ruts, clad in plain russet tunics, their arms resting loosely at their sides.

"Six men," Berrin said, settling back into his saddle.

"Six," Nib repeated, his breath coming in ragged gasps.

"Then we will ride straight through them," Berrin ordered, drawing his broadsword. "Nib, signal the rear guard to close up, and instruct the left flank to loose two shafts above their heads to scatter them into the brush."

Berrin turned in his saddle to verify the disposition of his troop.

The turnpike behind him was entirely deserted.

---

The nineteen horsemen were gone.

Berrin could see the dirt road stretching for half a mile between the limestone banks in the clear winter light. A heartbeat earlier, nineteen mounted lancers had been trotting behind his shoulder in tight military order.

Now, there was not a living soul upon the road.

Yet the horses remained.

That was the horror that shattered his sanity. Nineteen cavalry mounts stood quietly in a loose cluster along the highway, their reins trailing in the dust, their ears pricked forward toward the ford. Not a single rider sat in the stirrups. The leather saddles were utterly vacant.

Nib emitted a high, choked whimper in his throat.

"Be silent," Berrin hissed, his blood turning to ice water.

"Captain... where did they—"

"Do not look upon it!" Berrin shouted, wheeling his mare violently. "Ride!"

They fled in blind panic—Berrin, Nib, and the two terrified rear scouts who had closed the distance only to find empty mounts. They abandoned the road, plunging their horses through the thorn hedge into the pasture, bypassing the willow ford where the six unarmed figures stood waiting. They drove their mounts across the frozen turf, through drainage ditches, and up the steep scree of the ridge, riding without looking back until their beasts collapsed from exhaustion in the upper scree.

Nib sat in the frozen heather, weeping uncontrollably without making a sound.

---

That catastrophic report was barely eleven days old when it was reinforced by an even more chilling testimony from an independent observer.

The witness was a parish woodward from Ashgrove named Orm, an elderly laborer who had never held a military weapon and who had spent his life managing coppice timber for the river freeholders.

He had been walking the lane above the mill on the morning of the twenty-second of February, carrying an iron billhook and accompanied by an unruly mongrel dog, inspecting pasture fences for frost damage.

Eight armed men had marched down the lane in single file. He had observed them from fifty paces: seasoned fighters wearing dark wool cloaks with their hoods thrown back, three carrying upland bows, four bearing broad-axes, and a tall man leading with an ash spear.

Orm had scrambled over the drystone wall and flattened himself into the mud of the ditch, reciting the twenty-fourth psalm under his breath while pressing his hound into the dirt.

When he raised his head, the lane was vacant.

"Vacant?" Hask asked, leaning forward from his high chair in the council room.

"Completely empty, chieftain," Orm said, clutching his greasy wool cap.

"How long did you remain concealed?"

"The space of two recitations of the psalm," Orm answered. "I speak the verses slowly, as my mother taught me. Perhaps four minutes."

"And you heard no shouts? No sound of boots running?"

"There was only the dog," Orm said, his voice dropping into a frightened whisper. "That is why I came to your hall. My dog is an unruly beast that barks at crows, sheep, and passing carts. In that ditch, the animal ceased to breathe. It flattened its belly into the freezing slush, shivering so violently its teeth rattled. When I stood up, the dog refused to rise. Its legs were turned to water, and I had to carry seventy pounds of hound across my shoulders for half a league."

The council chamber grew cold as an open tomb.

"Eight fighters," Hask murmured.

"Eight men entered the lane, and then no men walked upon it," the woodward said. "And on the following morning, I returned with two parish neighbors to inspect the ground. It was soft clay from the thaw. My own boot-marks were pressed deep where I tumbled over the wall. Yet along the center of the lane, where eight armed men had trodden sixty paces earlier, there was not a single footprint in the mud. Not an iron heel-mark. Not a broken twig."

---

The four survivors of Berrin's expedition straggled into the upper camp at nine that night, their horses stumbling from fatigue.

Twenty-three had ridden out beneath the midday sun; four returned.

Hask heard the confession standing upon his feet, refusing to sit throughout the interrogation.

"State the number again," Hask said, his face carved of dark wood.

"Nineteen," Berrin answered, staring at his boots.

"Upon the open road? In broad daylight?"

"In broad daylight," Berrin insisted, his voice cracked with exhaustion. "I looked upon them, turned my head to address the van scout, and when I looked back, the road held only riderless horses. No battle-cries were raised. No steel was drawn. There was no blood upon the gravel, and nothing that a soldier could strike with an axe."

"And the six watchers at the ford?"

"They remained standing in the ruts," Berrin said. "And when we crested the ridge and looked down through the scrub, two of the six had departed, leaving four figures standing motionless in the water."

---

The nineteen abandoned mounts returned to the northern camp on the afternoon of the twenty-sixth of February.

They were led up the drovers' track by a fourteen-year-old carter's boy and three elderly peasants from Ashgrove, who carried ash staves and proclaimed loudly that they were merely hired draymen returning stray livestock.

Nineteen cavalry horses ascended the trail, moving in quiet pairs with their reins knotted together, stepping with the gentle docility of plow-beasts. The boy held the leading line, puffed up with pride at earning four silver groats for his trouble, completely oblivious to the terror he was ushering through the palisade.

Hask Veyr descended into the muddy yard, accompanied by Berrin and Tolan, and inspected the string of animals.

Every saddle had been carefully unbuckled, lifted from the horse's withers, and laid straight across the beast's loins, with the leather girths folded neatly over the seat.

It was the precise, deliberate method an experienced groom employed when resting mounts after a long march—an action requiring patience, calmness, and ten minutes of unhurried labor. It was an arrangement that no troop executed in panic or haste.

"Is the tack complete?" Hask asked the eldest villager.

"Every strap is sound, chieftain," the old peasant answered, baring his grey head. "Not an iron buckle missing, and not a hair chafed upon their backs. The mounts were standing quietly by the ford at dawn, waiting for someone to lead them up the drove."

A suffocating silence gripped the courtyard.

"Pay the carters their silver," Hask commanded.

"A full mark each?" Berrin asked.

"Give them a silver mark apiece, turn the beasts into the high meadow, and place a double guard upon the gate," Hask ordered, turning toward the timber hall. "And issue a strict injunction: no man under my command is to place a bridle upon any of those nineteen mounts while I draw breath."

Tolan stepped into his path at the threshold. "Why forbid the mounts, Hask?"

"Because an armsman who rides one of those horses into a valley village is broadcasting an indictment that will finish us," Hask answered grimly. "And because I do not know what agency removed their riders, and a horse carries no conscience, but I would have to look upon the beast every morning."

---

Tolan turned and walked deliberately toward the lower stable.

He did not inspect his mount as he had on previous nights; he hauled down his heavy traveling saddle, buckled the girth, and secured his rolled blankets across the cantle. From beneath the timber lining of his feed-chest, he retrieved the three leather-bound notebooks containing eight years of observations.

Hask found him tightening the stirrup leathers by the dim light of an oil lantern.

"You are riding tonight," Hask said, leaning against the stall post.

"I ride before the moon rises," Tolan replied, checking the bit.

"Where will you turn?"

"To the single man in this province who has maintained a coherent theory regarding what haunts our perimeter," Tolan said. "There is an ecclesiastical Warden stationed at Harrowmere who has spent eleven years composing petitions to the Synod regarding an ancient horror in these hills. I have considered him a superstitious fool since my youth. I intend to inspect his documents."

"Tolan—"

"And from Harrowmere, I ride south to Harven," Tolan continued, swinging his saddlebags into place. "If I survive the turnpike, I will present my evidence to the Count's marshals. I will be branded a deserter, a coward, and a lunatic, and I will be mocked in the ducal hall precisely as I have been mocked at your supper board."

Hask Veyr stood in the dim stable, his broad shoulders slumped beneath his mantle.

"I should have sealed the keep when the boy poured the wine in November," Hask admitted quietly.

"You should have assigned twenty spearmen to the Lowmere barn in October," Tolan countered, his voice steady and devoid of rancor. "You would have lost all twenty, and it would have preserved your authority, because a warlord who loses twenty fighters to an overwhelming assault remains a commander with an army. A chieftain who loses nineteen mounted lancers upon an open turnpike in broad daylight to absolute nothingness is a chieftain commanding a garrison that will never draw steel again."

He led his grey mare through the stable door into the freezing night.

"What course remains for me?" Hask asked, his voice rough with despair.

"You will pursue the course you have followed for eleven seasons," Tolan answered, pausing with his hand upon the reins. "You will wait, you will hoard your diminishing silver, and you will pray that the storm passes over your head. It will not pass. In the spring, Baron Osric will deliver your eighty lances to the Church to preserve his municipal toll-roads, and I will not be present to witness the slaughter."

Tolan swung himself into the saddle.

"Tolan," Hask called out into the dark. "Was it a mortal man?"

Tolan looked down from his mount, his scarred face shadowed by his hood.

"I cannot answer that," Tolan said. "I have expended six months attempting to persuade myself that our adversary was an ordinary conspirator, and every observation has demolished my hypothesis. Therefore, hear what I know to be true: whatever power governs this valley, it has never struck a soul who was not carrying a weapon against an innocent roof; it granted nine raiders bread and their boots; and it erased nineteen armed fighters from a road while leaving their horses uninjured."

He gathered his leather reins, turning his mare toward the southern pass.

"I have served three commanders across twenty winters," Tolan said, "and not one of them would have spared the horses."

He touched his heels to the flank, descending into the misty dark of the gorge.
