Berrin had been a captain for nine years and had been unpaid since August, and the thing that had been done to him at Bradey's in February had left him with a story he could not tell without being laughed at, which was worse than the seven men his own carelessness had lost.

That was the part he could not get past and he turned it over in his head for four days. He had put a boy on the byre door and gone into the yard himself and had been in the yard for a quarter of an hour and had not put out a man above the road. He had known, vaguely, in the way a man knows a thing he has decided not to weigh, that there was a bell at a mill half a mile off.

He had been a captain for nine years. A bell had beaten him.

---

The nine who had been taken came home on the Friday, and that was the thing nobody in the valley could make sense of.

They came up the Ashgrove road in the cart they had gone down in, with the cart's side still arrowed, and there were nine of them and none of them had been hanged, and they had their knives and their boots and their horses.

Berrin met them at the ford. He had spent three days expecting them dead.

"What happened?"

"They let us go," said the man in the good coat, who was called Skeat and worked for Rennick and was, that evening, a great deal less cheerful than he had been.

"Let you go."

"Opened the guardroom door at dawn and gave us bread and told us to be off before the baron woke up."

Berrin looked at the nine of them.

"Why?"

And Skeat said the thing that Berrin would still be thinking about ten years later, and that would be repeated in every village from the Veyr to the cut inside a month.

"They had us in a room and a man came in and looked at us," he said. "And then the second night. And then the third night. And then the fourth night, and it was the same man every night and he never said a word, and on the fourth night he came in and stood at the end of the room and said, 'You'll go in the morning and you'll tell them what you saw.'"

"And what did you see?"

"Nothing," said Skeat. "That's the whole of it. Nothing. Four nights of nothing, and a man who never spoke, and then a door opened."

---

Hask heard it all and did not laugh, and the fact that he did not laugh was the reason that half the valley took it seriously and half the valley decided their chieftain had gone soft.

Tolan heard it and went out and stood in the yard with his arms folded for a quarter of an hour.

*That's not a man who's lost his temper,* he thought. *That's a man who has worked out precisely what frightens a company.*

Because it did frighten them. That was the thing that no man in the hall could explain and every man in it could feel. Nine men had gone out to burn a byre and take sheep and they had come back with their boots and their knives and their horses and they had been given bread, and there was not one of them who would agree to go out again.

"Why not?" said Hask, to the room. "You're all here. You're all whole. Nothing was done to you."

And the oldest man in the room — a man of fifty-five who had been a soldier in his youth and had a limp and did not talk — said: "That's it, though, isn't it. Nothing was done to us. We went in nine and we came out nine and there's no mark on any one of us, and a man could say we were let go because we were no trouble, and none of us have got a thing to show that we weren't." He looked round. "I've been in a fight that went wrong and I've been in a cell and I've been beaten and I know how to be a man who was beaten. There's no way to be a man who was let go in the morning like a boy who's been kept in at school."

---

The thing that finished it was Berrin's own idea, which he would regret for the rest of his life.

They would not go to a walled holding. That was agreed. What Berrin wanted was a show: not a raid, a thing seen from a distance, a column of men on a road in daylight going the long way round, so that the Vale would spend a fortnight looking at the wrong road and the valley would have its courage back.

Twenty men. Daylight. The Ashgrove road down to the cut and back over the drove, seen by everybody, burning nothing, and home by dark.

Hask told him no twice and refused to forbid it the third time, which was the worst thing he did all winter.

---

They went on the twenty-fourth of February, twenty-three of them, in daylight, in good order, on the road, and it went perfectly for four miles.

Berrin had his outriders out. That was the whole of the plan and it was not a stupid one: two men half a mile ahead, two half a mile behind, and a column of nineteen on the road between, and every one of them visible from the ridge.

The outrider on the left was called Nib and he was twenty-four and he had good eyes and he was the one who saw it.

He came back at a gallop, which a good outrider does not do, and pulled up beside Berrin.

"There's men on the road."

"Where?"

"There. On the road. Ahead." Nib's hand was shaking. "Six of them. Standing in the road at the ford. Not moving."

Berrin stood up in his stirrups.

The road ran down from the shoulder and turned at the ford and went up the far side, and at the ford, on the road, in the middle of the afternoon, in the open, there were six men standing.

They were not armed. That was the thing. Berrin looked at them for a long time and could see no spear and no bow and no shield, and they were standing in a line across the road with their hands at their sides.

"Six," said Berrin.

"Six."

"Then we'll go through them," said Berrin, "and I'll have the two on the left put a shaft over their heads before we do."

He turned to give the order.

And the road behind him was empty.

---

The nineteen were gone.

He could see the road for half a mile in both directions from where he sat on his horse and there were nineteen men on it a moment ago and there was nobody on it now and the horses were still standing.

He could see the horses. That was the thing that broke him. There were nineteen horses standing on the road in a loose bunch with their reins down and their heads up, and not one man with them, and the saddles were empty.

Nib made a sound beside him.

"Don't," said Berrin.

"Where did they—"

"Don't look at it," said Berrin. "Ride."

They rode. Berrin and Nib and the two behind, and they went off the road into the field because the road went through a ford where six men were standing, and they went up the bank and through the hedge and over the shoulder and did not stop until the horses were blown, and Nib was crying by then and making no noise about it at all.

---

That story was eleven days old when it was overtaken by a worse one.

The worse one came from a woodward at Ashgrove called Orm, who was not a soldier and had never held a weapon in his life, and who had been in the lane above the mill on the twenty-second of February with a dog and a billhook, going to see about a hedge.

Eight men came down the lane. He counted them because a woodward counts things: eight, walking, in good order, three of them with bows and the rest with axes and one of them with a spear and all of them wearing dark coats with the hoods down.

He got over the hedge and into the ditch and lay still, which is what a woodward does, and he lay there for the length of time it takes to say the four-and-twentieth psalm twice, which is what he did, out loud, under his breath, in a ditch.

And when he put his head up the lane was empty.

"Empty," said Hask.

"Empty, chieftain."

"For how long did you have your head down?"

"Four minutes. I said the psalm twice and I'm not a quick sayer."

"And there was no noise."

"There was a dog," said Orm. "That's the thing I've come to say and I'll say it and then I'll go home. My dog was with me in the ditch and my dog is a fool of a dog and barks at its own shadow, and it did not make a sound. It got down flat with its chin on the ground and it stayed there, and it was shaking, and when I got up it wouldn't get up and I had to carry it a quarter of a mile."

The hall was quiet.

"Eight men," said Hask.

"Eight men in the lane and then no men in the lane," said the woodward, "and I'll tell you what I've told nobody, because the priest will have me for it. Chieftain, I went back the next day with two men and we walked that lane end to end and there is not a track in it. Wet ground. A ditch either side. Eight men walking, and not a boot-print, and my own prints where I went over the hedge are there still."

---
They came into the hall at nine that night. Twenty-three had gone out; four came back.

Hask heard it standing up and did not sit down for the whole of it.

"Say the number again."

"Nineteen."

"On the road. In daylight."

"On the road in daylight," said Berrin, "and I was looking at them and I turned my head and they were not there, and the horses were there, and the saddles were empty, and there was no noise and no blood and nothing on that road that a man could point at."

The hall was silent.

"And the six at the ford?"

"There were six men standing in the road at the ford and when we came round the shoulder and looked back there were four."

"Four."

"Four," said Berrin, "and they were standing in the same place."

---

---

The horses came back on the twenty-sixth, in the afternoon, driven up the road from Ashgrove by a boy of fourteen and three men of the village who wanted it known they had only been driving horses.

Twenty-three had gone out. Four had come home under men. Nineteen came up the road with their saddles on and their reins tied in pairs, and the boy had them in fours and fives on a leading rein and he was very proud of himself and had no idea what he was bringing.

Hask came out into the yard and looked at them for a long time.

Every saddle had been unbuckled and laid straight. That was the thing Berrin could not look at. Nineteen saddles unbuckled and laid straight across nineteen horses and the girths folded back over the seat, which is how a man leaves a saddle when he means to come back to it in an hour, and which nobody does in a hurry or in a fright.

"Where's the tack?"

"On them, chieftain. There's not a strap missing and there's not a horse marked." The oldest of the three villagers took his cap off. "And chieftain — they came up the road on their own as far as the ford and stood there till we came out. Nineteen horses standing at a ford in the evening waiting for somebody to lead them home."

Nobody in the yard said anything.

"Give the boy a mark," said Hask.

"A mark?"

"Give him a mark and give the men a mark each and put the horses in the long field and put a man on the gate of it." Hask turned for the hall and then stopped. "And nobody is to ride any of them. Not this year. Not while I'm alive."

Tolan caught him at the door.

"Why not?"

"Because a man who rides one of those horses into a village is a man telling a story we can't afford," said Hask Veyr, "and because I do not know what happened to the men who were sitting on them, and a horse doesn't care who's on its back, and I'd have to."

---

Tolan walked out of the hall.

He went to the stable and checked the horse, which he did every night, and this time he did more than check it: he put a saddle on it, and a bag, and the three books out of the lining of the other one.

Hask found him there.

"You're going."

"I'm going tonight."

"Where?"

"To the only man in this county who has a thing to say about any of it," said Tolan. "There's a Warden at Harrowmere who's been writing letters for eleven years about an old horror in these marches. I've thought he was a fool since I was thirty. I would like to go and read his letters."

"Tolan."

"And then I'll go to Harven," said Tolan, "if I'm alive, and I'll tell the Count's man what I've seen, and I'll be a deserter and a madman and I'll be laughed at in a hall in the capital the way I've been laughed at in this one."

Hask Veyr stood in his own stable.

"I should have sealed the yard in November," he said.

"You should have put twenty men on that barn in October," said Tolan. "You'd have lost them, all twenty, and it would have been the best thing you ever did, because a chieftain who loses twenty men to a thing nobody understands is a chieftain with a story, and a chieftain who loses nineteen out of twenty-three on an open road in daylight to nothing at all is a chieftain with a barn full of men who won't go out."

He led the horse out.

"What do I do?" said Hask.

"You'll do the thing you've been doing for eleven years," said Tolan, "which is to be careful and to wait and to hope it goes away. And it won't. And in the spring a baron in Thornwall will hand you to a Church to save his road, and I'll not be here to tell you I told you so, which I'd have liked."

He mounted.

"Tolan."

"Chieftain."

"Was it a man?"

Tolan looked down at him from the horse.

"I don't know," he said. "I've spent six months deciding it was one thing and I've been wrong about every particular. So I'll say what I know, and it's this: whatever it is, it has never once done a thing to a man who wasn't carrying a weapon, it gave nine men bread and their boots, and it has taken nineteen out of a road and left the horses." He gathered the reins. "I have served three men in twenty years and not one of them would have left the horses."
