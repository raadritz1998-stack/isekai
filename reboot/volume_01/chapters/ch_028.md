Seraine Varyn came back east on the third of March because of a letter from the Baroness of Fenwick that contained four sentences and no request.

*Lady Varyn. On the twenty-fourth of February twenty-three men came down the Ashgrove road in daylight. Four came home. I am not writing to tell you what happened, because I do not know what happened and neither does anybody in this county. I am writing because you are the only person who has ever come here and asked a question without a reason, and because somebody from the south ought to see the road before the story gets told four hundred times.*

*If you come, come before the thaw.*

She went.

---

She went to the road first, alone except for two men and her maid, and she spent a whole day on it and found almost nothing, and the almost nothing was the reason she stayed another four days.

The road ran from the shoulder down to the ford and up again, and it was a good road, and there was nothing on it.

She walked it in the morning, in the wet, in the way she had learned from a miller at Vale: from the ground and not from the story. She found a place where twenty-three horses had stood in a bunch, because horses leave a thing in wet ground that is not subtle. She found the place where two horses had gone off the road into a field at a gallop and up the bank and through a hedge, and she followed that for a mile and it was a bad ride and she could see where one of them had nearly come down.

And then she went back to the place where the twenty-three had stood, and she walked out from it in every direction, and she found no blood, no broken tack, no dropped bridle, no arrows, no scabbards, no sign of a struggle of any kind.

Twenty-three men, their horses, their saddles. Four horses with riders. Nineteen horses standing.

She sat down on the verge in the wet and looked at it for a long time.

*If there had been a fight there would be blood and there is none. If they had been taken prisoner there would be tracks of twenty-three men walking and there are none. If they had run there would be tracks and there are not.*

*The only thing the ground says is that nineteen men got off their horses and then were not there, and the horses stayed.*

---

She got one of the four who came back.

Nib was twenty-four and had been a soldier since he was fifteen and he had been in two battles and a siege and he sat in the kitchen of the inn at Harrowmere with his hands round a cup and he answered every question she put to him, in order, without excuses, which is a thing that frightened her more than any story.

"There were nineteen on the road behind me. I'd looked at them a minute before. I was riding back to say what I'd seen and I turned my head at the ford and the road was empty and the horses were standing."

"You turned your head. For how long?"

"For the length of it. A moment."

"Could they have gone over the bank?"

"The bank's a wall of thorn and it's nine feet and a man on a horse can't go through it and nineteen men on foot couldn't do it in a moment and make no noise." Nib looked at her. "My lady, I've been a soldier ten years. I know what men look like when they've run and I know what a rout looks like. A rout makes noise. Nineteen men going off a road makes noise, and carts, and dropped things, and shouts. There wasn't any of it. There wasn't the sound of a man shifting his feet."

"And what did you do?"

"I did what the captain told me and I rode, and I've not slept a whole night since."

"Who do you think did it?"

"I think it doesn't matter what I think," said Nib, "and I'll tell you why, and then I'd like to go to bed. In the hall that night there were men saying it was a spirit and men saying it was the Church and men saying it was a trick with mirrors. And then the old man who'd been a soldier in his youth said the thing I've thought about every day since. He said it doesn't matter what it was. What matters is that in this county there is now a thing that a company of men will not go up against, and every man in these hills knows it, and that is worth more than a thousand men on a wall."

---

She went to Bradey's holding, where nine men had taken a village and lost it and been given bread.

The Ord family were there. The old woman, who was seventy, told her the whole of it in the yard with her hands folded, and she told it well, and what she told was this:

Nine men had come. Four in the yard and a boy on the byre door. Nobody had been struck. They had taken a churn and two sheep and a copper pan and the men had been polite in a bored sort of way and one of them had apologised for the byre.

And the bell had rung, and then the Vale men had come, and the nine had been taken, and there had been a great deal of shouting and one of the raiders had run twenty feet and stopped.

"And then?"

"And then they had them in the cart," said the old woman, "and they went up the road, and I thought we'd see them hanged in Harrowmere in a fortnight, because that's the law and it's a good law."

"And you didn't."

"We didn't. They came back nine days later, all nine of them, and one of them gave my grandson a penny in the road." The old woman looked at her. "I've been in this county seventy years, my lady. I've seen a hanging and I've seen a man flogged and I've seen a family put out of a house in the bad winter. There's never once been a thing where the men who took a village came back and gave a boy a penny."

"And what do you think happened?"

"I think the Vale boy talked to them," said the old woman, "and I've no idea what he said."

---

She went to Vale on the second day and this time the Baron of Vale did not come out into the yard.

Gareth did, and he was courteous and grey-faced and said his father was unwell, which was true, and that he would answer anything she asked, which was not true in the way he said it.

"Twenty-three men came down the Ashgrove road," she said. "Your father has the law and a magistrate at Harrowmere and he did not use either. Why?"

Gareth Vale looked at her for a moment.

"Because he was asked not to," he said.

"By whom?"

"By the man who took nineteen men off a road in daylight and left the horses," said Gareth, "and I'm going to say a thing, my lady, and then I'd like you to leave, because I've been in this house since November and I've got a wall and fourteen men and a name in this county and I have never once been the one who was asked." He put his hands behind his back. "Whoever did that is not a thing in the hills. It's a person. It's a person who has been in this county all winter doing a great many small things and one enormous one, and my father knows who it is and has known since August, and he will not say, and he is my father and I will not be the son who says it first in front of a duke's daughter."

There was a silence in the yard.

"My lord —"

"Ask me about the wall," said Gareth. "Ask me about the men. Ask me anything a soldier can answer."

She looked at him.

"You think it's your brother," she said.

Gareth Vale did not answer that, and did not have to, and the not answering was the whole of it.

---

She walked the lane at Fenwick that evening because Calla asked her to, and it was the fourth day, and by then she had a thing she could not put down.

"There's a woman at the mill," she said. "Corran. She's the only person in this county who tells me a thing and then tells me what she thinks of it."

"And?"

"And she said a thing to me in December that I've been carrying about like a stone. She said that a thing a lot of people are doing and nobody has said out loud is a thing that falls apart the first time it's frightened." Seraine looked out at the dark. "I've been three months looking for a man. Every man I meet gives me a version. The Baron of Vale is tired, his second son is furious, the chieftain in the hills told me to my face to go and ask the man who took the eleven out of the barn, and a baron in Thornwall gave me three days of the best courtesy I have ever been shown by a man who was lying to me the entire time.

"And the only thing all of them have in common is that they are all, in different ways, being managed by something none of them will name."

Calla of Fenwick was quiet for a moment.

"You're going to write a report," she said.

"I'm going to write a report."

"Then write it true and don't write it clever," said Calla. "I've been in this county six years and I've been written about twice. The clever things were wrong both times."

---

She wrote it in Harrowmere over four days, and the draft was eleven pages, and it was the best piece of work she had ever done and it was completely useless.

She wrote what she had: the levy, the recognisances, the queue in Thornwall, the forged-enough paperwork, the fact that the Baron of Thornwall's own steward had countersigned a receipt for money that went over the cut. She wrote the raids — the August burning, Lowmere, Bradey's — and set them against the levy in a table, and the table said a thing so plainly that it could not be argued with.

And then she came to the last three pages and she sat with them for a day and a half.

*And then there is the other matter.*

*On the twenty-fourth of February twenty-three men went down the Ashgrove road in daylight. Nineteen did not come back and there is no body, no grave, no prisoner, and no mark on that road.*

*I have walked it. I have questioned nine men separately. I have examined the ground for four miles. I am satisfied that a thing was done in this county in February that cannot be explained by any means known to me, and that it was done by a person or persons resident here, and that the same agent was responsible for the removal of eleven villagers from a barn at Lowmere in October and for the delivery of nine raiders from Bradey's in February, unmarked, unhurt, and with their horses.*

*I am also satisfied that the same agent has been responsible, since September, for a number of good outcomes in this county that no one will claim.*

She read it back.

She read it out loud, once, in the room, to hear how it sounded — which was a thing her father had taught her to do at fifteen and which had never once failed her.

And it sounded, out loud, in a rented room in Harrowmere, exactly like the ravings of a duke's daughter who had spent too long in the east.

---

She crossed it out.

Then she sat with her pen for a long time and wrote, in the margin, in the small hand she used for the things she had not decided:

*If I send this, my father will believe me and the Count will not, and the Count will read the levy in the same page as a story about nineteen men vanishing off a road and he will decide that the whole report is the work of a woman who has been frightened by a county. And then Thornwall is safe and the levy stands and in two years there is a forged title and a burned chapel, and all of it will be because I put a true thing in a place where it could be used to discredit three true things.*

*If I do not send it, then a thing is happening in these marches that I cannot explain and no one will ever write it down, and in ten years there will be a barony here with a mill and a road and a great many people who cannot do a thing without it, and no record anywhere of how that came to be.*

She put the pen down and went and stood at the window.

Then she came back and wrote the thing that made her, at nineteen, a person her father would not have recognised in November.

*I will not put it in the report. I will put it in a letter to my father, in my own hand, which is not a thing anybody can read but him, and I will tell him to keep it and not to act on it, and I will tell him why.*

*And I will send the report without it, and the report will do what a report can do, which is to put the Baron of Thornwall in front of a Count in the spring with a levy he cannot explain.*

---

She wrote both.

The report went south on the eleventh of March, eleven pages, and it was the driest and most careful thing she had ever written and it did not contain the word *thing* anywhere. It contained the levy, and the queue, and a table, and a recommendation that the Baron of Vale be heard at Harven, and a note that the Baron of Thornwall had asked to see the report before the Count and had been refused, which she put in because she had promised him she would and because a promise is a promise and because it also looked like integrity, which it was.

The letter went in the same packet, sealed separately, addressed to her father alone, and it was four pages long.

She wrote in it everything. The lane. The barn. The nineteen. The road walked for four miles. The ord woman's penny. Hask telling her to ask the man who took the eleven. The grey hill horse. The four cups and the jug in a banked room.

And the last paragraph, which took her an hour and which she wrote three times:

*Father. You told me to be certain a thing matters before I said it. I am certain of this: there is a person in the eastern marches doing a great deal of good and killing a great many men and telling nobody, and he is not a monster, and he is not a fool, and he is going to be the most important thing in Varenthos within ten years, and the crown has no idea he exists.*

*I am not asking you to do anything. There is nothing to do. I am asking you to remember that I told you, in the spring of the year, before anybody else, and that I told it to you straight and not in a report.*

She sealed it and sent it and then went and stood in the yard of the inn at Harrowmere and found that her hands were shaking, which was the first time in eleven years of being a duke's daughter that anybody would have seen that.

---

The answer came back in April, and it was five lines, and it was not from her father.

*To the Lady Seraine Varyn.*

*The Duke has read your letter and has read your report and will say what he has to say to you in person and not in writing.*

*He asks that you remain in the east until the Count's hearing at Harven in the spring, and that you say nothing of any of this to the Royal Marshal, who is travelling in that country this month and who will ask you questions.*

*He asks also that you burn his reply, and I have told him that you will not, and he has said that he knows, and that this is the reason he is sending it.*

*— his clerk*

Seraine Varyn read it twice and then sat down on the settle in a public room in Harrowmere and thought, with a clarity that was almost physically unpleasant, that she had just told a secret to the one man in the world who would not use it, and that he had immediately written back to tell her a marshal was coming.
