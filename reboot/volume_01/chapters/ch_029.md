Vesna had been left without an assignment for nine full weeks, and the enforced idleness was making her restless and strange.

It was not an anxiety she would have voiced aloud. Since mid-October, she had been the solitary soul who traversed the border terrain: nineteen miles of the high escarpment, eleven ancient stone gates, and every isolated sheepfold, field byre, and storehouse between the Veyr gorge and the Fenwick ditch, twice over, through sleet and freezing drizzle. She carried a pocket ledger in her vest containing thirty names of upland tenants, and those names had been gathered through her own inquiries and inscribed in ink by Corin Ahl—because Vesna could not read or write, a deficit she had never acknowledged in the presence of a baron's son.

And since the middle of January, nobody had asked her to lace her boots for a run.

She had reported in December, sitting on a bench in the dairy: *There are three tenant families above the willow crossing who have been conversing with your freight carters.* Casian Vale had thanked her with quiet courtesy, noted the coordinates in his journal, and taken no visible action. She had returned to her mother's upland cottage to await further instructions, because that was the discipline expected of a hired scout.

Then in mid-February, nine raiders assaulted Bradey's holding and the mill bell pealed across the valley. Ten days later, nineteen mounted mercenaries vanished from the Ashgrove turnpike in the midday sun. And by the first week of March, armed horsemen were riding through the northern hamlets asking pointed, terrifying questions under a chieftain's seal.

Vesna learned of the inquisitions from a breathless shepherd's boy who had run eleven miles across the heather to reach her threshold.

---

The questions were simple and brutal, asked in a tone Vesna recognized from her childhood—the flat, menacing cadence an elder used when interrogating a novice over broken harness-leather before reaching for a birch switch.

*Who has been carrying messages to House Vale?*

*Who provided the layout of the holding above the ford before the eleven captives were extracted?*

*Who crossed the border cut in November and returned with silver groats?*

The man conducting the interrogations was an upland captain named Cull Sillow. The Sillow clan had pastured sheep among these granite crags as long as the boundary stones had stood, and Cull was conducting his inquiries with four mailed spearmen at his back and a rolled parchment bearing Hask Veyr's wax seal gripped in his fist.

For three days, Cull traversed six outlying hamlets, interrogating freeholders in their own yards.

On the third night, a woman named Ayleth walked four miles through the freezing dark to Vesna's mother's cottage. She was an upland widow possessing a half-thatched byre, eleven sheep, and two adolescent sons, and she stood in the doorway shivering with fear.

"They will return for my boys at dawn," Ayleth said, her rough hands twisting her woolen apron.

"They have no legal cause," Vesna replied, barring the wind with her shoulder.

"They require no cause," the widow said, her voice breaking into a dry sob. "My eldest was driving our wain down the cut on the night the eleven captives were brought off the ridge. He is seventeen, Vesna. He drinks two flagons of small beer at the crossroad tavern and his tongue turns to grease. If Cull Sillow holds a blade to his throat and demands names, the lad will recite everything he saw, and they will hang both my sons from the nearest ash tree."

Vesna stood upon the threshold, listening to the north wind whistling through the thatch. A hard, cold clarity crystallized in her chest—a sensation she would remember for decades.

*Nobody from Vale is going to dispatch a messenger tonight,* she realized. *Master Casian is four leagues away in his garden, Corin is checking guardroom rosters, and an armed squad with an iron seal is coming to drag two boys into the scrub.*

---

She arrived at the widow's byre at first light with a two-wheeled cart, a borrowed mule, and no orders from anyone living.

That single decision was the entirety of the rebellion; everything that followed was merely the heavy arithmetic of movement.

By noon, through three frantic journeys along the sunken sheep-tracks, she had assembled four endangered families at the edge of the heather.

There was Widow Ayleth and her two terrified sons. There were the Dunstans, a family of six whose stone cottage stood within bowshot of the high cut, clutching bundles of coarse bedding and an iron pot. There were the two elderly Coate sisters, spinsters who had spun mountain wool for sixty winters and refused to abandon their solitary brindle cow. And there was Tam Sillow, an old man of seventy-four who was a third cousin to the chieftain's interrogator and had cursed his kin's cruelty aloud before a dozen witnesses. Tam was the most skilled willow-basket maker in the province, and he suffered from a chronic rattle in his chest that every neighbor knew and politely ignored.

She chose to escort them to the abandoned fold at Hanging Rake—one of the eleven tactical redoubts Corin Ahl had fortified during the December frost. Corin had rebuilt the drystone perimeter, laid a timber roof over the interior byre, and left an embrasure in the masonry where a watcher could lie sheltered from the gale and observe the highway below.

It was eleven miles to Hanging Rake by the high shoulder trail. It was barely nine miles by the drovers' highway.

Vesna led them along the eleven-mile shoulder through the heather and scree, because four armed spearmen were picketed at the willow ford along the shorter road.

---

The march was an agonizing, slow-motion disaster that Vesna would recount years later in her flat, unembellished upland voice.

Before they crested the first ridge, the widow's cart snapped an ash axle upon a granite boulder. They spent an hour attempting to lash the splintered timber with hemp rope and an ash hurdle, but the repair gave way under the weight of the grain sacks, and the men were forced to shoulder the baggage while the women carried the iron kettles.

The two Coate sisters could not maintain the pace across the wet scree. Their leather shoes had worn through at the toes, and within two miles their stockings were soaked with freezing peat water.

The Dunstans had a child of two winters who wept incessantly for five miles as the sleet intensified. When the child finally fell silent, shivering in grey exhaustion beneath his mother's soaked shawl, Vesna seized the bundle and carried the boy against her own wool tunic for the final league, her temper fraying so severely from terror that she snapped an oath at the mother for which she felt ashamed for a twelvemonth.

And Tam Sillow marched four miles in grim silence before collapsing upon a flat boulder beside the sheep-cote, his breath wheezing like a broken smithy bellows.

"Proceed without me, girl," Tam rasped, his face pale as lard. "I will rest my joints and follow when the wind drops."

"You will freeze upon this stone before sundown," Vesna told him bluntly, gripping his rough wool sleeve.

"I have made baskets for fifty winters," the old man wheezed, stubborn pride flaring in his rheumy eyes. "I buried a wife and three strong sons before your mother was wed. I will not be bullied across a bog by a nineteen-year-old girl with a hunting bow."

He dragged himself into the sheepfold at Hanging Rake at two hours past midnight, leaning upon a hazel staff, his boots caked in grey clay. He sat down beside the peat fire the boys had coaxed to life, wrapped his wool blanket around his shoulders, and died quietly in his sleep two hours before dawn, his hands folded peacefully across his chest.

The two Coate sisters knelt in the straw and chanted the psalm for the dead over his body as the grey light touched the gaps in the stone wall.

Vesna walked out to the edge of the ravine, staring down at the mist filling the valley. Tam had not been run down by outlaws; no iron blade had touched his flesh. He was seventy-four years old, and he had marched eleven miles across frozen heather in the dead of night because a nineteen-year-old girl had decided at sunrise that he could not remain in his cottage.

*I caused his death,* she thought, leaning her forehead against the freezing stone. *I acted without permission. If I had remained in my mother's kitchen and waited for an order from Vale, Tam would be asleep in his warm bed. Or he would be hanging from an ash bough behind Cull Sillow's saddle. I will never know which fate was true, and I must learn to carry the weight of that uncertainty for the rest of my life.*

---

She descended to the Vale water-mill on the morning of the ninth of March, finding Corin Ahl in the wagon yard reviewing carriage tallies with an iron stylus.

She planted her boots before his table, her wool leggings crusted with dried mud, her face drawn and hard.

"I moved four families off the high ridge to the fold at Hanging Rake," she stated. "And one of them expired in the night."

Corin set his tally-board upon the timber bench.

"Expired by what agency?"

"Old age. Exposure. My command." Vesna's jaw tightened until her teeth ached. "I require speech with the master."

"He is in the walled garden," Corin said.

"Then I will see him there."

Corin studied her impassive countenance for several moments before picking up his board. "I will accompany you," he said quietly—an intervention that represented an extraordinary departure from custom for an armorer who avoided the private garden.

---

Casian was working along the eastern masonry wall, accompanied by two young carter's lads, using an iron crowbar and an ash lever to true a massive foundation boulder that had shifted during the winter frosts.

He laid the crowbar aside when he observed them approaching the wicket gate, wiping limestone dust from his palms onto his russet tunic.

"Vesna," he said, stepping into the path.

"Four tenant families removed from the high cut to Hanging Rake," she recited without pausing for breath. "The Dunstans, the Coate sisters, Widow Ayleth with her two lads, and old Tam Sillow, who perished of a chest stoppage before daybreak. Cull Sillow was scouring the hamlets with Hask's seal, demanding the names of every carter who aided House Vale. The widow's boys would have been taken in irons before the week ended. I executed the extraction without your consent, and I report it now because it is an accomplished fact, not to request your pardon."

Casian listened with total, motionless concentration.

"Which trail did you take?" he asked calmly.

"Over the high shoulder," she answered. "Eleven miles of scree."

"Why did you bypass the drovers' highway? It saves two leagues."

"Four mounted spearmen were stationed at the willow crossing."

Casian nodded slowly. "That was the correct tactical deduction. It is the route I would have designated, and had you consulted me on the fourth of March, I would have commanded you to execute the movement on the twentieth."

Vesna stared at him, anger flaring behind her eyes.

"Why delay until the twentieth?"

"Because on the twentieth, Corin's outriders will complete the secondary timber barrier along the shoulder trail," Casian explained evenly. "There would have been two draft horses waiting at the summit to assist the carts, fresh blankets and dried meal stored in the byre, and two lads with a hand-wain to transport the elderly spinsters down the incline. I have spent eight days preparing the supply for an undertaking you executed in a single night. I would have conducted it with vastly superior preparation, you conducted it with superior speed, and an honorable old man is dead because neither of us had the humility to communicate our design."

The garden became exceedingly quiet. The wind stirred the bare twigs of the young walnut sapling.

"I would have rejected your command to wait," Vesna said bluntly.

"You would have been entirely justified," Casian agreed.

"I would have rejected it because waiting for an order has become a disease in this valley," she said, her voice rising with fierce conviction. "I have waited for two months while you weeded cabbage beds, and Tam Sillow is lying upon a bed of straw at Hanging Rake because of it."

Casian picked up the iron crowbar, resting its point against the flagged path.

"You speak the absolute truth," he said softly. "It is my greatest tactical failure, and I will inevitably repeat the error in the years ahead. I request that you repeat that condemnation before our council this evening so that I am not permitted to forget it."

---

The council convened in the mill office that evening, attended by Corin, Maeve, Kael, and the Baroness Calla of Fenwick, who had ridden south to inspect the flour shipments.

Vesna stood before the timber desk, her hands resting near her knife-sheath, addressing the assembled elders with unyielding defiance.

"I will not petition for leave again," she declared, looking directly into Casian's grey eyes. "If an emergency arises along the high crags, I will act upon my own judgment and report the consequence when the maneuver is complete. If that autonomy violates your ledger, strike my name from your book and I will return to my mother's hearth."

"That is acceptable," Casian replied from his stool.

Vesna blinked, thrown off balance. "You are supposed to debate the principle."

"I have spent the afternoon being instructed in my own deficiencies, and I see no profit in arguing against sound counsel," Casian said with a dry, self-deprecating smile. "What further indictment do you carry from the hills?"

Vesna took a slow, deep breath, voicing the grievance she had carried across eleven miles of freezing bog.

"Nobody in those mountains understands what you are for," she said, her words cutting through the warmth of the room. "They observe the grain drays of Corran and Son on the highway. They see the millrace cleared and the wheel turning. They know eleven captives returned from the ridge, and there are four hundred peasants who will whisper extraordinary tales about the uncanny fourth son of House Vale. Yet not a single freeholder could state what you actually seek."

She pointed an ink-stained finger toward the window.

"The warlord's riders are demanding to know who collaborated with the Vale. Within two months, you will have a province that regards your name as a terrifying curse, and an ecclesiastical court arriving to investigate a heresy. And it is occurring because you have accomplished thirty subtle deeds while maintaining absolute silence. A power that refuses to state its purpose is a power around which other men invent monstrous fables."

Maeve Corran set down her cider cup, exchanging a grave glance with Calla.

"And what would you have me declare to the valley?" Casian asked quietly.

"I would have you declare to four hundred people the identical promise you spoke to me in October," Vesna demanded. "State aloud that the turnpikes belong to your protection, that an honest merchant will not be robbed, and that a widow in an isolated byre will not be terrorized by armed mercenaries bearing a baron's seal. Proclaim it in broad daylight where ordinary folk can hear the syllables. Not inside an aristocratic hall."

A profound stillness settled over the room.

"You have just articulated the definition of a territorial boundary," remarked the Baroness Calla, her sharp hazel eyes assessing the upland girl with intense interest. "And the moment he speaks those words, he ceases to be an unlanded fourth son. He becomes a sovereign lord."

"He will become a lord whose neighbors understand his purpose," Vesna countered.

---

On the following morning, Vesna approached the eldest Coate sister at the Hanging Rake shelter, holding a clean sheet of vellum and an ink-horn.

The old spinster possessed a rough, calloused hand that could wield a goose quill—a rare accomplishment among marcher women, which Vesna had known of for years and never previously valued.

"Inscribe four names upon this parchment," Vesna requested, setting the horn upon the stone bench.

"Recite the families," the old woman said, dipping the nib.

"Inscribe Ayleth, Dunstan, Coate, and Sillow."

The spinster penned the characters slowly, her lips moving in silent concentration, before returning the quill.

Vesna studied the dark ink upon the sheepskin.

"Four families," she murmured. "Represented by four marks."

"They are not merely four marks," the old woman remarked gently. "They represent four lineages entered into the official register of this shelter. When royal bailiffs or marcher captains arrive in five winters to demand who holds title to these sheepfolds, that parchment is evidence that ordinary folk lived, laboured, and were protected upon this ground."

She turned the quill in her fingers, offering the shaft to the girl.

"You must learn the craft yourself, Vesna. Not the complete alphabet; that requires years of schooling. But you must learn to forge the specific characters of your own name. When an armed officer thrusts an imperial decree before your face, you must be capable of placing your own mark upon the parchment rather than surrendering a peasant's thumb-print."

Vesna Orrow took the goose quill, feeling its unnatural lightness in fingers accustomed to yew bows and hunting knives.

"Instruct me," she said.

It required nine days of grueling, clumsy labor. She tore three sheets of scrap parchment, spilled ink upon her woolen apron, and cursed her stiff knuckles.

Yet on the tenth of March, seated at the timber table in the Hanging Rake byre, she inscribed a jagged *V*, an awkward *E*, and a resolute, sharp stroke that completed the cipher of her identity.

She placed her signature in the left-hand margin, directly adjacent to the four names of the families whose lives she had chosen to preserve.

Maeve Corran inspected the entry two days later during her supply round, nodded once in silent approval, and turned the page without uttering a syllable of praise—and for that unadorned respect, Vesna was profoundly grateful.

---

On the eleventh of March, Vesna stood beside the freshly dug mound in the courtyard of the old chapel at Hanging Rake.

Corin had dispatched two Vale armsmen with a hand-wain loaded with timber planks, a sack of barley meal, and two kegs of salt. Nessa had traveled north from the surgery to dress the frost-cracked heels of the Coate sisters and administer willow-bark tonic to the Dunstan infant.

Vesna stood before the weathered fieldstone cross and recited the burial psalm over Tam Sillow's grave. Her delivery was halting and rough, and the eldest Coate sister was obliged to prompt her through the second stanza, yet she spoke every line to its conclusion.

When the final prayer was completed, the old spinster touched Vesna's sleeve.

"You omitted the third blessing," the old woman muttered, though her tone held no reproof. "Yet you were present at our threshold when the horsemen were saddling their mounts, and no lord commanded you to ride. We will remember that upon the ridges."

Vesna walked away from the graveside, leaning against the drystone wall and gazing out over the vast, windswept basin of the eastern march.

A single tear tracked through the limestone grit upon her cheek. She wiped it away with the back of her woolen glove, drew her hunting knife from its sheath, and checked the edge against her thumb.

She was no longer an obedient runner waiting upon an errand boy's convenience.

She had chosen the ground; she had carried the dying; and she had inscribed her mark upon the ledger of the living.

She turned back toward the shelter to inspect the hand-cart, ready for whatever storm the spring might bring down from the hills.
