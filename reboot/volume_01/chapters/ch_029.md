Vesna had been given nothing to do for nine weeks and it was making her strange.

That was not a thing she said. She had been, since October, the person who walked: nineteen miles of the cut, then eleven gates, then every fold and byre and storehouse from the Veyr to the Fenwick boundary, twice, in weather. She had a book with a list of names in it and the names had been got out of her by asking, and the writing had been done by Corin because she could not write and had not yet said so out loud in a room with a baron's son in it.

And since the middle of January nobody had asked her to walk anywhere.

She had said, in December, in a room: *there's three families above the ford who've been talking to your carters.* And Casian Vale had said thank you, and written it down, and then nothing had happened, and she had gone back up the hill and waited to be asked, because that was what you did.

Then in February nine men took Bradey's and the bell rang, and in the last week of February nineteen men went off a road in daylight, and in the first week of March there were men in the hills asking questions with a chieftain's authority behind them.

She heard about the questions from a boy who had run eleven miles.

---

The questions were these, and they were being asked in a way that Vesna recognised because she had been asked them herself, by her aunt, when she was nine, about a broken yoke.

*Who has been talking to the Vale?*

*Who was it that knew about the barn?*

*Who has been down the cut and come back with money?*

The man asking was called Cull Sillow, and the Sillows had been in that valley as long as the stones, and he was asking politely with four men behind him and a paper in his hand, and the paper was the chieftain's and it had a seal on it.

Three days of that, in six hamlets.

And on the third night a woman called Ayleth, who was a widow with two sons and a byre and eleven sheep, walked four miles in the dark to Vesna's mother's house and stood in the door and said, "They'll come back for my boys."

"They won't."

"They will," said the widow. "Because my eldest was on the road the night they carried the eleven down, and he's a boy who can't keep his mouth shut when he's had a cup, and if a man asks him a question in the right voice he'll say the whole of it and then he'll cry."

Vesna stood in her mother's door and heard that, and something happened in her that she would think about for years and describe badly every time.

*Nobody has told me what to do,* she thought, *and nobody is going to, and there's a paper with a seal on it going round the hills for people who talked to a carter.*

---

She went to the widow's byre at first light with a cart and a boy and no instructions and moved them.

That was the whole of the choice; everything after it was arithmetic.

By noon she had four families — the widow and her two sons, the Dunstans who were six, the two Coate sisters who were old and had a cow, and a man called Tam Sillow who was seventy-four, who was a distant kinsman of the man with the paper and mentioned it exactly once, who was the best basket-maker in the marches, and who had a chest complaint that everybody knew about and nobody mentioned.

She took them up over the shoulder and down the long way, to the fold at Hanging Rake, which was one of the eleven places, and which Corin had made into a thing worth being by mending the roof and the wall and leaving the gap in the wall where a man could lie and see the road.

It was eleven miles by that route. Nine by the drove.

She took the road because the drove went past the ford and the ford had four men on it.

---

Here is what Vesna would say about it afterward, in the flat way she said everything, to a room that did not want to hear it.

The widow's cart lost a wheel at the top of the shoulder and they spent an hour mending it with a hurdle and a length of rope and it did not hold and they had to walk the rest with the load on their backs.

The two Coate sisters could not walk eleven miles.

The Dunstans had a boy of two who cried for six miles and then stopped crying and went quiet and grey and Vesna had to carry him the last mile and she was so frightened that she was angry and said a thing to his mother she was ashamed of for a year.

And Tam Sillow walked the first four miles without saying anything and then sat down on a wall and said he'd wait and be along.

"You'll not be along," said Vesna.

"I'll be along," said Tam Sillow, who had made baskets for fifty years and had buried a wife and three children and was not going to be argued with by a girl of nineteen on a wall.

He was along at Hanging Rake at the middle of the night, and he was alive, and he sat down by the fire, and he died in the morning before it was light, with his boots off and his hands folded, and the two Coate sisters said the psalm over him.

The thing Vesna could not get past was that he had not been killed. Nobody had done anything to him. He had been seventy-four and he had walked nine miles in a March night because a girl of nineteen had decided at first light that the widow's boys had to be off the hill by dark, and his heart had stopped in a byre with a good roof on it.

*I did that,* she thought, sitting on the wall at Hanging Rake with the sun coming up. *I did that and nobody told me to and if I'd waited to be asked he'd have died in his own bed in a year, or he'd have been taken and beaten by a man with a paper, and I don't know which, and I'll never know which, and I have to be a person who can hold that.*

---

She came down to the mill on the ninth of March and Corin Ahl was in the yard with a tally-board and she stood in front of him and said, "I've moved four families to Hanging Rake and one of them's dead."

Corin put the board down.

"Dead how?"

"Old. Walking. Mine." Vesna's jaw was set. "I want to see the master."

"He's at the garden."

"Then I'll go to the garden."

Corin looked at her for a moment and then picked the board up again and said, "I'll walk up with you," which was, from Corin Ahl, an act of considerable courage in both directions.

---

Casian was at the east wall with a bar and a crowbar and three boys, and he was doing a thing with a stone that Vesna did not understand and did not ask about, and he put the bar down when he saw them coming and wiped his hands on his coat.

"Vesna."

"Four families off the hill to Hanging Rake. Dunstans, Coates, Ayleth, and Tam Sillow, who died in the night. The widow's boys were being asked questions by a man with the chieftain's paper and they'd have been taken by the week's end." She said it all in one go and then stood there with her hands at her sides. "I did it without being asked. I'm telling you now because it's done and not because I want leave."

Casian Vale did not say anything for a moment.

"Show me the road you took," he said.

"Over the shoulder. Eleven miles."

"Why not the drove? It's nine."

"There were four men at the ford."

"Good," he said. "That's the right answer and it's the answer I'd have given, and if you'd asked me on the fourth of March I'd have told you to go on the twentieth."

Vesna stared at him.

"Why the twentieth?"

"Because on the twentieth the road over the shoulder was going to be closed and there'd have been men at Hanging Rake to meet you and a cart at the top of the shoulder and a man with a hand-cart to take the two old women down." He said it plainly, without any softening. "I've spent eight days arranging a thing you did in a morning, and I'd have done it better and you'd have done it sooner, and a man is dead because neither of us told the other what we were about."

There was a silence in the walled garden.

"I'd have said no to you," said Vesna.

"You'd have been right to."

"I'd have said no to you because you'd have told me to wait to be asked," said Vesna, "and I've been waiting to be asked since January and there's a man in a byre at Hanging Rake who's dead of it."

Casian picked the crowbar up and set it against the wall and then leaned on it.

"Yes," he said. "That's true, and it's the thing I'll be worst at for years, and I'd like you to say it to me again in front of other people so that I don't forget it."

---

She said the rest of it in the mill, that evening, in front of Corin and Maeve and Kael and a woman from Fenwick whose name she did not catch, which was not a thing she had planned and which she did because he had asked her to.

"I'll not ask you again," she said. "Not for a thing like that. If a thing needs doing on the hill I'll do it and I'll tell you after, and if you don't like it you can put me out of the book."

"All right."

"That's not — " Vesna stopped. "You're supposed to argue."

"I've had one day of being told what I'm worst at and I'd like to get the value of it," said Casian. "What else?"

And Vesna, who had been thinking about it for eleven miles with a dying man on a cart, said the thing she had come down the hill to say.

"Nobody in those hills knows what you're for. They know what Corran and Son does and they know the mill's got a race on it now and they know the eleven came back, and there's four hundred of them who'll tell you a story about the fourth son of Vale and not one of them could tell you what he wants." She looked at him. "The chieftain's men are asking who talked to the Vale. You'll have a county that knows your name and thinks you're a curse and a Church that'll be told it in the spring, and it's because you've done a great many things and said nothing, and a thing that says nothing is a thing that gets a story told about it."

Maeve Corran, in the corner, put her cup down very quietly.

"And what would you have me say?" said Casian.

"I'd have you say to four hundred people what you said to me at the mill in October," said Vesna. "That the roads are yours to keep and a man on them won't be robbed, and that a woman in a byre with two sons isn't going to be asked questions with a paper in her face. Say it where they can hear it. Not in a hall."

There was a silence in the mill.

"You've just described a boundary," said the woman from Fenwick, who was Calla, and who had not been introduced. "And if he says it, he'll be a baron."

"He'll be a baron with four hundred people who know what he's for," said Vesna.

---

She asked the eldest Coate sister, on the morning of the eighth, to write four names in the book for her.

The old woman had a hand like a fence-post and could write, which in the marches was a thing about one woman in forty could do, and which Vesna had known for eleven years and had never once used.

"Say them out and I'll put them down."

"Put down Ayleth, and Dunstan, and Coate, and Sillow."

The old woman wrote them, slowly, with her tongue between her teeth, and then read them back, and Vesna looked at the four words on the page for a long time.

"That's them," she said. "That's four families and they're four words."

"It's not four words, it's a page in a book with the mill's mark on the cover," said the old sister, "and a page in a mill's book is a thing a man can find in nine years when he wants to know who was where."

"Will they find it?"

"They'll find it if it's wrote plain and there's somebody at the mill who knows what it means." The old woman handed the pen over. "You'll learn to do it yourself. Not this year. Not the letters. But you'll learn to make the four marks of your own name, and then when a man puts a paper in front of you you'll do something to it that isn't a thumb-print."

Vesna Orrow looked at the pen.

"Show me," she said.

It took her eleven days and she did it wrong for nine of them, and on the tenth of March, in a room at the mill, she made a V and a shape that was meant to be an E and a stroke that could have been anything, in the margin of a page, next to four names that were her responsibility.

Maeve Corran saw it and said nothing about it at all, which was why Vesna liked her.

---

She went back up the hill on the tenth with a cart, and the cart had a hand-cart on it and a man to pull it, and there were two men from the Vale at Hanging Rake for a fortnight and a stock of flour and a barrel of salt and a woman from Fenwick who knew about chests and had come without being asked by anybody, which Vesna thought about for a long time.

And in the chapel at Hanging Rake — which was not a chapel any more and had a roof on it now — she said the psalm over Tam Sillow herself, badly, because she had never done it before and the Coate sisters had to help her.

Afterward the eldest Coate sister said, "You did it wrong. It's the second verse first at a burial and everybody in the marches knows that."

"I know," said Vesna. "I'll learn it."

"See that you do," said the old woman, and then, after a moment, and with a lifetime of not saying things behind it: "We'd have been took. The three of us. That man's a Sillow and the Sillows hold a grudge the way other men hold land." She looked at the fire. "You were here at first light and nobody sent you. I'll be telling people that."

Vesna went out and stood in the cold and found that she was crying, which she had not done since she was eleven, and she did it for about a minute and then stopped, and went to see about the hand-cart.

---

*Twenty-seven,* said Argius, in the garden, a week later, when Casian said the number aloud for the first time in ten days. *And the twenty-eighth is a woman of nineteen who'll be giving you orders by the summer, and you'll take them, and it will be the best thing that happens to you this year.*

*She'll hate that.*

"She will not be told that," said Argius. "She will be told that she was right about Tam Sillow, which she was, and that she was right about the fourth son of Vale needing to say what he is for, which she was, and then you will do the thing she asked and she will never forgive you for it, because it will work."
