Corin Ahl had spent eighteen years in the service of House Vale as an armorer and scout whose duty was to uncover secrets, and in the third week of March he was commanded to execute the exact inverse of his nature.

"Close the roads," Casian said, leaning over the timber table in the mill office.

"All of them?" Corin asked, pausing with his horn stylus suspended above the parchment.

"All of them save one."

They were standing in the small, flour-dusted tally-room of the Vale water-mill. Upon the lime-washed wall hung an sheepskin map of the eastern marches—a masterwork of tactical geometry that had required Corin nine years of patient footwork, endless conversations with roadside teamsters, and dozens of rough chalk sketches to compile. He valued that map with the fierce, possessive pride of a master mason regarding a vaulted arch he had trued with his own plumb-line.

"Which passage remains open?" Corin inquired.

Casian reached out, resting his forefinger upon the ragged, faded line of the upland drove.

Corin studied the track for several long heartbeats. It was an ancient, sunken drovers' path running from the southwestern peat-fens across the desolate expanse of the Common, descending into the eastern strip through a rocky ravine known as Hollow Beck. It was universally acknowledged as the most treacherous, waterlogged highway in the entire province. It possessed no roadside inns, no tenant cottages, and no drystone fieldfolds; for six leagues it was bordered by nothing save stagnant alder swamps and black heather. It was the solitary artery in the marches that could not be easily watched, because no honest traveller chose to ride it.

"You intend to channel Hask Veyr's cavalry across the Common drove," Corin said.

"I intend to leave Hask Veyr with one single entrance into this valley," Casian replied quietly. "And I want him to recognize that it is the worst possible approach. I want him to choose it because every alternative has been systematically obstructed, and I want him to congratulate himself upon his tactical cunning."

---

The operation occupied eleven days, consisting outwardly of eleven entirely mundane, lawful administrative events.

**The gravel ford below Hollow Beck** was terminated by the miller's formal petition. The new millrace had been excavated in January, the volume of water diverted from the upper stream had increased, and the winter current had scoured the gravel bed until the crossing was hazardous to heavily loaded wagons. Maeve Corran lodged a complaint with the peace bailiff at Harrowmere; a wooden warning hurdle was erected on the southern approach, a notice was affixed to the parish church door at Ashgrove, and a parish beadle was stationed on the bank with an iron-tipped measuring pole for a fortnight to turn back freight drays.

**The Middle Drove** was obstructed by the resolution of an ancient boundary quarrel between two adjacent manors. The freeholders of Fenwick and the tenants of the lower marsh had disputed six acres of grazing turf along the ditch for nine years—a bitter, tedious feud involving broken hurdles, impounded sheep, and seasonal brawling. Corin rode out to the disputed boundary on a Tuesday afternoon, accompanied by two neutral surveyors, and arbitrated the division in three hours of blunt, unyielding negotiation. He awarded the northern ditch to Fenwick, granted the water-gate to the marshmen, and ran the newly mandated boundary ditch and gorse fence directly across the turnpike. Both factions departed convinced they had outmaneuvered their rivals. The Baroness Calla of Fenwick, reviewing the signed agreement that evening, remarked dryly: "That was the most economical afternoon of arbitration in this county's history, and I am entirely certain I do not comprehend what I just purchased."

**The Ashgrove highway**, the broad, stone-surfaced turnpike that Hask's mounted columns had traversed on previous incursions, was closed by royal authority. An ancient two-arch timber bridge across the Ashford creek was condemned in late February by an ecclesiastical surveyor dispatched from Harrowmere. The complaint regarding decayed oak piles was entirely legitimate; the surveyor was an incorruptible clerk; and the carter who had pointed out the rotten timber had received four silver marks from an anonymous merchant at the crossroad tavern to ensure the petition reached the surveyor's desk.

**The high border cut**—the rocky escarpment with its eleven ancient gates that Vesna had traversed in the sleet—was left completely unobstructed. It was merely observed. From the first of March, thirteen concealed observation posts were established along the crest. Corin had selected each vantage point himself, having spent freezing nights crouching in the brush: hollowed-out turf banks, collapsed shepherd hurdles, and fissures in the granite scree where a young scout could lie wrapped in a dun wool cloak, overlook three hundred paces of the trail, and remain utterly invisible from below. Kael commanded the relay, stationing two nimble runners at the lower timber barn to carry word the instant a bridle jangled on the heights.

**And the Common drove** was left in its natural squalor: deeply rutted by autumn cattle drives, flooded to a depth of three feet at the fourth mile where the drainage culvert had collapsed, bordered by neither hedge nor stone wall, and terminating at the neglected water-mill on the eastern strip.

Except that during the first week of March, working under cover of early morning fog, Corin had led four trusted draymen from Corran and Son to the flooded fourth mile. They had laid down eighty bundles of stout birch faggots, surfaced the quagmire with three cartloads of dry river gravel, and bridged the deepest ditch with four-inch oak baulks. The entire operation was conducted in broad daylight, witnessed only by grazing sheep, and entered in the firm's municipal day-book as: *Carriage and causeway repair, four days' labor, eight silver marks, Fenwick boundary to Hollow Beck.*

---

"You are not closing roads, Corin," Maeve Corran remarked on the eighth evening, examining the ink tallies in her ledger. "You are isolating a single route, improving its foundation in secret, and concealing the trap behind an account roll."

"I am concealing nothing," Corin replied, resting his forearms upon the walnut desk. "I am recording every groat in a licensed ledger. That is the definition of public governance."

"Do not attempt to be clever with me," Maeve countered, dipping her quill. "You sound uncomfortably like young Casian."

"A clandestine maneuver that leaves no paper trail is an invitation to discovery," Corin said. "A road repaired under an official commercial drayage license, with tolls paid at the parish gate and laborers' wages entered in the church roll, is simply a boring municipal improvement. Men of power scrutinize mysteries, Maeve; they never inspect a gravel bill."

Maeve looked at him over the rims of her iron spectacles, her expression softening with genuine warmth.

"Your father would have been proud of that reasoning," she said quietly. "He would have sworn you took after your mother's kin to avoid admitting you possessed his own methodical stubbornness, but he would have slept soundly."

---

The second requirement of the campaign was considerably more delicate, demanding that Corin establish eleven emergency waystations along the perimeter.

In December, the eleven redoubts had been engineered as austere military hides—rebuilt sheepfolds where an outrider could lie concealed behind a drystone wall. By mid-March, each position had to be transformed into an operational sanctuary capable of sustaining non-combatants: dry thatch overhead, stored firewood, casks of potable water, and bags of roasted barley meal.

A storehouse required a custodian—someone capable of denying entry to a panicking mob while distributing bread to exhausted outriders—and Corin selected the eleven keepers one by one.

At Hanging Rake, Widow Ayleth maintained the fold, possessing a fierce, protective resolve born of nearly losing her sons to the raiders.

At the water-mill, Maeve presided over the grain bins with eighteen years of commercial authority.

At the Fenwick turn, an elderly carter named Osgood, who walked with a severe limp from an overturned wain and had traversed the county roads for thirty-five winters, guarded the timber shed.

At the Ashgrove crossroad, an eccentric apiarist named Tolly, who despised his neighbors and maintained immaculate records of seasonal honey yields, agreed to oversee the storehouse simply because Corin promised that nobody would be permitted to speak to him.

And Watt, the taciturn innkeeper at the Ashgrove taproom, was the final and most crucial link in the chain.

Watt was sixty-one years old. He had managed the timber tavern at the junction of the two main highways for nineteen seasons, and for eleven of those years he had maintained a private tally of every freight wagon, royal courier, and armed detachment that halted before his horse-troughs. Those nineteen oilskin notebooks, tucked beneath the oak counter beside his ale-taps, were the very foundation upon which Corin had constructed his tactical map of the province.

Corin entered the taproom on the evening of the fourteenth of March, ordering a flagon of small beer and seating himself at the far end of the scrubbed trestle board.

"I have anticipated this visit for a fortnight, armsman," Watt said, wiping the counter with a greasy linen rag.

"The Common drove will be reinforced through the fourth mile," Corin said in an undertone. "The bridge at Ashford will remain closed until Michaelmas."

"Who pays for the gravel?"

"Corran and Son. Eight marks, registered in the county roll."

Watt paused in his wiping, his rheumy eyes studying Corin's face.

"And what is the underlying design, Corin?"

"A mounted column of northern mercenaries will descend through these marshes before the spring thaw," Corin answered, keeping his voice level. "We have prepared the Common drove so that it will bear their horseflesh, and we have closed every alternative passage so that their lances have nowhere else to deploy. When they arrive, four hundred tenants will demand to know why the worst road in the valley was repaired by a Vale boy's enterprise. I am informing you tonight because you maintain the road tallies, you would have deduced the pattern within three days, and I would rather face an honest neighbor than a suspicious accountant."

Watt set the cloth upon the timber board, staring out the leaded casement into the rain.

"You would have been wiser to leave an old innkeeper in ignorance," Watt murmured.

"I disagree," Corin said. "An ignorant man panics when the horses begin to scream."

"Aye," Watt admitted after a long silence. He reached beneath the counter, extracted his current ledger, and pushed it across the wood. "Nineteen years the wool drays have rattled past this porch. Eleven years I have inscribed their waybills. In February, a royal surveyor condemned the stone bridge, and the freight was diverted through the fens. I recorded the detour as an ordinary commercial inconvenience, because that was the truth upon the ground."

"And when the commissioners arrive?"

"When the royal marshals halt at my horse-trough in May, they will demand my opinion of the bridge," Watt said, his weathered jaw setting firm. "And I will tell them the unvarnished truth: the timber was rotten, the stones were displaced, and the road was diverted by an honest magistrate who understood his craft. It is a rare privilege for a tapster to speak the complete truth to a noble magistrate. I am sixty-one, Corin, and I have not had the opportunity nearly often enough."

---

At the ford below Hollow Beck, where the drove crossed the shallow stream, Corin had been unable to locate a reliable tenant to oversee the crossing. He had visited the abandoned gravel bank three times in February, finding only overgrown rushes and the collapsed masonry of an ancient salt-house that had stood vacant for forty years.

When he returned on the sixteenth of March, he discovered that the salt-house had acquired a new roof.

Fresh reed thatch covered the rafters, smoke drifted from a crude clay chimney, and two lean mountain cows were tethered in the willow scrub. A hand-cart stood beside the doorway, and an iron kettle was boiling over an outdoor hearth.

A broad-shouldered man of forty, with calloused hands and an old scar across his cheekbone, stepped out to meet him, holding a wood-splitting adze.

"What is your business upon this bank?" Corin asked, reining in his gelding.

"Our business is living, master," the man replied calmly. His name was Hollis Wray. He had come down from the high northern hills in February with his wife, two young children, and a hand-cart. He carried himself with the quiet, watchful wariness of an upland farmer who had witnessed the destruction of other men's roofs and understood the necessity of keeping his back to the wall.

"Who authorized you to settle upon this boundary?" Corin inquired.

"Your freight carters halted at the crossing three weeks ago," Hollis answered, resting his adze against a timber post. "They observed my boy shivering by the ditch and gave him a bowl of hot porridge and a woolen scarf. My wife inquired what freight they were hauling, and the carter answered that a miller at Vale kept the grain. She asked who governed the valley, and the carter replied that nobody would believe him if he recited the truth."

Hollis spat into the mud, crossing his arms.

"We walked down from the cut the following morning. There is the Common drove to the west, the escarpment above, and the gravel ford at our feet. A crossing with an inhabited cottage is twice as secure as an empty ditch. We laid thatch upon the rafters, cleared the stones, and here we remain."

"And what do you expect from House Vale?"

"Not a brass farthing," Hollis Wray said flatly. "And that is not peasant humility; it is the truth. I was one of the eleven captives dragged off the Lowmere ridge in October. I sat in an upland barn for four nights expecting to be sold south as a bond-slave, and something moved through the dark and carried my family off the hill without a single blow being struck. I have spent five months desiring nothing from any baron, duke, or warlord, and I intend to preserve that quiet upon this bank."

---

Corin returned to his cottage at midnight on the sixteenth, slumping into the oak chair beside the hearth, entirely devoid of appetite.

His wife, Ivet, set a bowl of cold mutton broth before him, folded her apron across her lap, and studied his haggard features.

"You have been away fourteen hours," she said quietly.

"I have spent a fortnight transforming an entire province into a military bottleneck," Corin muttered, staring into the dark broth. "And the keystone of the entire trap is an upland refugee who re-thatched an abandoned salt-house because our carter gave his boy a bowl of porridge."

Ivet watched her husband, the firelight catching the grey streaks in her dark hair.

"Corin."

"An armed engagement will occur along that stream within three weeks," he said, his voice dropping into a register of heavy, agonizing guilt. "Hask's column will enter this valley across the Common drove, because I have closed every other avenue of retreat. And I was proud of the craftsmanship, Ivet. I rode home this afternoon feeling the terrible vanity of an armorer who has forged an unyielding breastplate. I built a snare out of legal petitions, road dockets, and an old man's ledgers, and not a soul in this county can decipher what has been done."

He gripped his rough hair with both hands.

"And the first souls who will witness two hundred armed cavalrymen charging through the mist are Hollis Wray, his wife, and two children under six years of age."

Ivet reached across the table, covering his calloused knuckles with her steady, warm hands.

"What would you have preferred, husband?" she asked softly. "Would you prefer that Hask's raiders descended upon our own village at midnight, burning the thatch over our daughters' beds while you waited for a royal herald from Harven?"

"No," he whispered.

"Then go to the garden tomorrow," she instructed him, her tone firm and unyielding. "Confront the boy. You have served this house for eighteen years, Corin. You are entitled to ask what price he intends to extract from the folk who dwell upon his road."

---

On the morning of the seventeenth of March, Corin walked up to the postern gate of the walled garden, halting in the muddy lane without crossing the threshold.

Casian was kneeling beside the young walnut sapling, examining the tender green buds that had begun to swell beneath the wicker protective wrapping.

"Move the family at the Hollow Beck ford," Corin said without preamble. "Fabricate an excuse—a hiring fair at Harrowmere, a livestock muster, anything that will clear that gravel bank for ten days."

Casian rose, brushing damp garden loam from his woolen knees.

"No, Corin," he replied gently.

"Master, they are non-combatants," Corin protested, his voice tightening. "A man, his wife, and two small children."

"If we evacuate that cottage on the eve of the engagement, every peasant in these marches will deduce that the road was engineered as a slaughterhouse," Casian explained, meeting Corin's gaze with absolute, unblinking clarity. "We will have executed a brilliant tactical trick, and every neighbor from here to the coast will recognize that it was a trick. They will look upon our house with terror, and when the next border warlord rises, nobody will trust our word."

He stepped toward the stone gate.

"And furthermore, Hollis Wray would understand that his family was manipulated as an expendable lure rather than treated as sovereign human beings. I will not be the commander who commits that betrayal in this valley."

Corin stood rooted in the mud, feeling the cold wind off the moors cut through his leather vest.

"Then explain what you intend to do when the lances cross the stream," Corin challenged him. "Because I have constructed your road, and I have an obligation to lead my own wife and daughters past the end of it."

Casian stepped through the gate—an action he had never previously taken—and halted beside Corin in the open yard.

"I do not possess the complete design yet," Casian admitted, his voice quiet, steady, and entirely devoid of arrogance. "I know only this: when the column approaches the ford, Hollis Wray will be informed of their strength, he will be told the truth of what is occurring, and he will be granted the freedom to choose whether he stands or retreats. Whatever fate befalls the rest of this barony will befall the salt-house as well. If I make an artificial exception of that bank, I have constructed an ambitious drama rather than an honest defense."

Casian looked out across the misty fields toward the eastern strip.

"You executed a miracle during this fortnight, Corin. No armsman in this province could have achieved it. The roads are sealed. There is only one entrance remaining into our home, and it is the worst passage in the march because an official municipal ledger records that an honest carter hauled brushwood for eight marks. The arithmetic holds."

Corin stood in the damp air, feeling a sudden, unexpected stinging behind his eyelids. At thirty-five years of age, burdened by eighteen winters of guardroom duty, he felt an immense, quiet pride that he had not known since his youth.

"I should like that stated in the presence of my wife," Corin muttered gruffly.

"Come to supper on Sunday," Casian said, clapping him lightly upon his leather shoulder. "And bring the road book."
