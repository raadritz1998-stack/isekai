Corin Ahl had spent his whole life being a man who found things out, and in the middle of March he was asked to do the opposite, and it was the hardest work of his life.

"Close the roads," said Casian.

"All of them?"

"All but one."

They were standing at the mill with a map on the wall of the tally-room, and the map was Corin's and it was accurate, and it had taken him nine years and a great many conversations with carters to make it accurate, and he loved it the way a man loves a thing he made with his hands.

"Which one?"

Casian put his finger on the drove.

Corin looked at the drove for a long moment. It ran from the fen in the south-west up over the Common and came down into the eastern strip at a place called Hollow Beck, and it was the worst road in the county, and it was the one road in the whole of these marches that could not be watched, because there was nothing to watch it from and nobody lived on it.

"You want Hask Veyr to come in over the drove."

"I want Hask Veyr to have one way in and to know it's the worst way," said Casian. "I want him to choose it because it's the only one left, and I want him to be right."

---

The work took eleven days and was, on the face of it, eleven ordinary things.

**The ford below Hollow Beck** was closed by the miller's own complaint: the race had been dug, the water stood higher, the crossing was unsafe, and there was a notice at the church and a man with a pole for a fortnight.

**The Middle Drove** was closed by an argument between two villages about a boundary, which was a real argument, and had been a real argument for nine years, and which Corin settled in one afternoon in a way that happened to put the boundary fence across the drove. Both villages were satisfied. Neither of them could believe their luck. Calla of Fenwick, who was one of the two, said afterward: "That was the most expensive afternoon of my life and I don't know what I paid for."

**The Ashgrove road**, which was the good road, the one Hask's men had come down twice, was closed by the Crown: a bridge at Ashford was condemned in February by a man from Harrowmere, on a complaint about stone, and the complaint was real, and the man who made it was real, and he had been paid four marks by a carter he did not know.

**The cut** — the old way down off the hills, the gates, Vesna's eleven — was not closed at all. It was watched. There were, from the first of March, thirteen places along the cut where a boy could sit and see three hundred paces of it, and Corin had chosen the thirteen and knew every one of them because he had sat in most of them himself and Vesna had sat in the rest.

**And the drove** was left exactly as it was: rutted, flooded at the fourth mile, with no wall, no house, no fold, and nothing at the end of it but the eastern strip and the mill.

Except that Corin had, in February, in the name of Corran and Son, put a ferry plank over the beck and mended the fourth mile with brushwood, and had done it in March in front of nobody, and had entered it in the book as *carting work, four days, eight marks, Fenwick to Hollow Beck.*

---

"You're not closing a road," said Maeve, on the eighth day. "You're making a road the only one, and then making it *good*, and then telling nobody."

"I'm not telling nobody. I'm entering it in a book."

"That's not the same thing and you know it."

"It is if the book is the sort of book that gets read," said Corin, "and mine is. It's dull and it's got costs in it and it's been seen by the factor, the priest, and two men from Fenwick. A road that isn't in a book is a road somebody will remember being closed. A road that is in a book is a road that happened."

Maeve Corran looked at him across the mill floor with an expression he could not read.

"Your father would have liked you," she said. "He'd have said you took after your mother and he'd have been lying, and he'd have been pleased."

---

The other half of the work was harder and Corin did not enjoy it, which is why he did it himself.

The eleven places had been made in December so that a man could lie behind a wall and see the road. In March they were made into something else: a place to stop.

That meant water and it meant a roof and it meant a store, and a store meant a book, and a book meant a person who could say yes and no to a carter about a sack of flour, and Corin found the people one at a time and none of them were the people he would have chosen and all of them were right.

At Hanging Rake there was Ayleth, the widow, who had lost a byre and gained a byre and did not intend to lose it again.

At the mill there was Maeve, and there had been Maeve for nineteen years.

At Fenwick there was a carter called Osgood who was fifty and had a bad hip and had been on that road since he was fourteen.

At Ashgrove there was a man called Tolly who kept bees and hated everybody and kept the accounts immaculately.

And Watt, at the inn at Ashgrove, was the last of them and the one who mattered most, and Corin had been putting him off for a fortnight because he was afraid of the answer.

Watt was sixty-one and had kept the inn on the Ashgrove road for nineteen years, and he had kept a tally of the carts on that road for eleven of them, and the tally was in nineteen books under the bar and it was the reason Corin had been able to make an accurate map of a county.

Corin went in on the evening of the fourteenth and drank a cup of his own and then said, "I'm going to tell you a thing and I'd like you to hear all of it before you say anything."

"I've been waiting a fortnight for this," said Watt.

"The drove's going to be mended. Not the whole length. The fourth mile and the ford."

"Who's paying?"

"Corran and Son. Eight marks and it's in the book."

Watt wiped the bar with a cloth that had been doing that job for some years.

"And what's it for?"

"It's for a column of men to come down in March," said Corin Ahl, "and I've mended it so that it'll hold, and there'll be four hundred people who'll want to know why the drove was mended in the spring, and I'm telling you now because you keep the tally and you'd have known inside a fortnight and you'd have been right."

Watt put the cloth down.

"You'd have been better not telling me."

"I'd not."

"No," said Watt, after a moment. "You'd not." He reached under the bar and took out the current book and opened it on the wood and turned it round and pushed it at Corin. "Nineteen years the trade's gone up the Ashgrove road. Eleven years of it I've wrote down. And in February there's a bridge condemned by a man from Harrowmere and by the fourth week the carts are going round by the fen, and I've written that down too, and I wrote it in the ordinary way because that's what it was."

"And?"

"And in the spring there'll be a man from Harrowmere come round asking about that bridge and he'll come in here and have a cup and ask me what I remember," said Watt, "and I'll tell him the truth, which is that the trade went round by the fen in the fourth week of February and the bridge was condemned by a man who was right about the stone." He closed the book. "It's a good thing to be able to tell the truth. I'm sixty-one and I've not often been able to."

---
At the ford below Hollow Beck there was no place and no person, and Corin went there three times and looked at it and could not make it work, and then on the third visit he found that a family had come.

They had come in February from the north, from over the cut, and they had two cows and a boy and a girl and a hand-cart, and they had put a roof on the old salt-house which nobody had used for forty years, and they were there, and they had not been sent by Corin or by Casian or by Vesna or by anybody.

"What are you doing here?" said Corin.

"Living," said the man, who was called Hollis Wray and who had come down off the cut in February with a hand-cart and two cows, and who had a way of standing in a doorway that suggested he had once stood in other doorways and had learned from them.

Corin stood in the mud and looked at the salt-house with a roof on it.

"Did anybody send you?"

"Your carters come up the drove in February and stopped at the ford and gave a boy a cup of something hot and my wife asked what they were carrying and they said flour, and she asked where it was from and they said, 'a man at the mill keeps it,' and she said, 'what man,' and they said, 'you'd not believe me.'" Hollis Wray shrugged. "So we came down and looked at the ford. There's the drove, and there's the cut above it, and there's the beck, and there's nothing here except a ford, and a ford with a family on it is worth two fords with nobody on them. So we've come."

"And what do you want?"

"Nothing," said Hollis Wray, "and that's not a courtesy, it's the whole of it. I've wanted things from men before. I got took out of a barn by a thing I never saw and I've had four months of wanting nothing from anybody, and I'd like that to go on."

---

Corin went home on the fifteenth of March and sat down at the table with his wife and could not eat, and she let him sit for a while and then said, "Well?"

"I've spent a fortnight making a county into an argument," said Corin Ahl, "and the thing that's finished it is a family that came on its own and put a roof on a salt-house because our carter gave a boy a cup of something hot."

His wife, who was called Ivet and who had been telling him what to do for eleven years, put the bread down.

"Corin."

"There's going to be a war in these marches before the summer and it will come in over the drove, and there's a man at the mill who has arranged for it to come in over the drove, and I have spent eleven days making sure of it." He put his hands flat on the table. "And I've been proud of it all afternoon. I came home proud of it. I've made a trap and I've made it out of roads and bread and a widow's byre, and there's not a man in the county who'll be able to say afterward what was done to him."

Ivet looked at her husband.

"What would you have done instead?"

"I don't know. Nothing. Something." He rubbed his face. "There's a family at the ford that came because a carter was kind to their boy, and they'll be the first people on that road when two hundred men come over it."

---

He went up to the garden on the seventeenth and told Casian so, in exactly that shape, standing at the gate and not coming in, because he had learned by then that the gate mattered.

Casian listened to the whole of it.

"Move them," said Corin. "On the day, before it's a day. Give them a reason — a christening, a hiring, anything that'll put them off that ford for a week."

"No."

"Master—"

"If they're off that ford and a thing comes over it, then afterward there are four hundred people in this county who can work out what a road was for," said Casian, "and I'd have made a trick and everybody would know it was a trick, and I'd have to make a bigger one next year." He was quiet for a moment. "And Hollis Wray would know that a thing was arranged around him and not with him, and I'm not going to be the man who does that twice in one county."

Corin stood in the mud at the gate of the walled garden.

"Then tell me what you're going to do," he said, "because I've made you a road and I've got to walk my own family past the end of it."

Casian came out of the garden, which he had never done, and stood next to him in the yard.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I know that on the day, whoever is on that road will be told what's happening and asked what they want to do, and I know that whatever happens to the rest of the county will happen to the ford as well, because the ford is the whole point and if I make an exception of it I've made a story instead of a road." He put his hands in his pockets. "Ask me again on the day."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It's the shape of an answer and I've got eleven days to fill it, and I'd rather tell you that than say something clever." Casian looked out at the dark coming down over the eastern strip. "Corin, you've done a thing this fortnight that no man in this county could have done and I've not said so. The roads work. There's one way in and it's the worst way and it's the worst way because a book says it was mended in February by a man who carted brushwood for eight marks, and the book is true."

Corin Ahl stood in the yard and found that his eyes were wet and that he was, at thirty-five, absurdly pleased.

"I'd like to be told that in front of Ivet," he said.

"Supper on Sunday," said Casian. "Bring the book."
