On the twenty-third of March, the Baron of Vale issued orders for an impending border siege, walked into the smoke-cured hall, and inquired mildly whether the cold mutton was the last meat remaining in the larder.

That was the tenor of their supper. Twelve souls were seated around the long oak trestle board, and the twelfth was young Emric, barely thirteen months old, who had been provided with a wooden pap-spoon and had not yet learned that an oak tabletop was not an instrument intended for percussion.

"Recite the disposition again, Gareth," Baroness Maren commanded from the head of the table. "Speak it plainly, and do not employ the word *flank*."

Gareth Vale placed his grease-stained palms flat upon the oak boards. He had occupied the outer ward since daybreak, drilling the household levies in freezing mud, and had entered the hall late, splattered to the thighs with grey clay. He had spent the week stationing thirty armsmen upon the eastern timber curtain—a deployment his younger brother had quietly advised him against.

"There are two hundred mounted fighters assembling along the high ridges," Gareth stated, his rough voice carrying the flat certainty of an officer who has counted iron spears. "Berrin's detachment of veteran mercenaries, sixty auxiliaries provided through Master Rennick's purse, and an upland levy raised from the Veyr gorge. They will descend across the Common drove because every alternative highway has been obstructed, and their vanguard will reach the gravel ford below Hollow Beck by Thursday dusk. If they press their mounts, they will surround this keep by Friday daybreak."

He leaned forward, his dark eyes challenging his father.

"If I march our thirty retainers to the Hollow Beck ford tomorrow morning, we can fortify the gravel bank with timber hurdles. Three days of forced marching through peat-bogs will exhaust their horses. We can shatter their vanguard from the heights before their baggage wains cross the stream."

"And what butcher's bill do you anticipate upon that gravel bank?" Edric asked, continuing to slice his bread with a dull clasp-knife.

"Some of our men will fall, father," Gareth conceded stiffly. "It is an armed engagement."

"I am not asking for a philosophical homily, Gareth," the baron replied, setting his knife down with deliberate calm. "I am demanding a precise accounting. If thirty of our retainers hold that ford against two hundred northern spears, how many will answer the roll on Saturday morning?"

Gareth's jaw tightened. He stared into his pewter cup.

"Twenty," he muttered. "Perhaps twenty-two, if our archers maintain their discipline."

"And what reserves remain behind them in the hills?"

"Four hundred clansmen who have not yet taken the field," Gareth admitted. "They would retreat into the crags, winter in their byres, and descend upon our sheepfolds again before harvest."

"Then the proposal is rejected," Baron Edric declared with flinty finality. "And before you grumble to your comrades that your father has grown timid in his old age, hear the truth: that is not an act of mercy; it is elementary arithmetic. I have governed this estate for thirty winters, and I will not trade eight honorable lives for two weeks of quiet on an empty road."

---

Casian remained silent throughout the initial half of the meal, an abstinence that was observed and weighed by every person at the board.

Elna observed that he ate with steady, mechanical focus. She was twelve years old, possessing an intense, trap-like memory, and she had concluded since February that her fourth brother was not entirely present in the room; he occupied a private architecture of cause and effect, and the domestic table was merely an incidental obstacle in his path.

Nessa observed that he inquired after an elderly weaver at Ashgrove whose frostbitten ankle she had dressed the previous afternoon. He inquired after the woman's health in the unhurried, gentle tone a farmer uses when evaluating the rainfall, and Nessa—twenty-one years of age, having washed the blood from Aldous's dying chest four months earlier—recognized that his quiet detachment was neither coldness nor performance, but an unyielding, terrifying focus.

Aldric, the eldest son and heir, observed that his father repeatedly sought the fourth son's counsel regarding the state of the roads while offering no inquiries to the man who would inherit the barony. Aldric sat stiffly in his chair, nursing his wine, feeling the subtle displacement of authority that had settled over the household like autumn frost.

Gareth observed that Casian had consumed four slices of mutton, had cleaned his wooden trencher, and had offered not a single syllable of triumph regarding the thirty dismissed spearmen on the wall.

Baroness Maren noted the undercurrents swirling between her sons, set down her spoon, and turned her piercing gaze upon Casian.

"Casian," his mother said quietly. "Utter whatever scheme you have constructed in your garden, or permit your brothers to digest their meat in peace."

---

Casian wiped his fingers upon a coarse linen napkin, resting his elbows upon the table.

"Hask Veyr cannot be broken at the river crossing, and he cannot be contained within his hills," Casian began, his voice calm, measured, and entirely free of rhetorical ornament. "Those military solutions fail because they measure the conflict purely in lances. Hask is marching two hundred men across eleven miles of inundated moorland where there is not a single roof, chimney, or sheep-cote to provide shelter. By Friday dawn, his horsemen will have marched forty-eight hours through freezing sleet without a hot ration, and his mounts will have spent two nights standing hock-deep in peat mud."

"They are hardened mountain fighters," Gareth argued.

"They are common tenants from the northern valleys called out upon ten days' notice to seize a border mill for a warlord," Casian countered. "They have been promised a share of our winter barley, and their ranks include boys of fourteen who have never slept beyond their mothers' hearths. They are wet, miserable, and profoundly uneasy."

Casian turned his cup between his fingers.

"Therefore, our preparation is straightforward: we will bar the outer gates at dusk tomorrow, maintain the customary watch through the night, and at first light on Friday morning, we will throw the timber gates wide open."

A dead, breathless silence descended upon the long table. Even young Emric ceased beating his spoon.

"We will stoke the hearth fires in the courtyard," Casian continued, looking around the circle of faces. "We will roll out the bread barrels, unlock the granary doors, and ensure that not a single armsman of House Vale displays an iron blade or an unstrung bow anywhere that can be observed from the turnpike."

"That is not a defense," Aldric protested, color rising in his pale cheeks. "That is a shameful capitulation."

"It is an invitation to breakfast," Casian replied. "Hask Veyr will arrive before our gateway with two hundred freezing, exhausted fighters, anticipating a desperate siege against arrows and boiling pitch. Instead, he will discover an open threshold, full granaries, and the baron's family seated peacefully at their morning porridge. He will be forced to determine his conduct before his own retainers, and that is a dilemma a marcher chieftain cannot resolve in haste. Every hour he hesitates in our courtyard is an hour his followers spend consuming our warm bread, drying their boots by our fires, and smelling the smoke of an ordinary hearth."

"And if he takes us hostage?" Maren asked, her gaze fixed upon her youngest son.

"He will not take hostages," Casian answered with cold assurance. "He will claim the mill, he will proclaim authority over the turnpike, and by midday Saturday he will find himself commanding a hundred and fifty well-fed mercenaries who have not struck a single blow, who are two days' march from their homes in the rain, and who have begun to wonder why they were summoned into the lowlands to inspect an open kitchen. That is the entire design. It requires no martial genius; it requires four days of disciplined hospitality. And during those four days, the county surveyor will complete the repairs upon the Ashford bridge, and four hundred neighbors will witness what transpired within this yard."

Old Oswin, seventy-one years of age, who had observed the fortunes of House Vale for three generations, leaned forward from his corner seat.

"And what if the man simply draws his broadsword and butchers the lot of us where we sit?" Oswin asked.

"Then he is an outlaw who murdered an imperial baron's lineage under an open flag of truce," Casian said, shrugging slightly. "And Duke Varyn's commissioners will arrive at Harven in six weeks to discover that the Baron of Thornwall's client slaughtered an innocent household. Baron Osric will be formally indicted for high treason before the king's bench, and Hask's clan will be hunted through the crags like rabid wolves. I have calculated his political margin. He cannot strike a blow."

"You have calculated his margin," Oswin repeated, shaking his grey head in wonder.

"The Count has formally summoned the marcher lords to Harven to explain the carriage levies," Casian explained. "Baron Osric has registered a fraudulent claim against our eastern titles, and the absolute last catastrophe he can afford in April is a slaughtered baron upon his border. Hask has been instructed to intimidate us into compliance, not to commit a massacre. He will be remarkably courteous. He possesses no other maneuver."

---

The remainder of the evening unfolded with astonishing, deliberate ordinariness.

Casian understood the immense psychological value of that domestic calm, and he observed its preservation with profound gratitude.

Maren retrieved her wicker basket of mending, inspecting the worn knees of Brennan's wool hose by the firelight. Elna was permitted to remain at the hearth on the strict condition that she performed useful labor; she spent an hour winding coarse fleece into balls, working clumsily with her small, chapped fingers, and not a single soul corrected her tangled knots.

Emric was conveyed to his pallet by the maidservant, escaped twice into the flagged corridor, and was finally recaptured by Gareth, who hauled the gurgling infant across his muscular chest and paced the hall for twenty minutes with an expression of fierce, unyielding dignity.

Brennan, who had returned from Harrowmere that afternoon, shared the gossip of the market: the county surveyor had condemned the stone piers at Ashford, the trade wagons were detouring through the southern marsh, and the municipal guild was financing the masonry repairs out of the highway fund—news that the family received with polite indifference, unaware that the diversion had been bought with Casian's silver.

Baron Edric recounted an elaborate, humorous narrative regarding a boundary arbitration he had conducted in '09, involving two stubborn sheep-farmers, an eccentric curate, and a disputed drainage ditch. He recited the tale slowly, embellishing the dialogue with practiced relish, enjoying the familiar cadence of his own voice while his children listened in affectionate silence.

Nessa sat near the corner brazier, reviewing her collection of dried roots in an oilskin pouch. When the elder sons departed to inspect the stables, she approached Casian's seat.

"You display no apprehension," she said in an undertone.

"None," he answered.

"I have observed you since October to determine whether your calm was an adolescent posture," she remarked, closing her pouch. "It is not a posture."

"I am simply reviewing the numbers, Nessa," he said. "I felt terror only twice this winter. Once when Elna was concealed behind the boulder at the millrace, and once..." He halted, his gaze drifting toward the shadowed rafters.

"When?" she asked softly.

"When Aldous collapsed in the lane," Casian whispered. "I was crouching beside the byre, waiting for the trap to spring, and I heard the knife strike his rib. That terror was instantaneous; it lasted perhaps two seconds, and then it evaporated because there was immediate work to perform."

Nessa looked at him for several long moments, her dark eyes reflecting the dying peat coals.

"You will endure an agonizing reckoning one day, Casian," she said. "It will not occur this spring, but it will arrive, and I wish it remembered that I warned you."

"Record it in your medical journal."

"My journal is reserved for remedies," she said, rising from the bench. "Answer one question honestly. On Friday morning, when two hundred armed strangers fill our courtyard—if a drunken mercenary draws a hunting bow, or an upland reiver seeks to make a reputation—will you intervene?"

"I will."

"Before the blood falls, or afterward?"

Casian looked at the twenty-one-year-old healer who had taken up her master's surgical needles without a tremor.

"Before," he promised. "I erred upon that road once, and I have carried the corpse in my thoughts every hour since."

---

Gareth intercepted him in the courtyard after midnight, as the cold stars emerged between the scudding clouds.

"Thirty armsmen," Gareth muttered, leaning against the timber well-head. "I stationed them upon the curtain on Tuesday because you advised it, and tonight you instruct the household to open the gates on Friday. I would appreciate knowing whether this campaign is an executed strategy or an improvisation you invent as the hours pass."

"It is both," Casian answered.

"That is an intolerable ambiguity for an officer."

"On Monday, I intended to hold the Hollow Beck crossing and accept twenty casualties," Casian said, turning to face his brother. "On Tuesday, Corin completed the municipal survey of the roads and demonstrated that the Common drove could be isolated. I withheld the adjustment until tonight because a commander who revises his dispositions three times in four days loses the confidence of his men."

Gareth studied his younger brother's calm features in the starlight.

"You are sixteen years of age," Gareth said, shaking his head.

"I am aware."

"I am twenty-one," Gareth continued, his rough voice tinged with honest frustration. "I have drilled our garrison through three months of freezing sleet. I have fourteen veterans who would charge into an armed breach at my shout, and this evening you instructed the baron that our salvation lies in distributing loaves of bread like parish almoners. And the agonizing truth is that you are correct, and I have spent the afternoon feeling like a decorated ornament whose duties are utterly superfluous."

"Your duty is essential to the design, Gareth," Casian said firmly.

"Do not attempt to console me."

"I am stating a tactical reality," Casian insisted. "If our courtyard stands vacant on Friday morning, two hundred invaders will seize our father, bind his arms, and the campaign is lost before noon. What anchors our defense is your presence. On Friday dawn, I require you stationed upon the timber catwalk with thirty spearmen behind you. I want your longbow in your fist, unstrung, your boots planted upon the parapet, visible to every rider on the turnpike. And I require you to look completely, thoroughly bored."

Gareth burst into a dry, startled laugh that echoed off the stable walls.

"That assignment I can execute with perfection," Gareth said. "I have had six months of practice looking bored in this hall."

---

Casian climbed to the parapet of the eastern masonry wall at an hour past midnight.

The air was sharp and clean with the smell of approaching frost. Below him, the valley stretched away in vast, shadowed folds, the Common drove visible as a pale, ghostly ribbon winding across the black expanse of the moor toward Hollow Beck.

*State the total,* Argius murmured in the silence of his thoughts.

*Twenty-eight,* Casian replied silently. *Including Hollis Wray and the four families at Hanging Rake.*

*And on Friday?*

*Twenty-eight,* Casian repeated. *No additional names will be added to the ledger.*

*And what transformation will have occurred?*

*Four hundred inhabitants of this march will witness a border baron open his gates to an invading force and render them powerless with bread and law,* Casian said. *After Friday, not a single freeholder in this valley will believe that their survival depends upon royal heralds in Harven. They will begin to govern their own ground. That is the objective I am purchasing, and it costs a few bushels of winter rye.*

Argius was silent for the space of several breaths. In the courtyard below, an armsman coughed, and the watchman on the gate called out the hour.

*You have engineered an enterprise that will succeed provided men behave according to their nature,* Argius said. *That is the solitary species of architecture that endures, and it is also the design that collapses through circumstances that exceed your foresight. You have not yet experienced that catastrophe.*

*I experienced it at Ashgrove,* Casian countered.

*You experienced a mortal accident that possessed a single name,* Argius answered softly. *Aldous. In the decades ahead, you will confront failures that possess no names—calamities that cannot be recorded in a mill ledger—and the inability to balance those accounts will render you formidable.*

Casian rested his hands upon the cold parapet stone.

*My father warned me of that word in November,* Casian reflected. *You have repeated it since midsummer. State what you truly foresee, Argius. You have withheld the conclusion since we sat upon the garden bench.*

The night became utterly still, the wind dying among the timber eaves.

*You will not pose a danger to the tenants of this province,* Argius revealed, his voice echoing in the depths of Casian's mind. *You will be dangerous to the very premise that authority can be exercised from a distant court. Within a decade, not a single dispute in these marches will be submitted to the king's bench at Harven, because imperial justice is slow, venal, and indifferent, while the master at Vale resolves the matter in a morning. It will represent an immense blessing, it will be an order that no council ever legislated, and the morning will arrive when you yearn to lay down the burden and discover that four thousand souls will refuse to permit you to depart.*

Casian gazed out toward the pale line of the drove winding through the dark.

*Then I had better prove worthy of the trust,* Casian whispered.

*Yes,* Argius said, the resonance of his thought lingering like iron against stone. *That is the solitary honorable response, and you have spoken it without vanity. I will ensure it is remembered.*
