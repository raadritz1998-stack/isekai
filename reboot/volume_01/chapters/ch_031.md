On the twenty-third of March the Baron of Vale gave orders for a war and then came in and asked whether the mutton was the last of it.

That was the supper. Twelve at the table, and the twelfth was a boy of one who had been given a spoon and had not yet been told that a spoon is not a thing to beat a table with.

"Say it again," said Maren. "Slowly, and don't use the word flank."

Gareth put his hands on the table. He had been in the yard since noon and had come in late and muddy, and he had spent the week putting thirty men on a wall that his brother had told him not to put them on.

"There's two hundred in the hills. Berrin's lot and the men Rennick's lent him and a levy out of the Veyr country. They'll come in over the drove because the drove's the only road that isn't watched, and they'll be at the ford below Hollow Beck by Thursday, and if they push on they'll be at the mill on Friday morning." He said it flatly. "If I take thirty men and put them at the ford, they'll not get past. Three days on that road in the wet and a hundred men on a bank above it."

"And what happens to the men on the bank?"

"Some of them die."

"Yes," said Edric Vale.

"Father—"

"I'm not being cold. I'm asking a question and it's the whole of the question. If thirty men hold that ford, how many of the thirty are alive on Saturday?"

Gareth was silent.

"Twenty," he said. "Maybe twenty-two."

"And in the hills?"

"There's four hundred behind them that aren't in the field. It'd be a fortnight and then a longer fight in a worse place."

"Then no," said the Baron of Vale. "And before anyone says it, that is not a soft answer. It's arithmetic and I've been doing it for thirty years and I'd do it again."

---

Casian did not speak for most of supper, and this was noted, and every person at the table noted it differently.

Elna noted that he ate. She had been watching him since February and she had decided that her brother was not a person who was in a room with you; he was a person who was doing a thing and the room was in the way.

Nessa noted that he asked after the woman at Ashgrove with the bad leg, and asked it the way a man asks about the weather, and she had learned in the autumn that this was the most frightening thing about him and that it was not a performance.

Aldric noted that he was being ignored and that his father had asked the fourth son's opinion first about the roads and had not asked the heir's at all.

And Gareth noted that his brother had eaten four slices of mutton and had said nothing about the thirty men on the wall, which was the only decision at that table that had been overruled by somebody who was not the baron.

Maren noted all of it and said, "Casian. Say the thing you're going to say, or let us have our supper."

---

He put his knife down.

"Hask can't be stopped at the ford and he can't be stopped in the hills, and both of those are true because everybody's counting men." He looked up. "He's bringing two hundred men over a road that's eleven miles of mud with no house on it and nothing at the end of it, and by Friday morning he'll have men who haven't had a hot thing since Wednesday and haven't slept under a roof since Tuesday."

"They're soldiers," said Gareth.

"They're men from the Veyr country who were called out at a week's notice in March to take a mill from a baron, and they've been told they'll be paid out of it, and there's a boy in that column who's fourteen and has never been eleven miles from his own byre." Casian turned his cup round. "So: I want the walls manned and the gate shut, and I want the gate opened again at dawn on Friday and the fire lit and the bread out and no man of ours with a weapon in his hand anywhere that can be seen from the road."

Nobody moved.

"That's not a defence," said Aldric.

"No."

"It's a surrender."

"It's a mill with the baron's family in it and a hundred men who are cold," said Casian Vale. "He'll come to the gate at dawn with two hundred and find the door open and the granary unlocked and the family at breakfast, and he'll have to decide what he is in front of his own men, and it will not be a thing he can decide quickly, and every hour he stands in that yard deciding is an hour his men are eating our bread in our yard with the smell of it going back up the road."

"And if he takes you?" said Maren.

"He won't take me. He'll take the mill and the granary and the road, and then he'll have a hundred and fifty men who've been fed and haven't been in a fight and are two days' walk from home in the wet, and by Saturday they'll be asking what they were paid for." Casian picked the knife up again. "That's the whole plan, and it's not a clever one. It's a dull one and it takes four days, and in four days I'd like the bridge at Ashford mended and four hundred people to have heard what happened in that yard."

There was a long silence at the table.

"The family," said Edric Vale.

"All of us. In the yard. Elna and Emric included."

"Casian—"

"Father, if we sit in this house behind thirty men and let the mill be taken, then in April there's a story in the county that the Vales shut the gate and let a warlord take a mill and fed nobody, and the story will be true, and it'll be told for a hundred years." Casian looked at his father. "If we stand in that yard and hand out bread, there'll be a story in the county that a baron opened his own door to two hundred men and none of them could do anything to him, and that story's worth more than the thirty men and it'll cost less."

Oswin, who was seventy-one and had been at that table since Casian was born, said, "And what if the man simply kills you all and takes it?"

"Then he's a chieftain who killed a baron's family in a baron's mill and there's a Count in Harven in six weeks with a dead baron's forged letters in his hand and no reason at all to be merciful." Casian shrugged. "I've read his situation. He can't."

"You've read his situation," said Oswin.

"The Count's called the eastern lords to Harven in the spring about a levy nobody can account for, and the Baron of Thornwall has a finding filed against the eastern strip and two pieces of paper he'd not like read out loud, and the last thing in the world he wants in April is a dead baron." Casian said it mildly. "He'll be very polite to us. He has to be. That's the arrangement he made in the autumn and he made it without noticing."

---

The rest of the evening was ordinary, and Casian knew precisely how much that was worth and did not say.

Maren got the mending out. Elna was allowed to stay up on the condition that she did something useful, and the something useful was winding wool, and she wound it badly and nobody said so.

Emric was put to bed and got out again twice and the second time was carried down by Gareth, who was the only one of them the boy would go to, and who carried him about the hall for a quarter of an hour with a face like a wall.

Brennan had come up from Harrowmere that afternoon and had news: the bridge at Ashford was condemned by a man from Harrowmere and there was a complaint about stone and the whole thing was being mended by the county at the county's cost, which nobody in the room thought was interesting, and Casian said, "That's good," in a tone that everyone heard and nobody could place.

Edric told a long story about a drainage dispute at Harrowmere in the year '09 which he had told before and which had got better with the telling, and which was about two farmers and a ditch and a bishop's man, and which was, in its own way, about everything in that room, and he told it well and slowly and enjoyed himself.

Nessa sat with her book and asked Casian a question about the woman at Ashgrove and then, when the hall had thinned, asked a second one.

"You're not frightened."

"No."

"That's not a boast, is it," she said. "I've been watching you since October to see whether it was a boast."

"It's not a boast. I don't know what it is." Casian turned his cup. "I've been frightened twice this year and neither time was about me. Once when Elna was behind that stone, and once — " he stopped.

"Once when?"

"When Aldous was in the lane and I was in the hedge and I had already decided and was waiting," said Casian, "and I heard him fall and I knew the count was wrong. That was fear. It was very fast and it lasted about a second and a half and then it was gone and there was a great deal of work to do."

Nessa looked at him for a while.

"You'll have a bad year one day," she said, "and it won't be this one, and I'd like it on the record that I said so."

"Put it in the book."

"I'm not putting it in the book. The book is for money." She closed it. "Casian. On Friday. If a man in that yard does something you didn't arrange for — a boy with a bow, a drunk, a man who wants to be famous — will you stop it?"

"Yes."

"Before or after?"

Casian looked at his sister, who was nineteen and had been in a room with a dying man and had taken his tools.

"Before," he said, "and I've been wrong about that once and I've thought about it every day since."

---

Gareth caught him in the yard afterward.

"Thirty men," he said. "I put them on the wall on Tuesday because you told me to and then you told me to open the gate on Friday and I've not slept since, and I'd like to know whether this week was a thing you planned or a thing you've been making up as you went."

"Both."

"That's not an answer a captain can use."

"I know." Casian stopped in the middle of the yard. "The wall was Monday. On Monday I thought we'd hold the ford and lose twenty men and I'd decided that was worth it, and on Tuesday I sat in the tally-room with Corin's map and found a better one, and I did not tell you because I was not sure of it until Thursday and a captain who's given a plan and then a second plan and then a third is a captain with no men by Friday."

Gareth looked at him for a long moment.

"You're sixteen."

"I know."

"I'm twenty-one and I've been drilling that wall since November and I've got fourteen men who'd go into a fire for me and thirty more who wouldn't, and this morning you told the whole table that the thing a county needs is a baron standing in a yard handing out bread, and it was right, and I've been in the yard all afternoon feeling like a man who's been given a job that doesn't matter."

"It matters enormously."

"Don't."

"I'm not being kind," said Casian Vale. "If there's nothing behind that gate on Friday, then two hundred men have a baron for a prisoner and it's over by noon and it's my fault and it's the worst thing anybody in this family has ever done. What's behind the gate is you and thirty men on a wall that nobody is going to look at, and the reason nobody is going to look at it is that it's there." He put his hands in his pockets. "On Friday I want you on that wall with a bow in your hand and no arrow on the string, and I want you to be visible from the road, and I want you to look bored."

Gareth Vale laughed — a short, ugly, grateful noise.

"That I can do," he said. "I've had six months' practice."

---

He went out to the wall at about eleven and Argius was waiting, which was a thing he did not do, because Argius did not wait; Argius was there or was not there and it was not a matter of his choosing.

The night was cold and clear and the wall was cold through his coat. From the top of the east wall you could see the drove as a pale line going up over the Common and out of the light.

*Say the number,* said Argius.

"Twenty-eight."

*And on Friday?*

"Twenty-eight," said Casian. "Nobody new. Not on Friday."

*And what will have changed?*

"Four hundred people will have seen a baron open his door to two hundred men and hand out bread," said Casian Vale, "and after that there'll not be a man in these marches who'll believe that a thing has to be done to them from outside. They'll start doing it themselves. That's the whole of what I'm buying, and it costs a granary, and I've got a granary."

Argius was quiet for a while. Below them, in the yard, a man laughed at something and was told to be quiet.

*You have arranged a thing that will work if men behave as men,* said Argius. *That is the only kind of thing that ever works, and it is also the kind that fails for reasons that are not your fault, and you have not yet had one of those.*

"I've had one."

*You have had one and it had a name. Aldous. You will have others that don't, and you will not be able to write them in the book, and the not being able to write them is the part that will make you dangerous.*

Casian stood on the wall.

"You keep saying that word," he said. "Dangerous. Everyone in this family's said it now. My father in November, Edric in the hall in January, you since the summer." He turned. "Say the rest of it. You've been not saying it since the bench."

The night was very quiet.

*You are not going to be dangerous to the people in this county,* said Argius. *You are going to be dangerous to the idea that a thing can be settled anywhere else. In eleven years there will not be a dispute in these marches that anybody thinks to take to Harrowmere, because it will be slower and it will be worse and everybody will know a man at a mill who settles it in a morning. And that will be a good thing, and it will be a thing no one voted for, and the day will come when you want to stop and there will be four thousand people who will not let you.*

Casian looked out at the pale line of the drove.

"Then I'd better be worth it," he said.

*Yes,* said Argius. *That is the only answer there is, and you have just given it without being clever, and I am going to remember that you did.*
