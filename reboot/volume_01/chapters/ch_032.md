Tolan came back into the Vale country on the twenty-fifth of March at the head of a column of two hundred men, and he had been against it in the room and had said so, and had come anyway, and had written that down as well.

He had come back from Harrowmere nine days before with Warden Selwyn's letters in his saddlebag and had read them twice and had found them mad and careful and, in the places where they were mad, entirely correct. Then he had ridden home and been told there was a war on, and had gone to bed, and had got up and gone to it, because a man who leaves a thing in the middle is a man who has decided and Tolan had not decided.

The column was in bad shape by Thursday.

They had come down the long way, because the Ashgrove road was closed at Ashford and the ford below Hollow Beck was the only crossing with water in it that a hundred men could use, and the drove in March was eleven miles of mud with a flood at the fourth mile and no house on it anywhere.

That was the thing Tolan had said in the room and had been waved away.

"There's no house on the drove," he had said. "That's the whole of what's wrong with it. A column that goes over the drove arrives wet and empty and it arrives at the only place in the county where a man can be seen coming from a long way off."

And Osric's man had said, "We'll take the mill on the Friday and be dry by the Friday night."

---

They were at the ford at dusk on Thursday and there was a family there.

That was the first thing that was wrong and Tolan saw it before anybody, because he was in the front of the column and he had good eyes and he had been a soldier for twenty years.

There was a salt-house with a new roof on it and smoke coming out of it, and a woman in the door with her sleeves tied back, and a hand-cart, and two cows in a hurdle pen, and a boy of about ten sitting on a wall doing nothing at all.

Berrin called a halt and twenty men sat down in the mud where they stood.

"What's this?"

"A family," said Tolan.

"There's no family on the drove. There's never been a family on the drove."

"There is now," said Tolan, "and they've put a roof on the salt-house in the last six weeks, and they've got a fire, and that boy has been sitting on that wall since we came over the shoulder a mile back."

Hask rode up and looked at it.

"How long has that boy been watching us?"

"An hour at least."

"And nobody thought to mention it."

"I mentioned it," said Tolan. "Four times. You were talking about the mill."

---

The woman at the salt-house was called— he never learned her name — and she had a pot of something in which there were barley and bacon and two onions, and a stack of bread that was not new, and she sold it to them.

That was the thing.

Tolan stood in the mud and watched her sell it to them. Two pennings a cup. She had made the bread that afternoon and she had made too much of it and she had known how many men were coming because a boy from the mill had ridden up the drove on Wednesday and told her, and she had asked what she ought to do and the boy had said, *the master says it's not his to decide, and if you want to shut the door and go up the hill there's a cart and there's a byre at Hanging Rake, and if you want to stay and sell them something hot that's yours to do too.*

She had chosen to stay and sell them something hot.

Tolan had a cup of it because he was thirty-seven and wet and had learned in twenty years that a man who does not eat when there is food is a man making a point. He stood by the beck in the dark and drank it and looked at the column.

They were eating. That was all. Two hundred men in the mud, eating barley and bacon out of cups, and the ones at the back were coming up and being told to wait, and there was a queue.

*There's a queue in a field,* he thought. *We are eleven miles into a march to take a mill and there is a queue.*

---

He went to Hask at the second watch.

"Turn it round."

"Tolan."

"I'm not being careful. I've been careful for eleven years and I'm finished with it, and this is not caution, it's a thing I can see." Tolan crouched by the fire. "We've come over the worst road in the county and there's a family sitting on it selling us supper, and nobody has seen a single Vale man for two days, not on the shoulder, not on the cut, not at the crossing. There's supposed to be a wall with thirty men on it and I have not seen a man on that wall."

"They've shut the gate."

"Then where are the outriders? A baron who's frightened puts outriders out eight miles. There's been nothing. Nothing for two days." Tolan looked at him. "Chieftain. A man who shuts his gate and waits behind a wall sends out horsemen to see where the army is. A man who has decided something doesn't need to."

Hask Veyr looked into the fire for a while.

"And what has he decided?"

"I don't know," said Tolan. "That's why I'd turn it round."

---

At dawn on Friday they came down the last mile and found the gate open.

The mill at Ashgrove stood at the bottom of a shallow hill with a wall round the yard and a gate in the wall, and the gate was open, and there was smoke going up, and there was a table out in the yard.

Not a bench. A table, with a cloth on it, and bread on it, and a barrel of small beer, and the Baron of Vale sitting behind it in a chair with his wife on one side of him and a boy of one on his knee.

There were twenty men in that yard and not one of them had a weapon in his hand.

Behind them the granary stood with its doors open, and it was full.

The column came down the hill and stopped, and stopped badly: two hundred men in no order, in the road, in the mud, staring at a baron having breakfast.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Then the Baron of Vale stood up and said, in the voice of a man who has given orders in that county for thirty years, "You'll be the men from the hills. There's bread and there's beer and there's water at the pump. Form a line at the barrel and don't put your boots on the mill floor."

---

Tolan did not go in for a long time. He sat on his horse in the road and looked at it and worked out, in about four minutes, what had been done to them, and by the end of the fourth minute he was aware that he had been beaten by a thing so simple that he would never be able to explain it to a room.

*If we burn it, we've burned a baron's mill with a baron's family in it and there's a Count at Harven in six weeks.*

*If we take him, we've taken a baron hostage and we've got to feed him and house him and there's no road in these marches that a hostage gets us that we don't have now.*

*If we stand in the road, we've got two hundred men in the wet who haven't eaten and there's a table of bread ten feet away.*

*And if we eat it—*

He watched the front of the line.

*If we eat it, then in a week there'll be four hundred people in the Veyr country saying the Vales fed the levy and the chieftain couldn't stop them and no man was hurt, and no man in those hills will ever come out again.*

That was it. That was the whole of the thing, and it had been done with a table.

---

Berrin went up to the table first, because Berrin had lost seven men at Bradey's and nineteen off a road and had nothing left but the wish to be doing something.

"Baron."

"Captain."

"I'm to take your mill."

"Then take it," said Edric Vale. "You'll want to look at the store first and I'd be obliged if you'd not put it about that the second door's broken, because my wife's been after me about it since the autumn."

Berrin stood in front of him with his hat in his hands.

"You're not going to resist."

"No."

"Then what's this?"

"It's Friday," said the Baron of Vale. "And you've been two days in the wet."

---

The trouble started at about eight, and it started over the small beer.

A man from the Veyr country — not one of Hask's own hundred, a levy man, called out at a week's notice — was at the barrel with a cup in each hand, and one of Rennick's men said something to him about taking two.

And the levy man put both cups down and hit him.

It took six men to get them apart and Tolan got there fourth and pulled the levy man out by the collar and held him against the wall, and the man was crying and swearing and saying, over and over, "He's got no right, he's got no right, we've been in the mud two days."

"Who?"

"Him. That one. Rennick's. He's been at me since Tuesday and he's not had a wet boot on and he's had a horse." The man wiped his face. "And now there's bread out and he's saying we're not to eat it because it's the baron's, and I'll tell you something, sir, and then you can flog me. I've been called out in March to take a mill and I don't know what for. Nobody's told me what for. And I've got a wife and two children at home with the roof needing doing, and I'd have done the roof in four days, and instead I'm here, and the man who called me out isn't here and the men who'd pay me aren't here, and there's bread."

Tolan let go of his collar.

"Go and sit down," he said. "At the back. And don't hit anybody for an hour."

---

By noon there were eleven men gone.

Not run — gone. They had walked out of the yard, up the road, and away over the shoulder in twos and threes, and one of them had asked a mill hand which way it was to Harrowmere and had been told, and had gone that way because he had a sister there.

Hask had two men brought back at the second hour and had them flogged in the yard because he had to, and Tolan watched the yard watching it, and the yard was not frightened, and that was the thing that finished it.

Six months ago a flogging in a yard made men stand straight.

Now they were eating bread and watching a flogging, and there was a boy of about fourteen at the back who was eating his own bread while he watched and who was not, Tolan could see, taking in a single word of the lesson.

---

Elna Vale carried a jug round that yard for six hours and it was the best day of her life.

Nobody had told her to. That was the thing. On Friday morning her mother had said, *stay by me and keep your mouth shut and if a man speaks to you answer him and don't look at the ground*, and she had done that for an hour, and then the queue at the barrel had got long and there had been nobody to take the water round, and she had picked up the second jug and gone.

Her father saw her and said nothing. Her mother saw her and said nothing, and later, at about the fourth hour, her mother caught her by the arm in the passage and held on for a moment and said, "You'll do," which Elna had never heard her say to anybody.

She learned the column by its boots. That was the thing she would remember. Two hundred men in a yard and the ones from Rennick's had good boots with the mud scraped off and the ones from the Veyr country had boots tied up with cord, and the ones with the cord were polite and the ones with the good boots were not, and it took her about an hour and a half to work that out and when she did she started going to the corded men first.

There was a boy of about fourteen with a bow that was too big for him, standing at the wall, and Elna gave him water, and he said thank you, and then said, "Is that your father?" and she said yes, and he said, "He's not like I thought," and she said, "He's not like I thought either," and they both stood there for a moment, and then she went on with the jug.

And at the fourth hour there was a flogging in the yard, and Elna Vale stood in the passage doorway where she could see it and made herself watch it, because she had decided in February, behind a stone in a millrace, that she was not going to be a person who looked away from a thing and then said she hadn't seen it.

It was two men and it was done with a strap and it took a long time. Her mother came and stood behind her and did not tell her to go inside.

"That's the chieftain doing a thing he doesn't want to do," said Maren Vale.

"Why?"

"Because thirty men have walked off up the road and after today there'll be more, and a man in his position has to be seen to be a man who does something." Her mother's voice was quite level. "Watch it properly. It's the only thing in this yard that isn't bread."

Elna watched it properly.

And afterward, going round with the jug again, she saw her brother standing at the gate.

He was not doing anything. He was standing at the gate with his hands in his pockets, in the way he stood in the garden, and men were going past him with cups and not seeing him at all, and Elna knew — because she had spent five months in the same house as him and had learned to look at him the way you look at a fire to see which way it's going — that he was working.

She took the jug over.

"Casian."

"Elna."

"What are you doing?"

"Standing at a gate."

"Do you want something to drink?"

"No," he said. "But I'd like you to stay here for a minute and then go round the yard with the jug the way you've been doing, and not look at me, and if a tall man with grey in his beard comes across the yard toward me, go and stand in the mill door and don't say anything to anyone."

Elna looked up at him.

"Is he going to hurt you?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

"Because he's spent six months being careful and he's just had the worst day of his life," said Casian, "and a careful man on his worst day doesn't do a new thing. He does the thing he came to do and then he goes home." He looked down at her. "Off you go. Mind your feet."

She went, with the jug, and she did not look at him, and it was the longest quarter of an hour she had ever spent in her life.

---

He found Hask at the gate at about two, with the mill taken and the granary counted and the baron's family in their own hall, and Hask's face was the face of a man doing arithmetic in the dark.

"Chieftain."

"How many gone?"

"Thirty-one. And nine more that'll go tonight when it's dark and nobody's counting."

"Sooners said they'd follow me into a valley and they have."

"They've followed you over a road and into a yard where a baron gave them bread," said Tolan. "They'll follow you home. That's what they'll do. They'll follow you home and they'll tell their wives about the bread."

Hask put his hand flat on the gate.

"The trap was never the trap," he said.

"No."

"I looked at every wall in this county for six months. I put a man on the cut and a man on the Ashgrove road and I counted the men a baron could raise and I never once counted—" he stopped.

"Bread," said Tolan.

"Bread."

There was a silence at the gate of a mill in a county that had just been taken by two hundred men.

"There's a thing I'd say now if I were a man who gave advice," said Tolan.

"Say it."

"Take the mill and leave forty men in it and go home today, and be at Harven in the spring with the deed and the finding and let the lawyers do it, and don't take another step in this county with a column." Tolan looked at him. "You'll not do it."

"No."

"Then I'll be at Ashgrove tonight," said Tolan, "and I'll have the books out of the tally-room before dark, and I'll go on writing it down, because that's what I am."

---

And at the gate of the mill there was a boy.

Tolan saw him at about the fourth hour and did not think anything of him, because the yard was full of people — mill hands, carters, three of the family's servants, a girl of twelve carrying a jug round and being told to mind her feet — and a boy of sixteen standing at a gate with his hands in his pockets was the least remarkable thing in the county.

Except that he had been standing there for an hour and nobody had asked him what he wanted.

And except that when Tolan looked at him properly — a thin boy, plain coat, no boots worth a mark, a face that was pleasant and unremarkable and stayed that way while he watched two hundred armed men eat his father's bread — Tolan felt the back of his neck go cold, for the second time in six months, and this time he did not look away.

*That's him. That's the one from the kitchen with the jug.*

He started across the yard.

And the boy looked up, and saw him coming, and did not move, and did not look away, and Tolan had crossed half the yard before he understood that the boy had been waiting there for an hour not to watch the column, but to be seen.
