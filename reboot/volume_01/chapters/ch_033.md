Gareth was at the mill door when he came out of the yard with the cup.

"You're going in there."

"I'm going in there."

"With a cup."

"With a cup," said Casian. "If I go in with a message he'll have to be a chieftain at me. If I go in with a cup he'll have to be a man at me, and a man at me is a man who answers questions without knowing he's been asked."

Gareth looked at the door of the tally-room.

"He put a strap to two of his own men this morning and laughed at nothing at supper."

"Yes."

"And if he takes you by the collar and puts you against that wall?"

"Then he'll have taken the fourth son of Vale by the collar in front of a hundred of his own men and made an enemy of a county with a lawyer in it," said Casian, "and that's the whole of what he's spent six months being careful about, and it's why he came himself instead of sending two hundred men to do it in the dark in January." He adjusted his grip on the cup. "He's not going to touch me, Gareth. He's going to be courteous to me and then he's going to tell me something he shouldn't, because a courteous man in a bad week wants somebody to be courteous to."

"And that's what you're for."

"That's what I'm for this afternoon."

Gareth Vale stood aside from the door.

"When this is over," he said, "I want a week in which nobody in this family arranges anything, and we all eat mutton and I'm allowed to be bored."

"You'll have it in May," said Casian, and went in.

---

At the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth of March the Chieftain of the Veyr country sat down in the tally-room of a mill he had taken that morning, and the fourth son of the Baron of Vale brought him a cup of small beer.

"You'll not remember me," said the boy.

Hask Veyr looked at him.

He was a thin youth with a plain coat and a face that was not doing anything, and he came in with the cup in both hands the way a servant does, and he put it down and stepped back and said nothing, and waited.

"Should I?"

"You gave me a coin in your own hall in January," said the boy, "and you told me to go to bed."

Hask looked at him properly then, the way a man looks at a thing that has turned out to be in the room.

"The jug," he said.

"The jug."

There was a silence in the tally-room. Outside, in the yard, two hundred men were eating and men were being flogged and a baron's wife was telling a mill hand where the boards were kept. In the tally-room it was very quiet.

"Sit down," said Hask.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down," said Hask, "because I've been in this county four months looking for a thing and I've had a day that would break a better man than me and I'm not going to have this conversation with a boy standing over me."

Casian sat down.

---

"How old are you?"

"Sixteen."

"And you've been doing this since when?"

"Since August," said Casian, "and I don't know what *this* is, and I'd be obliged if you'd tell me, because I've had a great deal of practice at not being the person in the room who says the most and I'd like one conversation where I find out what I look like from outside."

Hask turned his cup round on the table.

"You look like a boy who's been sent in to be harmless," he said. "They've done it to me since I was twelve. There's always one at these things. He's the one whose hands you don't watch."

"And what would be the harm in me?"

"Nothing at all, and that's what makes it work." Hask drank. "You're the one they'll not hang if it goes wrong. You're the one they can put in a room with men like me and say afterward, with a straight face, that the boy only came in with a jug."

"I did only come in with a jug."

"You did." Hask put the cup down. "And you came in with it twice, and the second time you came in from the wrong side of the room, and you'd been sent the first time and you weren't the second."

Casian said nothing.

"My old captain had a way of saying it," said Hask Veyr. "He said the forest is full of men who were sent in to be harmless." He looked up. "What do you want?"

---

It was the question Osric had asked his own room in January, and Casian noticed, and did not say so.

"I want you at Harven in the spring," he said.

Hask's face did not move for a moment.

"Say that again."

"You've got a deed against the eastern strip and a finding filed at Harven and the Count's called the eastern lords to answer for a levy that doesn't add up. You'll go south in April and you'll stand in a room in Harven with a lawyer, and it'll take two years and it'll cost you four hundred marks and you'll win the strip if you're lucky." Casian put his hands flat on the table. "I'd rather that than what you did this morning."

"You'd rather a lawyer than two hundred men."

"I'd rather anything than two hundred men," said Casian Vale, "because two hundred men in a yard is a thing that can go wrong in nine ways and a lawyer is a thing that goes wrong slowly and in writing, and the county can watch a lawyer and can't watch a yard."

Hask sat back.

"You've got a strange way of talking for a boy of sixteen."

"Everybody says so."

"No — " Hask stopped. "Everybody says it because you're clever. That's not what I mean. You talk about a county the way a man talks about a horse he's got to get through a winter."

---

They were in that room for the better part of an hour, and this is what Casian got out of it, and he got all of it without asking a question that could be noticed.

He learned that Hask's hundred were paid at the quarter and the quarter was in arrears since August and that the arrears had been paid out of Thornwall, and that Osric's money had gone quiet in February and Hask had not asked why, because a chieftain does not ask a man who is feeding him why the food is late.

He learned that Berrin's men — the men from Rennick's, the men with good boots and a horse each — were not Hask's and would not go where Hask went if Hask's road was dirty.

He learned that the levy from the Veyr country had been called out on a promise of a share of what was taken, and that nothing had been taken, and that the promise had been made in Hask's own name by a man who was not Hask.

And he learned the thing he had come in the room for, which Hask told him in the last five minutes because a man who has decided that the person he is talking to is a boy will answer a boy's question without weighing it.

"You'll go home tomorrow?"

"Tonight, most of us," said Hask. "We'll take the long field above the Veyr at the ford of Rye and be home by Sunday, and the levy can go on to their own villages from there." He rubbed his eyes. "Two hundred men on the drove for eleven miles, and half of them will be gone before the ford."

"The long field at Rye."

"Aye."

"Because it's got water."

"Because it's got water and it's got a wall on two sides and it's the only field in that country that a hundred men can camp in without being seen from three miles off." Hask looked at him. "Why?"

"Because you asked me what I want," said Casian, "and I've been trying to work out whether you were a man who was careful or a man who'd been told to look it."

---

Hask Veyr laughed.

It was the first time he had laughed since February and it was a bad laugh, an old man's dry noise, and it went on a little too long.

"And which am I?"

"You're careful," said Casian. "You've been careful all winter. You looked at every wall in this county and you counted every man a baron could raise and you never once asked what a road was for."

"That's a boy's answer to a soldier's work."

"It's a boy's answer and it's right," said Casian Vale, "and it's the reason you've got two hundred men in a yard eating my father's bread and no way to be a thing tomorrow morning."

---

There was a long silence in the tally-room.

Hask put both hands on the table.

"Say the rest of it," he said. "You've been sitting there for an hour being useful and pleasant and I've not once heard a thing out of you that a man couldn't have said in front of his own priest. So say the rest of it, and say it as yourself, because I've had a day where I put a strap to two of my own and I'd like one honest thing before I sleep."

Casian looked at him.

"Go home tonight," he said. "Go now. Don't wait for the light and don't wait for the levy and don't stop in the long field at Rye. Get on the road while there's still enough dark to be out of this county by morning, and when you get to your own house, burn the deed, write to the Count that you want nothing from the eastern strip, and be at Harven in April as a man who came to answer a question."

"And if I do that?"

"Then in ten years you'll be an old man in a slate-roofed house with a hundred men who think you were unlucky," said Casian, "and in this county they'll say the chieftain came, and was fed, and went home, and that'll be the whole of the story. That's what I'm offering you. I'm not offering you a victory. There isn't one."

Hask Veyr looked at the boy across the table for a long moment.

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll do the other thing," said Casian.

"What other thing?"

"You'll find out," said Casian, "and it'll come a piece at a time, and it'll take about eleven days, and at the end of it there'll be a horse standing somewhere with nobody with it, and everybody in the marches will have a story about what happened to you and they'll all be wrong."

The tally-room was very quiet.

"Are you threatening me?"

"No," said Casian. "I'm telling you what I'll do, which is different, and I've just told you what I'd rather do, and you'll notice that I've not asked you for anything."

---

Hask stood up and went to the window.

"Tolan says you're in the county and nobody can find you. Osric's man says you're a rumour. My own captains have been at each other for a fortnight about whether you're a spirit or a trick with mirrors." He turned round. "And here you are. A boy of sixteen in a plain coat who's told me to burn a deed and go home."

"Here I am."

"You're not going to be anything, you know," said Hask Veyr, and he said it without cruelty, which is what made it a mistake. "I've met a hundred of you. Boys who work things out. There's five in my own country and two of them are dead of it. You'll be given a farm and you'll be clever in the market and in thirty years you'll be the man the village asks about a boundary." He picked up his cup and drank the last of it. "You're not a warlord and you're not a bishop and you've done clever things to me this winter that I'll not forget, and I'll tell you what I've learned in thirty-one years of this. A man of your sort does not last past his patron. Your father's a good man and he'll die and your brother'll not be half as clever as you and you'll be a clever man in a cold house."

Casian stood up.

"Yes," he said. "That's probably true."

"It is true. I've seen it." Hask put the cup down. "And so I'll not be going home tonight. I'll be going home tomorrow with my two hundred men and I'll take the long field at Rye because it's got water, and I'll be in the marches again before the harvest, and I'd like you to remember this conversation when that happens, because I've been polite and I've told you the truth and you'll not get both from another man in this county."

---

Casian went out into the yard.

It was getting dark and there were men asleep against the mill wall and men sitting on the mill step and a boy of fourteen with a bow too big for him watching a card game. The queue at the barrel was down to nine.

Tolan was standing at the corner of the granary, and had been standing there for some while, and Casian saw him and did not avoid him, and the two of them stood ten feet apart in a baron's yard full of the baron's enemies.

"You were in the room with him for an hour," said Tolan.

"I was."

"Nobody's been in a room with him for an hour since January." Tolan looked at him. "What did you want?"

And Casian Vale, who had spent five months being the person in the room who says the least, looked at the one man in the county who had been writing it all down, and said, "I wanted him to go home tonight."

"Because you'd rather he didn't fight."

"Because if he goes home tonight he lives," said Casian, "and there are about a hundred and forty men in that column who'd have gone home to their villages on Sunday and lived, and eleven of them will not, and it will be my fault, and I'll not be able to write it in the book." He put his hands in his pockets. "You're the man who writes things down. Write that."

Tolan did not move.

"What's your name?"

"Casian."

"Casian Vale." Tolan said it slowly, as a man says a thing he has been trying to say for months and has finally got his mouth round. "I've read six months of this county and I've had a horse saddled since February, and I'd worked out that it was a person and that the person was in that house, and I came here this morning to be certain." He looked at the boy. "You've got a thing that works and I can't see it and I've been a soldier for twenty years. I would like to know what it is."

"It's a great many small things done early," said Casian.

"That's not what I asked."

"No."

"Then you'll not tell me."

"No," said Casian, "and you'll go on writing it down anyway, and one of us is going to be wrong about how this ends, and I'd rather it was me."

---

He went in through the mill door and up the stair to the room his family had been given, and his father was sitting on a stool with his boots off and a cup in his hand and had clearly been waiting.

"Well?"

"He'll not go home tonight."

Edric Vale looked at his son.

"You told him," said the baron. "You told him the truth and what you'd do and you gave him a way out, and he's a careful man of thirty-one years' service who has spent his life being underestimated by lords, and you're the fourth son of a small barony, and he heard a boy."

"Yes."

"I'd have done the same at your age and I'd have got the same answer." Edric rubbed his face. "What happens now?"

"Eleven days," said Casian. "And then he's a horse with nobody with it."

His father was quiet for a while.

"Casian."

"Father."

"Your mother's roasting a goose on Sunday and she has asked whether you'll be there and I'd like to be able to tell her yes."

Casian stood in the doorway of a room in a mill that two hundred men had taken that morning.

"Yes," he said. "I'll be there."
