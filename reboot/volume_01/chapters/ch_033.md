Gareth barred the doorway of the mill tally-room when Casian emerged from the scullery carrying a glazed earthenware mug of small beer.

"You intend to walk into that room alone," Gareth muttered, keeping his voice beneath the rumble of the iron wheel.

"I am carrying small beer," Casian replied.

"You are carrying a flagon to an armed chieftain who commands two hundred lances in our yard."

"Precisely," Casian said, shifting his grip upon the cold clay handle. "If an armsman enters that chamber with a written dispatch, Hask Veyr must receive him as a military commander facing an enemy garrison. If a sixteen-year-old boy enters with a cup of beer, he must receive him as a mortal man resting his boots. And a weary man drinking small beer answers questions without realizing he has surrendered the ground."

Gareth surveyed the heavy timber door of the tally-room, his scarred brow furrowed with anxiety.

"He applied an iron strap to the backs of two of his own retainers this morning," Gareth noted. "He laughed at nothing during supper. The man is navigating a disaster."

"I am aware."

"And if his temper breaks? If he seizes you by the collar and drives his dirk into your throat?"

"Then he will have slaughtered the fourth son of House Vale within plain sight of eighty witnesses," Casian answered with chilling composure. "He will have converted an administrative property dispute into high treason, and he will have united every border magistrate against his clan. That is the exact catastrophe he has spent six months avoiding. He will not lay a finger upon me, Gareth. He will behave with scrupulous marcher courtesy, and then he will divulge his destination, because an aging warlord enduring a humiliating setback desperately desires an audience to validate his dignity."

Gareth stood motionless for several moments, his broad shoulders squared against the lintel.

"When this campaign terminates," Gareth said, his voice husky, "I demand seven days in which no member of this family negotiates a lease, reviews a boundary, or maneuvers a tenant. I wish to consume boiled mutton, inspect my horses, and enjoy the luxury of being profoundly bored."

"You will have your boredom before the hay is cut in May," Casian promised, and pushed past him into the room.

---

At the sixth hour of the twenty-sixth of March, the Chieftain of the Veyr gorge sat behind the pine table in the mill's private office, and the fourth son of the Baron of Vale set an earthenware flagon before him.

"You will not recall my face," the youth said quietly.

Hask Veyr raised his head from his folded arms, his dark, weathered features scored with fatigue.

He observed a slender lad in a plain homespun tunic of undyed wool, wearing unbuckled cowhide shoes that were caked in dry flour dust. The boy held his hands loosely before his waist in the deferential posture of a country servant, yet his grey eyes remained entirely still—level, attentive, and devoid of the nervous flutter common to kitchen lads.

"Should I recognize you?" Hask asked, his voice harsh from peat smoke.

"You tossed me a silver groat in your council chamber in January," Casian replied. "You instructed me to inform the cooks to stoke the scullery fire and seek my pallet."

Hask stared at him for four slow heartbeats, the lines around his eyes tightening with sudden, sharp recollection.

"The jug," the chieftain breathed.

"The jug."

A heavy, unnatural stillness enveloped the timber room. Outside, in the muddy yard, two hundred horsemen were dividing rations, an iron pot clattered against a hearthstone, and Baroness Maren's voice carried through an open casement as she directed a carter toward the wood-shed. Inside the tally-room, the silence was absolute.

"Take a stool," Hask said.

"I am content to stand, my lord."

"Sit," Hask commanded, gesturing toward the pine bench opposite his chair. "I have traversed eleven leagues of swamp looking for the agency that has bled my lances all winter, and I have endured a day that would break the spirit of an imperial marshal. I refuse to conduct this discussion with a boy hovering over my shoulder."

Casian took the stool, resting his hands flat upon the pine planks.

---

"What is your age?" Hask asked, leaning back against the wall.

"Sixteen winters."

"And you have been executing these maneuvers since what month?"

"Since August," Casian replied. "Though I do not comprehend what you classify as a maneuver, and I would be grateful for your instruction. I have spent the winter attempting to remain unobserved, and I should like to understand how our household appears to an experienced soldier."

Hask turned the clay mug between his scarred fingers, his expression unreadable.

"You appear like a decoy dispatched to project harmlessness," the chieftain said flatly. "I have observed the tactic since my youth in the southern border wars. When a great house prepares an ambush, they place an innocuous lad in the foreground. He is the figure whose hands you fail to watch."

"And what peril do I present?"

"None whatsoever, and that is precisely why the stratagem succeeds," Hask said, taking a measured draught of the bitter ale. "You are the son nobody will hang if the enterprise miscarries. You are the messenger they can present to a royal commissioner with a straight countenance, swearing under oath that the boy merely carried a flagon of wine."

"I did merely carry the flagon."

"You carried it twice," Hask retorted, pointing a blunt finger across the table. "And upon the second occasion, you entered from the private corridor behind the dais, and you remained standing behind my shoulder listening to an imperial treason being negotiated. You were sent upon the first errand, but you walked in upon the second by your own volition."

Casian offered no denial.

"My old commander had an adage," Hask murmured, gazing toward the shuttered window. "He remarked that the high forest is choked with the shallow graves of men who were dispatched to appear harmless. What do you seek from me, boy?"

---

It was the identical inquiry Baron Osric had posed to his council three months earlier, and Casian registered the symmetry with cold satisfaction.

"I seek your presence at Harven at the spring assize," Casian stated.

Hask's weathered countenance did not flicker.

"Repeat that demand."

"You hold a legal brief challenging our eastern titles, and Master Rennick has registered a finding in the ducal exchequer," Casian said, his voice entirely level. "The Count has commanded the eastern magnates to present their tallies and justify their border levies. You have the right to ride south in April, retain legal advocates, and argue your claim before the royal bench. It will occupy two seasons, it will exhaust four hundred marks of coin, and if your documents hold, you will acquire four arable parcels under royal patent."

Casian leaned forward, his gaze locking with Hask's tired eyes.

"I would far prefer that legal trial to the spectacle that unfolded in our yard this morning."

"You prefer a courtroom of parchment-scribes to two hundred mounted lances?"

"I prefer any proceeding to two hundred mounted lances," Casian answered without hesitation. "Because an armed troop in an open courtyard is a catastrophe that can erupt into slaughter through a dozen accidents: a drunken spearman, an unbuckled horse, or a terrified child. A legal assize proceeds with deliberate slowness upon parchment. A county can survive an audit; it cannot endure an unchained fire."

Hask sat motionless, studying the lad's posture.

"You speak in an extraordinary register for a youth of sixteen," the chieftain observed.

"I have been informed of that defect previously."

"It is not cleverness," Hask said, shaking his head. "Clever boys quote Latin verses and recite courtly flourishes. You evaluate an entire province with the cold, detached economy of a carter calculating whether his draft horse can survive the winter on half-rations of oats."

---

They remained within the tally-room for the better part of an hour, and Casian extracted every operational fact he required without voicing an inquiry that attracted suspicion.

He confirmed that Hask's core troop of eighty lances was compensated quarterly, that their wages were seven months in arrears, and that the emergency funds had been supplied directly from Thornwall's municipal treasury. He discerned that Osric's subsidies had ceased abruptly in February following Lady Seraine's arrival, and that Hask had refrained from demanding explanation because an outlaw commander does not insult the patron who feeds his mounts.

He determined that Berrin's detachment—the mercenaries outfitted in fine beaver caps and clean leather boots—were contracted to Master Rennick personally, and would abandon Hask's banner the instant an engagement turned precarious.

He discovered that the upland levies had been raised upon the promise of plundering the Vale granaries, that the granary remained locked and untouched, and that the warrants had been forged in Hask's name by an agent in Thornwall.

And in the concluding five minutes of their conversation, Hask surrendered the vital coordinate—doing so with the casual indifference of a commander who assumes his companion is an unlettered youth incapable of processing strategic intelligence.

"Do you intend to march your force north tomorrow?" Casian inquired, rising to inspect the flagon.

"Tonight, for the majority," Hask answered, rubbing his bloodshot eyes. "We will bivouac in the long meadow above the Veyr gorge, beside the Rye crossing, and reach our home by Sunday dusk. The auxiliary levies can disperse to their respective hamlets from that position."

"The long meadow at the Rye crossing," Casian repeated softly.

"Aye."

"Because of the water?"

"Because of the river, and because the meadow is bounded by stone dikes on two flanks," Hask said. "It is the solitary position in that valley where a hundred mounted men can encamp without advertising their presence to outriders three leagues off."

The chieftain frowned, looking up with sudden sharpness.

"Why do you inquire?"

"Because you asked what I sought," Casian answered smoothly. "And I was attempting to determine whether you were a commander of genuine caution, or merely a soldier instructed to appear prudent."

---

Hask Veyr laughed—a dry, rasping sound that carried no amusement and persisted several beats too long.

"And what is your verdict?"

"You are exceptionally cautious," Casian said. "You have practiced caution across the entire winter. You calculated the height of every palisade in this valley, you tallied every spear my father could muster, and you never once asked what purpose a road serves."

"That is an adolescent's retort to a veteran's labor."

"It is an accurate assessment," Casian retorted. "And it is the singular reason two hundred of your followers are currently seated in our courtyard consuming our barley bread, completely unable to wage war tomorrow morning."

A heavy silence descended upon the chamber.

Hask planted his fists upon the table, rising slowly to his feet.

"Utter the remainder of your thoughts," the chieftain commanded, his voice dropping into a dangerous, gravelly tone. "You have occupied that stool for an hour playing the polite servant, and you have not voiced a single syllable that could not be repeated before a bishop. Speak the truth as yourself, lad. I have endured a day where I was forced to flog two of my own kinsmen to preserve order; grant me one unvarnished truth before I mount."

Casian met his gaze with unyielding clarity.

"Depart this valley tonight," Casian said, his voice dropping into the quiet, absolute authority of the Hollow. "Mount your horse now. Do not wait for daybreak, do not linger for the stragglers, and under no circumstances pitch your pavilions in the long meadow at Rye. Take the northern track while the night conceals your retreat, clear our parish boundary before sunrise, and when you reach your stone hall, consign Osric's forged deed to the hearth-fire. Write to Count Harven renouncing all pretensions to the eastern strip, and present yourself at the spring assize as an honorable marcher lord answering an imperial summons."

"And if I submit to that counsel?"

"Then in ten winters you will sit as an elder in a slate-roofed hall, surrounded by a hundred spearmen who believe you were merely unfortunate in your winter campaigns," Casian answered. "And in these marches, the folk will recount that the upland chieftain came down with two hundred horses, broke bread with House Vale, and rode home without spilling blood. That is the honorable resolution I offer you. I offer no victory, because none exists."

Hask Veyr stared at the boy across the pine table, his broad chest rising and falling beneath his leather mantle.

"And if I reject your terms?"

"Then I will execute the alternative," Casian replied.

"What alternative?"

"You will perceive it in due course," Casian said calmly. "It will arrive in progressive stages, occupying approximately eleven days. And at the conclusion, a saddled courser will stand deserted upon an upland trail with its reins trailing in the heather, and every gossip in the province will invent an explanation for your disappearance, and every single one of them will be false."

The room seemed to grow cold as frost.

"Do you threaten my life, boy?"

"I state an eventuality," Casian said. "Which is an entirely different matter. I have just detailed the course I would vastly prefer to pursue, and you will observe that I have demanded no tribute from your purse."

---

Hask turned away, walking to the narrow casement overlooking the yard.

"Tolan swears you are an omnipresent shadow that cannot be located," Hask said, staring out at his resting men. "Osric's factor swears you are a phantom manufactured by frightened millers. My captains have brawled over whether you are an ancient spirit or an illusion engineered with silver."

He turned back, his lip curling with dismissive pity.

"And here you stand. A youth of sixteen wearing a patched wool tunic, commanding a chieftain to burn his deeds and flee into the dark."

"Here I stand," Casian said.

"You will not achieve your ambitions, lad," Hask said, his voice devoid of anger, which rendered the error profound. "I have encountered dozens of youths like you. Boys who can calculate figures upon a slate. There were three in my own clan, and two are buried beneath the scree. You will inherit a modest sheep farm, you will haggle over fleece at the Michaelmas fair, and in thirty winters you will be the elderly neighbor whom tenants consult regarding a disputed drainage ditch."

He reached for his belt-knife, adjusting the scabbard.

"You are not an imperial warlord, and you are not an ecclesiastical inquisitor. You executed several ingenious tricks this season that I will not easily forget, but hear what thirty-one winters of warfare have taught me: an individual of your breed does not endure beyond the life of his patron. Your father is an honorable, failing soldier who will perish of a chest chill within three seasons, your elder brother possesses neither your cunning nor your nerve, and you will find yourself an isolated scholar shivering in a neglected hall."

Casian stood up from the stool.

"That is entirely possible," Casian acknowledged.

"It is an inevitability," Hask said, draining the last dregs of his beer. "Therefore, I will not depart tonight. I will march at midday tomorrow with two hundred lances, I will establish my camp in the long meadow at Rye because it commands the water, and I will descend upon this valley again before the winter wheat is harvested. Retain this conference in your memory, lad. I have treated you with courtesy and I have spoken the truth, and you will receive neither from another adversary along this border."

---

Casian emerged into the gathering twilight of the courtyard.

The rain had ceased, leaving the cobbles slick with brown mud. Scores of mercenary fighters were sleeping wrapped in their cloaks against the timber walls, while others sat upon the granary ramp gambling for brass buttons. The queue before the small beer barrel had dwindled to six men.

Tolan was standing in the deep shadow of the grain elevator, watching the gate with his arms folded across his leather brigandine.

He observed Casian's approach, stepped away from the timber post, and halted five paces distant.

"You were enclosed with him for an hour," Tolan said, his voice low and measured.

"I was."

"No man has held his ear for an hour since the council in January," Tolan remarked, his dark eyes scrutinizing Casian's impassive features. "What did you petition for?"

Casian looked at the mercenary captain—the solitary antagonist who had perceived the architecture from the beginning, who kept an accounting in leather notebooks, and who had chosen to stand witness.

"I petitioned him to mount his horse and ride home tonight," Casian answered.

"Because you wish to avoid an engagement."

"Because if he departs tonight, he survives," Casian said, his voice dropping into a cadence of quiet, chilling gravity. "And a hundred and forty peasant levies who marched down that drove would return to their upland hearths on Sunday and live out their days. Within eleven days, eleven of those men will perish. The responsibility will rest upon my conscience, and I will be unable to justify the loss upon any ledger."

Casian thrust his hands into his tunic pockets.

"You are the investigator who records the truth. Inscribe that statement in your third book."

Tolan stood motionless, the wind stirring his dark mantle.

"What is your name?" the captain asked slowly.

"Casian Vale."

"Casian Vale," Tolan repeated, rolling the syllables across his tongue as if committing a formal legal title to memory. "I have scrutinized six months of inexplicable events in this valley, I have kept a courser saddled since the February frost, and I deduced that an individual was orchestrating the design from within this manor. I rode down this morning to verify the hypothesis."

He took a step closer, studying the boy's calm grey eyes.

"You wield a mechanism that operates with terrifying precision, yet I cannot discern the levers, and I have campaigned across these provinces for twenty years. I desire to comprehend what it is."

"It is merely a substantial collection of minute preparations executed well in advance," Casian said.

"That is an evasion."

"It is," Casian conceded. "Yet you will continue to compile your observations, and one of us will prove mistaken regarding how this reckoning concludes. I would vastly prefer that the error belonged to me."

---

Casian ascended the narrow timber staircase to the upper loft of the mill, where his family had assembled for the evening.

Baron Edric sat upon an upturned meal firkin near the hearth, his boots removed, warming his bare, swollen feet before the peat coals.

"Well, Casian?" his father inquired, setting down his clay pipe.

"Hask Veyr refuses to depart tonight," Casian reported.

The baron nodded slowly, a weary, knowing sadness shadowing his eyes.

"You spoke the truth to him," Edric said softly. "You outlined the consequences, you displayed the trap, and you offered him an honorable withdrawal. And he is a veteran mercenary with thirty-one years of campaigning who has spent his career being dismissed by titled magnates, you are an unlanded fourth son of sixteen years, and he heard only the insolence of an inexperienced schoolboy."

"He heard a boy," Casian agreed.

"I would have committed the identical blunder at your age, and I would have received the identical dismissal," Edric murmured, rubbing his weathered brow. "What transpires now?"

"Eleven days," Casian replied quietly. "And then his mount will stand riderless upon an upland path."

The room grew very still. Outside, the great millstone continued its rhythmic, low rotation: *tick-tick-tick*.

"Casian," his father called as the boy turned toward the doorway.

"Yes, father?"

"Your mother is preparing a roast goose for our supper on Sunday," Edric said, looking up with profound, quiet affection. "She inquired whether you would take your place at the table, and I should like to assure her that you will be present."

Casian stood upon the threshold, feeling the immense, protective warmth of the domestic hearth anchor his thoughts against the cold void of the Hollow.

"Tell her yes," Casian said. "I will be at the board."
