The long field at Rye had water on the north side and a wall on two sides and it was, as Hask Veyr had told a boy in a tally-room, the only field in that country where a hundred men could camp and not be seen from three miles off.

He was in it for eleven days.

**The first day** they came off the drove at the ford and the levy went on to their own villages, which was what had been arranged, and he stood at the ford and counted them out because a chieftain counts.

Ninety-one went on. There had been a hundred and sixty levy men on the drove on Friday morning and there were sixty-nine at the ford on Sunday afternoon, and the rest had not deserted in a body; they had gone in twos and threes, some of them with permission and some without, and every one of them had a reason that a man could not argue with. A roof. A wife near her time. A brother who had gone on ahead.

"Ninety-one," said Weyl, who was his steward and had been with him nineteen years. "And they'll come back in the summer."

"They'll not come back," said Hask.

**The second day** the letters came up from Thornwall, three of them, and two were from Rennick and one was from Osric's clerk and none of them said anything at all.

*The Baron is indisposed. The matter of the finding is in hand. The Baron thanks you for your service in the east and asks that you hold your hundred in readiness but does not at present require them to be in the field.*

Hask read that last one four times, standing in the wet.

"Does not at present require them."

"My lord—"

"He's had a bad week," said Hask Veyr. "He's had a bad week and he's frightened, and a frightened man writes a letter like a clerk."

He wrote back that night, three pages, and he was careful with it and it was the best letter he had ever written, and no answer ever came.

**The third day** two of Rennick's men rode out of the camp without asking leave, and Berrin came to the fire and said the rest of them would be gone by the week's end.

"Why?"

"Because the word from Thornwall is that the Count's man is at Harven already with a levy book in his hand and there's a great many men in that town asking whose money paid for what." Berrin looked at him. "Chieftain, I've taken your pay and I've lost your men and I'm not going to be the man who's standing next to you when a clerk with a seal starts asking questions about a forged paper. I've a wife and a farm."

"And if I told you to stay?"

"Then I'd stay," said Berrin, "and I'd be no good to you, and you'd know it." He took his hat off. "I'm sorry. I've been a captain nine years and this winter has been the worst of them and I've not once known what I was doing."

He was gone on the seventh day with eleven men.

**The fourth day** the grain did not come.

There had been an arrangement since January: two hundred and forty measures out of Harrowmere, bought on credit against the spring, from a corn factor called Darke who had dealt with the Veyr country for thirty years and whose father had dealt with Hask's father.

Weyl came back from Harrowmere with the cart empty and a letter.

"He won't sell."

"Darke?"

"Darke's sold his whole spring contract to a carrying house," said Weyl. "A month ago. Corran and Son, out of Ashgrove. And he says he's sorry and he'd not have done it if he'd known, and he's not lying — he didn't know, he sold it in February when it was just a contract and a mark in a book."

Hask stood in the long field at Rye and looked at an empty cart.

"Corran and Son," he said.

"Aye."

"They carry flour."

"They carry a great deal more than flour," said Weyl, "and I've been trying to tell you that since the winter."

**The fifth day** he did the arithmetic himself, in the tent, with a lamp burning, and when he had done it he sat for a long time with his hands flat on the table.

He had sixty-eight men and eleven horses and no grain and no pay and no letter from a baron in Thornwall.

He had, in the hills, a country that would feed him for a year out of loyalty, and a name that still meant something, and four hundred men who would come if he called them in the summer.

And he had, in the eastern marches, a boy of sixteen who had said, *it'll come a piece at a time, and it'll take about eleven days.*

He had counted the days on the fifth day and there were six left and he sat in his tent with the lamp burning and understood that he had counted them because he had believed it.

**The sixth day** he sent Ferrin south.

Ferrin was the oldest of his captains and had known Hask's father and had been at his side for twenty-two years, and he rode to Harven with the deed and the finding and three letters, and he did not come back.

He was not killed. Hask knew he was not killed, because on the ninth day there was a letter, and the letter had been written in Harven and was in Ferrin's hand and was four lines long and was the worst thing Hask ever read.

*Chieftain. I have spoken with the Count's clerk and I have been told the state of the levy and the state of the papers dated '16. I am an old man and I have four daughters and I will not perjure myself.*

*I am going home. I do not ask your leave and I am not your man any longer.*

*God keep you. You were a good master to me for twenty-two years.*

**The seventh day** three horses went.

This was the thing that reached into him where nothing else had. There were eleven horses on the horse-line at the west wall and on the morning of the seventh day there were eight, and the three halters were still on the rail, unbuckled and laid straight.

The sentry had been on that wall all night and had seen nothing and heard nothing and was so frightened that he could not be shouted at.

"They'd not have left the halters," said Weyl.

"No."

"Nobody leaves a halter. A halter's worth two marks."

"No," said Hask Veyr, and he stood and looked at the empty rail for a long while. "And nineteen saddles were unbuckled and laid straight on a road in February, and I said at the time that it was a trick, and I've been at four tables since saying it was a trick."

**The eighth day** nothing happened at all.

That was the worst day. There was no news and no letter and no desertion and the weather was fine and the men sat about in the long field at Rye and mended tack and played at tiles, and towards evening a man began to sing and stopped after four lines because nobody joined in.

Hask walked the wall at dusk with Weyl and looked out at his own country.

"There's nothing out there," said Weyl.

"No."

"Then why are we walking the wall?"

"Because I've been walking walls for thirty-one years," said Hask, "and a man who stops walking them because there's nothing out there is a man who's decided to be comfortable."

**The ninth day** the thing came apart in a way nobody could describe afterward.

There were fifty-two men in the long field at Rye in the morning who had been sixty-one at night, and no man had walked out of that camp. The sentries had been on all four corners. The dogs had not made a sound. The gate log had nothing in it.

And the men who were left were not frightened in the way men are frightened of an enemy. They were quiet. They went about the camp doing their work and did not look at the walls and did not stand in groups, and by the afternoon Hask had understood that every man in that field was doing sums about how far his own village was.

**The tenth day** Weyl left, and he did it in front of Hask, which was the bravest thing anybody did that fortnight.

"My lord, I've been with you nineteen years."

"Nineteen."

"I'm going to go and see to my sister's roof."

"Your sister's roof."

"She's a widow and it's not been done and I've been telling you about it since January," said Weyl, and then his face broke a little. "Chieftain, I'll come back when you call. I'll come in the summer with the four hundred and I'll not be the only one. But I'm not going to sit in this field one more night, and I'd rather tell you to your face than be found gone in the morning like the horses."

Hask Veyr put his hand on the man's shoulder.

"Go on," he said. "And Weyl — when they ask you in the village what happened here, tell them the truth. Tell them we were fed."

---

**The tenth night** the answers to the summonses began to come back, and this was the thing that finished him, and it finished him quietly, and he read all of them himself by the lamp.

He had sent out eleven summonses to eleven villages. He had called out four hundred men in the summer's name, and every one of those summonses had been carried by a rider he trusted, into a country his father had held and his grandfather had held, among people who had buried his mother.

Nine men came.

Three of the eleven villages sent nothing at all and did not send word either, which in that country was an answer of its own kind and an old one: it meant the village had met and had decided and would not put it in writing.

One village sent four men and a letter that said they would send the rest after the hay, and the letter was courteous and the four men were boys of sixteen and seventeen.

One village sent five.

And the last letter — from a hamlet called Rye Stoop, of eleven households, that Hask had ridden through in the year of the bad winter and where he had stood in the road and had a roof put back on a chapel at his own cost — was one page, and it was from a woman, and Hask knew her name.

*Chieftain. We have talked about it in the beer-house and we are not coming.*

*It is not that we have anything against you and it is not that we are frightened of the Vale. We have been down to the mill at Ashgrove for the bread and my man was there in March and came home and would not talk about it for a week and then talked about it all night.*

*What he says is that there were two hundred of you in a yard and a baron gave you bread out of his own granary, and that the men who came home from that yard came home with their boots and their knives and nobody was beaten.*

*Chieftain, if that is what the Vale does to two hundred men, then I would rather be at the mill than in the field. I am sorry. You were good to us.*

Hask Veyr read that letter four times and then folded it and put it inside his coat, and did not show it to Weyl or to Berrin or to any man, and it was found on him — or rather, it was found in the tent, on the table, when the flap was untied in the morning.

---

**The eleventh day** began with Hask Veyr alone in a tent with a lamp and a table and a book of tallies, and there were nine men in the long field at Rye.

He had done everything a man of his sort does. He had sent for the four hundred and the summonses had gone out to eleven villages; he had written to Harven and to two men in the south who owed him money; he had made a plan for the summer that was a good plan and that he knew, writing it, would never happen.

At about the second hour he went out of the tent to piss and to look at the wall, and when he came back there was a young man sitting by his fire.

He was sitting on the stool, with his hands in his pockets, in a plain coat, and he had a cup in front of him that he had not drunk from.

Hask stood in the door of his own tent.

"You've got a nerve," he said.

"I've been told."

"There's nine men in this field."

"There were nine at the second watch," said the boy. "There are four now. The other five are on the road and they'll not be back and none of them will be able to tell you why they went, and in a fortnight they'll say they'd had a message about a roof."

Hask Veyr came and sat down opposite him.

"There's a sentry on that wall."

"There is," said Casian Vale. "He's looking the other way and he'll keep looking the other way until it's light, and he'll not be able to tell you anything in the morning either."

"You've done a thing to him."

"No. He's frightened and he's cold and he's been standing on a wall for eleven nights and he's decided, in a way he doesn't know about, that he's not going to see anything." The boy turned his cup round without drinking. "That's the whole of it, chieftain. That's all of it and it's been all of it since November, and I've never once done a thing to a man that he didn't do to himself when he'd had a fortnight of being frightened on my account."

---

They talked for a long time. That is the part that nobody in the marches ever believed afterward, in the versions that got told.

"You said eleven days."

"I said about eleven days."

"How did you know it would be eleven?"

"I didn't," said Casian. "I knew a fat corn factor in Harrowmere would sell his spring contract in February to a carrying house because he'd done it before in '14, and I knew a baron in Thornwall would write a clerk's letter to a man who was frightened, and I knew a man who'd been at your side for twenty-two years would read a levy book in Harven and go home to his daughters." He said it without any pleasure at all. "Everything else was ordinary. Men get frightened and go home to their villages. Men don't stay in a wet field in March for a chieftain who can't pay them. You'd have known that yourself in any other year."

"I'd have known it in any other year."

"You did know it. You said it to me in a tally-room: a man of my sort doesn't last past his patron." Casian looked up. "You were right about me and you were wrong about which of us had a patron."

The lamp burned. Outside, the wall, the dark, four men asleep.

"Why did you come?" said Hask Veyr. "You could have had this without sitting in my tent. You've had it since the horses."

"Because you gave me a coin in your own hall," said the boy, "and you told me to go to bed, and you were polite to a servant, and you've been polite to me twice now. And because there's a thing I want to say to you and I've been wanting to say it since January and there's nobody else in this county who'd understand it."

"Say it."

"You could have had this county," said Casian Vale. "Not the strip. The county. In October you had a hundred men who'd have gone anywhere for you and a baron in Thornwall paying for it and the only thing standing between you and the whole of the east was a miller with a book and a fat man who wrote a levy. If you'd come in October with two hundred men and burned the mill and taken the roads, there'd have been nothing in these marches that could have stopped you, and I'd have been sixteen years old with a garden."

Hask sat very still.

"Why didn't you?"

"Because you were careful," said the boy, "and because careful men are the easiest men in the world, and I've spent five months learning to be a thing that careful men can't see."

---

Hask Veyr looked at the lamp.

He thought about his father, who had died in that slate-roofed house in the year of the bad winter, and about a hundred men on a road, and about a table with bread on it in a baron's yard.

"I'd like to sleep," he said.

"Then sleep," said Casian Vale.

---

In the morning the flap of the chieftain's tent was tied from the inside, and the lamp was burning, and there were two cups on the table and one of them was full.

There was no man in the tent.

The four men who were left in the long field at Rye stood in the door and looked at it for a while, and then one of them — a boy of nineteen from a village eleven miles off who had never wanted to be there — untied the flap and went in and put the lamp out with his fingers.

Somebody found a horse three days later, saddled, at the crossing below the Veyr, with its reins over a rail and nobody with it. The saddle was unbuckled and laid straight, with the girths folded back over the seat.

Nobody ever found the chieftain. There were six stories about it inside a year and they all disagreed about everything except one detail which nobody could explain and which everybody told: that the lamp was still burning.
