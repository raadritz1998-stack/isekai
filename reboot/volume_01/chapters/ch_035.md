Osric of Thornwall had four hundred and twelve marks of levy in a chest under his own chamber floor and he had not been able to look at the chest since the middle of March.

That was the thing nobody understood about the last month. He had not been frightened of the boy, or of the chieftain, or of the Count. He had been frightened of a chest.

The levy had come in over six months in ninety-one separate sums from four hundred and twelve households, and every one of them had been entered by Rennick in a book that said *sundries*, and every one of them had gone north over the cut in nine separate payments to a chieftain with a hundred men, and none of that was written down anywhere that a man could point at.

He had told himself for six months that a thing not written down cannot be found.

He had been wrong about that in a way that took him until April to see, and the way he saw it was this: a thing not written down cannot be *defended* either.

---

The Count's clerk came to Thornwall on the eleventh of April with six men and a commission and a copy of a report.

The report was by the Lady Seraine Varyn and it was thirty-one pages and it was the most careful document Osric had ever read in his life. It had the levy in a table. It had the receipts, and the dates of the receipts, and a column for the money that had come out of the county and a column for the money that had gone into the barony's own works, and the two columns did not agree, and it did not say why. It did not accuse anybody of anything. It simply put four hundred and twelve households on one side of a page and the sum that should have been in a strongbox on the other, and then it said, in the flat way of a woman who has decided to be dry:

*I am not able to account for the difference and I do not believe that anybody in this county is unable to account for it.*

The clerk's name was Orlan and he was fifty and had been a clerk in Harven for thirty years and had no interest in any of it.

"My lord, I'm to see the barony's account books."

"They're in the factor's hands."

"Then I'm to see the factor's hands," said Orlan.

---

Rennick came in an hour later and Osric knew, from the way he came through the door, that the thing had happened that he had been waiting four months to have happen.

"Your man's in the counting-house with the ledgers."

"Let him look. There's nothing in the ledgers."

"There's nothing in the ledgers," said Rennick, "and there's four hundred and twelve marks under your floor and there's a book of mine that's got nine payments in it to a man in the hills, and I've been sitting in my own house for a fortnight working out what happens when the clerk finds one and not the other."

Osric put his hands on the table.

"Rennick."

"My lord."

"We have been at this together for nine years."

"We have," said Rennick, "and I've done the work and you've had the name, and I've never once minded that, because a factor with a name is a man who gets hanged. And I mind it now." He put a ledger on the table. "That's the second book. It's got the sundries in it and it's got the payments over the cut and it's got four letters in the back that were made at Harrowmere in the spring of last year by a man who's dead now."

"The forged letters."

"The forged letters."

Osric looked at the book on the table for a long moment.

"You've brought them to me."

"I've brought them to *me*," said Rennick. "They've been in my house since February, where you told me to put them, and I've come to say a thing plainly to your face, which is more than you've done for me. The clerk's going to find the levy and he's going to find the payments and he's going to write down that the Baron of Thornwall kept a warlord on the road. He's not going to find the letters, because the letters were never sent and there's not a man in the county who can say what they were for."

"And what will you say?"

"I'll say what I've said all along," said Rennick, "which is that I'm a factor and I kept accounts as I was instructed, and that I've never in my life made a paper without a lord's word behind it, and I'll say it with the book in my hand and I'll be believed, because I'll be crying."

---

---

This is what Wex did, and it took him a night and a morning and he was sick twice.

He was twenty-three and his whole work was to sit at a copying table in the factor's counting-house and make a fair copy of whatever was put in front of him, and he had been doing it for four years and was good at it, and it was the only thing he had ever been good at.

On the eleventh of April a letter came down from Thornwall to be copied into the letter-book and the original was left on the table overnight, which was not usual and was not unheard of, and Wex read it because it was a page and he had four hours of copying in front of him and the light was good.

It was dated the ninth year, and it gave the whole of the valley east of the cut to the Count of Harven, and it had a seal on it, and the seal was the Baron of Vale's.

He copied it. That was the first thing he did, because it was on the table and it was his work, and he did it well and it took him a quarter of an hour.

Then he sat in the counting-house with the original in front of him and his own copy beside it and thought about the fact that he had copied a document he did not believe in.

That was where it started. Not with courage. With a copy.

He knew the Vale seal was not that seal because the Vale's own steward had come into that counting-house twice a year for nine years with receipts and Wex had copied them, and a man who copies a seal four times a year learns it the way a man learns a face.

He knew the hand was not a clerk's hand of the ninth year because the letter-forms were the letter-forms of the last five years and a man of thirty in that office knew the difference the way a smith knows two hammers.

And he knew, because he had copied every account in that counting-house for four years, that there was no entry anywhere for the money it would have cost to have a thing like that made.

So he sat there until it got dark, and then he put the copy in the fire and burned it, and then he went home and was sick in the yard, and then he came back at the sixth hour of the morning and went in to the Count's clerk.

He was in that room for two hours and he cried for the first hour of it and the clerk, whose name was Orlan and who was fifty and had no interest in any of it, sat and let him, and gave him a cup, and then asked him the only question that mattered.

"Will you say all of it in front of the baron's factor?"

"I don't know," said Wex.

"Then you'll say it to me now," said Orlan, "and I'll write it down, and it'll be your words, and when you've read it back and said it's yours, it'll be a thing a baron can't take off you, and after that we'll see about the room."

---
The letters were found in the factor's counting-house on the fourteenth of April, in a strongbox with the factor's mark on it, along with nine payments to a chieftain in the hills and a book of sundries and a letter from a man in Harrowmere who had since died.

Nobody in Thornwall ever learned how the count's clerk came to open that particular box, and there were three versions of it by the summer.

The version that was true was duller than all three. Rennick's own clerk, a boy of twenty-three called Wex who had been copying ledgers for four years and had never been paid more than eleven marks a year, had gone to the Count's man on the twelfth and told him where the second book was, because a baron's factor had left a copy of a forged letter on a copying table in April and the boy had read it, and had sat up all night, and had understood, at twenty-three, that he was the only person in that house who could not be made to say a different thing.

He was not brave. He was terrified. He was sick twice before he went in. He did it anyway, and he was the reason Thornwall fell, and there is no stone anywhere in that county with his name on it.

---

Casian Vale came to Thornwall on the nineteenth of April with two men and a cart, and the cart was Corran and Son's, and it was carrying salt.

Osric received him in the small room off the hall, because the great hall had a clerk in it.

There were four cups on the table and a jug, and Osric noticed the boy notice them and did not say anything.

"You'll have heard," said Osric.

"I heard on the sixteenth."

"Of course you did."

"I heard it from a carter," said Casian, "who heard it from a man in Harrowmere who'd heard it from a boy who works in the factor's counting-house. By the time it got to me it had you selling the county to a warlord for a chest of gold and Rennick hanging himself in his own barn."

"Rennick's in Harven."

"Yes."

"He's giving evidence."

"He's giving evidence," said Casian, "and he's very good at it, and he has a book, and in the book there is a great deal that is true and none of it is his."

Osric sat down.

"And I'm to go south."

"You're to go south. The clerk will take you to Harven and the Count will hear you in June and you'll be stripped of the barony and the levy will be repaid out of the lands and you'll be given a house and a pension and told to live quietly near your wife's people, because a Count who hangs a baron for a levy has to explain the levy and a Count who sends a baron to a small house in the south has explained nothing at all."

"That's what will happen."

"That's what will happen."

"And you've come to tell me so."

"No," said Casian Vale. "I've come to tell you what I did, because in June you'll be in a room in Harven with a lawyer and a Count and you'll want to say a thing about this county that's true, and you'll not be believed, and it'll be the only thing in your whole life that was true and couldn't be used."

---

Osric of Thornwall looked at the boy for a long moment.

"Say it, then."

"There's no curse in the eastern marches and there never was," said Casian. "The eleven out of the barn at Lowmere were taken out by men I'd hired, in the dark, down a road I'd had watched. The nine at Bradey's were let go in the morning because I told my father to let them go and he did. The nineteen off the Ashgrove road were taken off that road by forty men who came out of the willow on both sides of the ford and walked them over the cut in the rain, and they're alive, and they're at work, and eight of them have written to their families. Hask Veyr's camp came apart because he couldn't pay it and because men go home in the spring. There's no spirit in this county. There's a miller with a book and a carter with a bad hip and a boy who walks nineteen miles of a night and four hundred people who found out in October that a thing could be done on time."

The room was very quiet.

Osric found that his hands were flat on the table and that he was breathing quite fast.

"And the gates?" he said. "The crossings? The eleven gates on the cut that a scholar at Harrowmere has been writing about for eleven years?"

"I don't know about the gates," said Casian. "Neither does anybody. That's the one thing in this county I've not been able to account for and I've stopped trying."

"You expect me to believe that."

"I expect you to write it down," said the boy, "and I expect you to take it south with you and give it to the Count in June, and I expect it to do nothing at all, because a baron who has been caught with four hundred marks under his floor and a forged deed in his factor's box could tell a Count that the county is being run by a gardener of sixteen and the Count would write it in the margin as *unwell*."

Osric sat very still.

"Why?"

"Because you asked the room in January what the thing wanted," said Casian, "and you asked it in front of five men, and nobody in that room knew, and I did, and I've thought about it since."

"And what does it want?"

"To be left alone," said Casian Vale. "You gave me a coin for it. It's still the answer."

---

He went out into the yard and Osric followed him as far as the door.

The cart was loaded and the two men were on the box and the boy climbed up between them, and Osric stood in his own doorway and looked at him.

"Vale."

"Baron."

"You'll be at Harven in June?"

"I'll be at Harven in June," said Casian. "I'll be the fourth son in a good coat sitting at the back with my father, and I'll be asked nothing, and I'll say nothing, and I'll be the only person in that room who knows what happened in this county this spring."

"You'll be one of two."

"No," said Casian. "You'll be the other and you'll be in the south in a small house with a pension, and that's the thing I came to say and I'm sorry for it and I'd not change it."

---

The cart went out through the gate.

Osric of Thornwall stood in his own yard for a while, in a county he had ruled for nine years, and then he went in and sat down in the small room with the four cups and the jug and looked at the wall.

"What did he want?" said Rennick, from the door.

"He wanted me to know."

"Know what?"

"That there was a person," said Osric, "and it was a boy, and that we spent nine months looking for a warlord and a curse and a thing in the hills, and the whole of it was a boy of sixteen in a good coat who grows things, and he has beaten us, and there is not a thing in the world that either of us can do about it."

Rennick laughed — a thin, appalled noise.

"My lord, that's the best news I've had all month."

"Is it?"

"Because a curse can't be reasoned with," said Rennick, "and a boy can."

Osric of Thornwall looked at the man who had been his factor for nine years and who had sold him on the fourteenth of April in a counting-house with a book in his hand.

"Go to Harven, Rennick," he said.
