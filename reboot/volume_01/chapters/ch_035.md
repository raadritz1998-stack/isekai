Osric of Thornwall had four hundred and twelve marks of levy in an iron-bound oak chest under his own chamber floor, and he had not been able to look at the chest since the middle of March.

That was the thing nobody understood about the last month. He had not been frightened of the boy in the coat, or of the chieftain in the hills, or of the Count down in Harven. He had been frightened of a chest.

The levy had come in over six months in ninety-one separate sums from four hundred and twelve households across the southern bailiwicks. Every single sum had been taken at the factor's wicket in coin or wool-drafts, every one of them had been entered by Rennick in a tall parchment ledger under the heading of *sundries*, and every one of them had gone north over the cut in nine separate payments to a chieftain with a hundred men. None of that was written down anywhere that an outside man could point at with a finger and say *theft*.

He had told himself for six months that a thing not written down cannot be found.

He had been wrong about that in a way that took him until the wet thaw of April to see, and the way he saw it was this: a thing not written down cannot be defended either.

When a man stands in front of a magistrate with an empty chest, he can swear to his grandfather's honour that the money was spent on ditching and bridge-timbers; but when the ditches are choked with winter wrack and the bridge at Harrowmere has four rotted stringers and the people remember every silver groat they laid on the board, honour is merely a word spoken by a man who has lost his breath.

---

The Count's clerk came to Thornwall on the eleventh of April with six riders and a royal commission and a copy of a report bound in stiff calfskin.

The report was by the Lady Seraine Varyn and it was thirty-one pages long, written in a clear, upright Chancery hand that did not waste an ounce of gall-ink on ornament. It was the most careful document Osric had ever read in his fifty-five years. It had the levy set out in a three-column table: the dates of the proclamations, the receipts acknowledged by parish headmen, and the sums that had left the villages. On the facing folios it had the barony's declared disbursements for works, maintenance, and the county watch.

The two sides did not agree. They missed each other by four hundred and twelve marks, five shillings, and fourpence.

The report did not accuse Osric of treason. It did not accuse him of embezzlement. It did not mention Hask Veyr, nor the armed men camped in the eastern scrub, nor the strange disappearances on the drove road. It simply laid four hundred and twelve tenant names on the left, the missing balance on the right, and then concluded with a single sentence in the dry, unsparing register of a woman who had weighed every syllable against the law:

*I am not able to account for the difference, and I do not believe that anybody in this county is unable to account for it.*

The clerk's name was Hesk. He was fifty years old, with thin grey hair cut round his ears and deep ink-stains under his fingernails. He had served the Chancery in Harven for thirty years, and he looked at Thornwall Keep not as a fortress of stone and lime, but as a filing cupboard that had been kept in disorder.

"My lord," Hesk said, standing in the lower hall while his six riders remained mounted in the wet bailey, "I am instructed by the Count's council to examine the barony's day-books and tallies for the sixteenth and seventeenth years."

"They are in the factor's keeping," Osric said. He stood by the hearth with his hands behind his belt, trying to give the stones of his hall the dignity they had possessed in his father's time.

"Then I am to examine the factor's keeping," said Hesk. He did not take off his riding cloak. He took out an ink-horn, a bundle of quills, and a flat horn ruler, and laid them on the corner of the long table as if he had already taken possession of the room.

---

Rennick came into Osric's private solar an hour later, through the narrow turret door that led down to the counting-house. Osric knew, from the way the man closed the latch without letting the iron click, that the boundary they had lived inside for nine years had dissolved.

"Your clerk is in the counting-house," Rennick said. His round, cheerful face was pale around the jowls, though his voice was still low and brisk. "He has already called for the day-tallies from Michaelmas."

"Let him call," Osric said. "There is nothing in the day-tallies. You swore to me in November that the payments over the cut were kept in your private memorandum."

"There is nothing in the day-tallies," Rennick agreed, stepping toward the table, "and there is four hundred and twelve marks in gold under your bed-boards, and there is a memorandum in my strongbox that lists nine dates, nine sums, and Hask Veyr's mark. And I have spent three nights sitting by my hearth working out what happens when a Chancery officer from Harven finds one of those things and not the other."

Osric leaned forward, planting his palms against the dark oak boards.

"Rennick. We have walked this lane together since my brother died."

"We have," said Rennick. "And I did the figures, and I took the risk of the road, and you sat in the high seat and had the title. I never begrudged you the seat, my lord. A factor who seeks a title is an idiot who asks for a noose. But I begrudge you this."

He drew a square, pigskin-bound volume from under his woollen mantle and placed it between them.

"What is that?" Osric asked, though the scent of old lambskin and sealing wax answered him before he spoke.

"That is the second ledger," Rennick said quietly. "It has the sundries set out against the hill-drove. It has the vouchers for the carters who took the flour north. And in the parchment sleeve at the back, it has the four letters drawn up at Harrowmere in the spring of last year by that drunken scrivener who fell into the mill-leat in December."

Osric stared at the sleeve.

"The forged conveyances."

"The forged conveyances," Rennick repeated. "The ones transferring the eastern strip and the lower pastures of the Vale to the Count's direct jurisdiction upon receipt of a border petition."

"Why did you bring them out of your cellar?"

"Because I am not an idiot, my lord," Rennick said, and for the first time in nine years his eyes met Osric's without deference. "They have been in my iron chest since February, where you commanded me to lock them. But I came here to tell you how this ends, because you have spent twenty years being noble and careful, and noble men never see the ditch until their horse is neck-deep in it. The clerk Hesk is going to audit the day-tallies. He is going to match them with Lady Seraine's tables. He will find the deficiency, and when he asks me where the money went, I will produce the second ledger."

"You would turn informer against your own lord?"

"I will produce the second ledger," Rennick said calmly, "because if I do not, Hesk will sign an order for the town bailiffs to break my cellar floor, and I will hang from the market cross before May day. I am a factor. I keep books as I am directed by my master. I have never drawn a line nor stamped a seal without a lord's warrant behind it. When I lay this book before the Count's commissioners, I will tell them that I was a servant following commands under duress. I will weep, my lord. I will weep honestly, because I have four nieces and a stone house, and the commissioners will fine me two hundred marks and let me go."

"And me?" Osric whispered.

Rennick looked at him with an expression that was almost sorrowful.

"You will be a baron who hired hill-raiders to terrorize your neighbour's land, paid for with the Count's own levy. They will take your spurs, Osric. They will take Thornwall. If the Count is merciful, he will leave you an orchard and a pension near your wife's kin in the south, so that you do not sit in Harven reminding everyone of what happened."

He turned and went back down the turret stair, leaving the pigskin book lying upon the oak boards.

This is what Wex did, and it took him a night and a morning, and he was sick twice into the water-butt behind the factor's kitchen.

Wex was twenty-three years old. His father was an ale-house keeper at Rye who had died of the quinsy, and his mother had paid eight pence and two bags of meal to apprentice him to a scrivener in Thornwall because the boy had small, steady fingers and could write thirty lines of script without spilling a drop. For four years he had sat on a three-legged stool at the copying desk in the corner of Rennick's outer office, paring goose quills, boiling oak galls, and turning rough merchant notes into clear, legible rolls. He had never been paid more than eleven marks in a year, and he had never owned a horse.

On the evening of the eleventh of April, after the Count's clerk had shut himself into the factor's parlour with the primary rolls, Rennick had come through the outer office in a hurry. He had laid an unsealed conveyance upon Wex's desk, weighed down with an iron sand-pot.

*Make a fair exemplar on vellum before dawn,* the factor had said without stopping. *Do not enter it in the docket.*

Wex had trimmed his quill, lighted two tallow dips, and pulled the sheet toward him. It was dated the ninth year of the current reign. It purported to be an agreement between Baron Edric Vale and the late Count of Harven, surrendering all manorial claims to the ditch-line east of Ashgrove in consideration of fifty pounds of silver.

Wex began to copy it, because copying was what he did. His hand moved smoothly across the skin for twenty minutes, following the legal formulas.

Then he laid his pen down.

He picked up the original sheet and held it near the guttering flame.

He had copied the Vale barony's annual rent-acquittances twice a year for four seasons, whenever Edric's bailiff brought the corn-receipts to Thornwall. He knew the Vale seal as well as he knew his own mother's face. The seal on this parchment had the Vale ermine and the five willow leaves, but the second leaf on the sinister side had a blunt tip where the true iron seal had a fine, needle-like point caused by a flaw in the matrix cut forty years ago.

Moreover, the text was written in a round court hand that had only come into fashion in the south five years ago. Scriveners in Harven had used it since '12; in the ninth year, every clerk in this valley had written the cramped, angular Gothic script of the old Bishop's school.

Wex sat alone in the chilly office while the town grew silent outside. The iron pot sat on the table.

He knew what the paper was. He knew that if this deed were produced before the Count's council, it would dispossess twelve villages and three hundred households along the valley border, and that it would be done upon the strength of a leaf that lacked a needle point.

And he knew that if he finished the copy and left it on the desk, he would be the clerk who had made the forgery whole.

He sat on his stool until the third watch, with his cloak pulled tight around his neck. At four in the morning, his stomach knotted and he went out into the lane and vomited until he was dry. When he came back in, his teeth were chattering so hard he could barely hold the paper.

He took the draft he had written and held it to the flame of the candle until the ink curled and blackened and the vellum shrank into grey ash. He rubbed the ash into the floorboards with the heel of his shoe.

Then he took the original conveyance, wrapped it in a clean piece of rag, walked across the bailey in the grey mist of dawn, and knocked on the door of the guest solar where the Count's clerk slept.

Hesk opened the door himself, wearing an old woollen dressing-robe, a grease-stained woollen nightcap, and spectacles tied behind his ears with hemp string.

Wex fell to his knees on the cold flagstones. He held out the rag.

"My lord clerk," he gasped, and then his voice broke entirely and he began to sob like an eight-year-old boy in a gutter. "I am the copying clerk. I have done no harm. I have copied only what was brought to my desk."

Hesk did not shout. He took the rag, carried it to the table, and smoothed out the sheet under the light of his single lamp. He looked at the seal for ten seconds. He smelled the ink. Then he poured a cup of small beer from a stone pitcher and set it in front of the shaking boy.

"Sit on the chest," Hesk said.

"They will hang me," Wex whispered. "Rennick will say I stole it from the box."

"Rennick will say whatever keeps his own neck whole," said the clerk, sitting down opposite him. "And you will say what is true. Did you write this?"

"No, sir. It was laid upon my board last night to be copied."

"Can you show me the box where the other sheets are kept?"

"Yes, sir. It is the cedar chest bound with brass, behind the wool-press."

Hesk dipped a fresh quill into his travelling bottle.

"Then drink the beer, lad," he said gently, "and tell me every name you have written in that office since Michaelmas. I will write it down word for word. When it is on Chancery paper with my signature across the fold, neither Baron Osric nor Master Rennick will be able to take it from you."

---

The factor's brass-bound cedar chest was opened on the fourteenth of April, under the eyes of Hesk, two bailiffs, and four armed men from Harven.

Inside lay the second ledger, nine signed acknowledgments from Hask Veyr's hill captains, the draft covenants for the eastern strip, and three letters in Osric's own seal-wax discussing the deployment of the border troop.

Nobody in Thornwall ever saw Wex again. On the fifteenth, Hesk gave the young clerk four silver crowns from the royal imprest, an official safe-conduct signed with the Count's seal, and a letter of recommendation to the chief archivist of the cathedral library at Harven. Wex walked out through the water-gate before sunrise, carrying his small bundle of clothes and a spare ink-horn, and took the carrier's wagon south into a life where no one ever asked him to copy a baron's seal.

No stone in the valley bears his name; but he was the wedge that split Thornwall Keep from within.

---

Casian Vale came to Thornwall on the nineteenth of April.

He arrived not on a war-horse, nor with an escort of lances, but seated on the board of an ordinary two-wheeled farm cart loaded with ten bags of coarse grey preserving salt. The side of the cart was painted with the blue barrel-mark of Corran and Son, carriers of Ashgrove. A carter with a stiff left knee held the reins, and an upland boy walked by the mule's head.

Osric received Casian in the little vaulted parlour off the great hall. The great hall itself was unusable: Hesk and three visiting scriveners had covered the long tables with rolls, tallies, and account sheets, calling out numbers from morning until dusk.

On the table in the parlour sat four earthenware cups and an unglazed stone jug of cider. Osric noticed the boy look at the jug, look at the cups, and faintly incline his head.

"You will have heard the news," Osric said. He had not shaved in three days, and his grey hair hung untrimmed over his woollen collar.

"I heard it at Ashgrove on the sixteenth," Casian said. He wore his plain brown coat, clean and brushed, with his hands resting easily in the wide side pockets.

"The whole valley knows, I imagine."

"The whole valley knows three different stories," Casian replied mildly. "The carters say you sold the eastern strip to the King of the Scots for a wagon of bullion. The blacksmiths say Rennick poisoned your wells and fled to sea. The truest story was told by a carter's apprentice in Harrowmere, who said the Count's clerk found an iron box that shouldn't have been there."

"Rennick is bound for Harven," Osric said, staring down at his fingernails. "Under guard."

"Yes."

"He will give evidence against me."

"He will give evidence against you," said Casian, "and he will do it with great skill and copious tears. He will produce his books, and in those books every crooked groat will be laid at your door, and the commissioners will believe him because he will seem small and frightened and thoroughly obedient."

Osric sank into the heavy oak chair by the cold hearth.

"And I am to be dispossessed."

"You are to be dispossessed," Casian said. He did not sit. He leaned his shoulder lightly against the stone jamb of the window, looking out into the bailey where the salt-cart stood. "The clerk will finish his inventory by Friday. You will ride down to Harven under escort. In June, the Count will convene a court of honour behind closed doors. You will be stripped of the barony. The four hundred and twelve marks will be levied from your demesne rents over seven years. You will be assigned a manor house and a modest stipend in the south, near your wife's cousins at Morville, and you will be advised never to cross the river again."

Osric looked up at him, his brow furrowed with bitter weariness.

"You speak as if you sat on the Count's high council."

"A Count who executes a baron for an unpaid levy has to explain the levy to the Duke," Casian said quietly. "A Count who quietly removes a troublesome baron to a southern garden has to explain nothing at all. It is what Harven has done for sixty years."

"And you came here in a salt-cart to gloat over an old man?"

"No," said Casian. "I came here to tell you what happened. Because when you sit in that chamber in June, with the Count and the Bishop and three royal proctors across the cloth, you will want to tell them that you were ruined by a power they cannot comprehend. You will want to invent demons, or an army of outlaws, or a conspiracy of nobles. And if you say any of those things, they will look at one another and mark you down as a man whose mind has broken under disgrace."

---

Osric sat motionless. The room was cold; the fire had died down to white ash an hour ago, but neither man moved to stir it.

"Tell me, then," Osric said.

"There is no curse in the eastern marches," Casian said. His voice was level, conversational, and entirely devoid of heat. "There never was. The eleven hostages taken from the barn at Lowmere were brought out in the night by four men who knew the ditches, while your hill captains sat drinking around a wet fire. The nine men who took Bradey's holding were dismissed in the morning with their boots and a loaf of barley bread because I asked my father to dismiss them; a hanged tenant makes thirty bitter kin, while an unhanged tenant goes home and tells his village that the Vale barony gives bread to fools."

Osric swallowed. "And the nineteen saddles on the Ashgrove drove?"

"A dozen woodwards with whistles and twenty lengths of stout rope in the gorse," Casian said. "A horse in a fog is a nervous beast, Baron. When nineteen horses bolt into the bog and the riders find themselves surrounded by shadows that do not speak, they step down. The saddles were laid out to let Hask know that his men were alive. They are alive now. They are working four farms behind Fenwick. Eight of them have already sent word to their wives."

"And Hask himself?"

"Hask Veyr's men left him because an army without bread or coin cannot survive eleven days in a wet field. When a chieftain summons four hundred spears and nine turn up, his chieftaincy is over. He saw it. He walked away."

Osric's hands tightened on the arms of his chair until his knuckles were white.

"And the crossings? The eleven ancient gates on the cut that Selwyn has been measuring for a decade?"

"I know nothing about the gates," Casian said simply. "Nobody does. The old people built them five hundred years ago, and some of the shepherds mend them when the sheep stray. That is the only mystery in the valley, and it has nothing to do with you, nor with me, nor with Thornwall."

Osric stared at the boy. The spring light fell across Casian's face, revealing only the clear skin of a youth of sixteen who spent his mornings pruning fruit trees and his afternoons checking water-channels. There was no malice in his grey eyes, no triumph, no haughtiness.

"You expect me to believe that?" Osric rasped. "That a boy of sixteen, with a cart of salt and a miller's ledger, pulled down the strongest lordship between Harven and the hills?"

"I expect you to believe it," Casian said, "because it is the only thing that happened. And I expect you to say nothing about it to the Count. If you tell the Count that a fourth son who grows roses out-thought your factor, your captains, and your chieftain, he will add madness to your sentence."

Osric looked down at the table. On it, the four cups and the jug seemed to mock him.

"Why?" Osric asked. The question was barely more than a breath. "Why do all this? You could have let the levy pass. You could have let Hask take the strip. Your father would have paid a fine and kept his orchards."

"Because you asked a question in this room in January," Casian said. "You had five men at your table, and you asked them what the thing in the valley wanted. None of them knew. I was standing by the sideboard with an ale-jug, and I gave you the answer."

Osric remembered. The memory struck him like a blow to the ribs: the warm hall, the smell of roasted mutton, the boy in the servant's apron.

*To be left alone,* the boy had said.

*Here is a coin for your cheek,* Osric had answered, laughing, tossing a silver penny across the boards.

"It is still the answer," Casian said. "The Vale wants to be left alone. We want our ditches clear, our corn ground, and our people unmolested. You would not leave us alone, Baron. So I made the cost of bothering us larger than the whole of Thornwall."

---

Casian turned and walked out through the heavy timber door.

Osric followed him into the bailey, drawn by an impulse he could neither control nor explain. The afternoon was turning grey with drizzle. The cart of Corran and Son was turning slowly in the gravel toward the main gatehouse. Casian swung himself lightly up between the carter and the sack-ties, pulling his woollen hood over his ears against the rain.

"Vale!" Osric called out.

The cart paused. Casian turned his head.

"Will you be at the trial in Harven?" Osric shouted across the muddy stones.

"I will be there," Casian called back. "I will be the fourth son sitting behind my father, wearing a decent grey wool coat, looking respectfully at the tapestries. I will not be asked a single question, and I will offer no answers. I will be entirely ordinary."

"There is nothing ordinary about you," Osric said, his voice dropping as the cart moved away. "Nothing in this world."

Casian did not reply. The cart rattled across the iron drawbridge, its wheels splashing in the ruts of the road that led south toward Ashgrove, until the mist swallowed it whole.

Osric stood in the rain until his sleeves were sodden. Then he turned back into the keep and went into the little parlour.

The door creaked behind him. Rennick stood there, wrapped in his heavy travelling cloak, his saddle-bags slung over one shoulder, two armed Harven bailiffs waiting at the stair-foot behind him.

"What did the boy want?" Rennick asked, glancing nervously about the room.

"He wanted me to know the truth," Osric said, sitting back down before the empty hearth.

"What truth?"

"That there was no demon," Osric said softly. "No warlord. No ancient curse rising from the moss. Just a boy of sixteen who wanted to prune his trees, and who did the sums while we were making grand speeches."

Rennick let out a short, harsh breath that might have been a laugh.

"Well, my lord," the factor muttered, adjusting his cloak, "if that is the truth, it is the best news I have heard this spring."

Osric looked up at him with cold, exhausted eyes.

"How could that be good news?"

"Because a curse cannot be reasoned with," said Rennick, his small eyes glittering with a sudden, sharp calculation that made Osric's skin crawl. "And a boy can."

Osric of Thornwall closed his eyes and leaned his forehead against his cold hands.

"Go to Harven, Rennick," he whispered. "Go to Harven, and may God have mercy on whoever tries to buy that boy."
