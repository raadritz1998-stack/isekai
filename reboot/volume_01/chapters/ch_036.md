Warden Selwyn of the Ash was fifty-two years old and had spent eleven of those years being wrong in public, and he had come to the eastern marches in the last week of April with two servants, a mule, four blank books and no authority whatsoever.

That was the thing men never understood about the Ash. A Warden had no power to compel, no power to try, and no power to burn. He could write. That was the whole of it: he could ride into a county, ask questions, write down what he was told, and send it to a college of eleven old men at Harrowmere who would read it and either file it or send it on to the Church.

In eleven years Selwyn had sent on four reports and all four had been about observances that had died out before his grandfather was born, and two of them had been praised.

---

He began with the gates, because the gates were his own subject and had been since he was forty-one.

There were nineteen miles of cut above the eastern strip and along it, at intervals that no man had ever been able to explain, there were crossings: old stone-paved fords led down through the bank in eleven places, each one with a gate that was not a gate.

He counted eleven. He had expected four.

That was the first thing.

The second thing was that seven of the eleven were in repair, and the repairs were of different ages, and one of them had been done with a mortar that Selwyn had seen before in a chapel at Harrowmere that had been built in the year 940.

He sat on the bank above the ninth crossing with a lens and a brush and a boy holding a slate and was, for the first time in eleven years, frightened in a professional way.

"And the newest?" he said.

"The one at the beck, sir. Two or three years, I'd say."

"Two or three years."

The mending at the beck was three courses of stone laid true and set with a lime-and-ash mortar, and it was the work of a man who had been taught, and it was not the work of the Church, and it was not the work of any man in the eastern marches, because Selwyn had spent four days asking.

He asked at the mill. The miller — a woman of forty with her sleeves tied back — listened to the whole of it and then said, "I know nothing about gates," and went back to her work, and Selwyn wrote, in his careful hand: *M. Corran, miller, Ashgrove. Knows nothing about gates. I believe her.*

He asked at the inn at Ashgrove and the innkeeper, who was sixty-one, said, "There's four gates on the cut and there always has been," and Selwyn wrote that down too, and then wrote underneath it: *W. keeps a tally of carts for eleven years and cannot be mistaken about a road. He is not lying. He believes there are four.*

---

He spent the first week on the ground and the second week on the people, and by the middle of May he had a book of testimony and it was, he thought, the best book of testimony he had ever made.

**From a woodward at Ashgrove.** Eight men came down a lane in February, in good order, three with bows. He counted them because a woodward counts things. He lay in a ditch and said the psalm. Four minutes later the lane was empty and there were no tracks in wet ground and his dog would not stand up for a quarter of an hour.

**From a captain called Berrin.** Twenty-three men on an open road in daylight. Nineteen of them off it while he turned his head. Nineteen horses standing, saddles unbuckled and laid straight.

**From an old woman at a holding above the mill.** Nine men came and were taken and were returned nine days later with their boots, and one of them gave her grandson a penny.

**From a woman from the upland** who would not give her name for the first half hour and then gave it — Vesna Orrow — who had walked nineteen miles of the cut and had a book with names in it and was the only witness in the county who answered a question with a number.

**From the chieftain's own man, in Harrowmere, in a room with the door shut.** Four hours, and at the end of it Selwyn had a full account of the eleven days in the long field at Rye, and he had to stop writing twice, because his hand was shaking.

"And the lamp?" he said.

"The lamp was burning," said Tolan. "Two cups on the table. One of them full."

"And the flap?"

"Tied from the inside."

Selwyn put his pen down.

"Master Tolan, I have written eleven years of these. I have written about wells that were filled in and a bell that was buried and a family that would not eat pork and did not know why. I have never in my life been given testimony like this and I have never once been given it by men who had no interest in the answer."

"You'll be the only man in the county who wants it," said Tolan.

"I know. That's why I'll get it." Selwyn took his pen up. "Give me the eleven days again, and this time tell me what the men said to each other in the mornings."

---

He went to Vale on the eighteenth of May and was received courteously by a baron who was tired and by a fourth son who was in his shirtsleeves and had soil on his hands.

He had not expected the garden.

And before the garden he sat with the Baron of Vale in a small room with the window open and asked him one question and got an answer he did not write down.

"Baron, I'm to write a report. What would you have me put in it?"

Edric Vale looked at him for a while.

"Nothing about my fourth son," he said.

"That's not a thing I can promise."

"No. I know." The baron turned his cup. "Then I'll tell you what I'd have you put in it, and it's true, and you may do as you like with it. Whatever has happened in these marches since the autumn, it has been better for four thousand people than anything that happened in the nine years before it, and it has been done without a lord, and I am a lord and I have thought about that every night since February."

"That's a large thing to say."

"It's the only large thing I've got," said Edric Vale, "and I'd have you notice that I'm not asking you to leave it out. I'm asking you to notice it. There's a difference, and it's the whole of what I am."

He put the cup down.

"And Warden — my son will be a great man, and I've known it since September, and the thing I want written down is not that. It's that he did it in a county where the thing that makes a man great usually costs eleven villages, and it hasn't. Not one. Not this year." The baron looked out of the window. "I don't know how long a thing like that holds. I'd like it in a box somewhere, in case it doesn't."

---

They walked it for an hour. Selwyn asked about the roses and got an answer of twenty minutes' length about cuttings and rootstock and the difference between a rose that is grown for a wall and a rose that is grown for a hedge, and it was the first time in three weeks that anybody in that county had answered a question with no story wrapped round it.

"You've a good hand with the knife," said Selwyn.

"I have a good hand with a knife and no patience," said the boy, "and the roses are the only thing in this county that will take a straight answer."

"And the rest of it?"

"The rest of it wants a story," said Casian Vale.

---

They sat at the end of the garden on a bench with the wall behind them, and the Warden of the Ash asked his questions in the order he always asked them, and got answers that were pleasant and true and useless.

*What do you make of the cut?* — An old road, poorly kept, and worth keeping.

*Do you know why the gates are mended and nobody will say who mended them?* — No, and I have wondered.

*Have you heard the stories about this county?* — Everybody has heard the stories about this county.

*Do you think they're true?* — I think something happened and the stories are what people have instead of the thing.

And then Selwyn, who was fifty-two and had spent his life being wrong in public, put his pen down and asked the one question he had come to ask.

"Master Vale, a great many good things have happened in these marches since the autumn. A millrace. A road mended in February at eight marks. A corn contract bought in the same month. A mill that has water in it and a barony that has four hundred people on it who will not go to a lawyer. And in the middle of all of it, a young man was quiet in his father's garden and nobody in the county could tell me why."

Casian Vale looked out at the wall.

"That's not a question," he said.

"No. It's a courtesy, and I've been offering it for eleven years, and about one man in fifty takes it up." Selwyn turned to face him. "So here is the question. Are you frightened of anything?"

"No."

"Not of the Church?"

"No."

"Not of me?"

"No."

"Not of what happens when men find out that a thing can be done in a county without a lord or a bishop saying so first?"

And the boy was quiet for a moment, and then said the thing that Selwyn of the Ash wrote down that night in his own book, in the back, where he wrote the things that did not go into the report:

"Warden, I'm not frightened of a thing finding out. I'm frightened of a thing being believed and being wrong, and being believed for a hundred years, and a man in the year 1317 doing a thing in the dark because of a story we told in this one."

---

He wrote the report in a room at the inn at Harrowmere over eleven days, and he wrote it slowly and it was forty pages and it was the best work of his life.

He put the facts first, because facts were his discipline and had been since he was forty-one.

*The crossings on the cut number eleven and not four. Seven are in repair. The most recent repair is two to three years old and is the work of a trained mason.*

*The stone at Fenwick was moved two or three seasons before the events described here, and no man in the county admits to it and no man in the county has been paid for it.*

*A column of twenty-three men was reduced to four on an open road in daylight on the 24th of February. Nineteen men are unaccounted for. Nineteen horses were found standing, saddled, with girths folded. Their halters were unbuckled and laid straight.*

*The chieftain of the Veyr country was not killed. No man witnessed his removal. His tent was found with the lamp burning, two cups upon the table, and the flap tied from the inside.*

Then he set out the observances, because observances were his subject. He set out the Keeping in its eleven recorded forms and its four surviving ones, and the carrying of the ash, and the fact that in the eastern marches alone there were nineteen places where the custom had been maintained without interruption by families who could not say what it was for.

And then he came to the conclusion, and he wrote it four times and crossed out three.

*I am of the opinion that the eastern marches are under an observance older than the Ash and older than the parish, that the crossings on the cut were kept by somebody for a great while and were let fall, and that in the last three years they have been taken up again.*

*I am of the opinion that what has been described to me in this county is not a warlord, and not a fraud, and not a company of men. It is a keeping that has woken up and does not know its own name, and it acts in the manner of such things — quietly, on time, and in the shape of the thing that each man already feared.*

*It has killed no man that I can establish. It has returned men to their villages. It has fed two hundred men who came to burn it, which no observance in my eleven years of study has ever done, and which I am not able to explain and have not attempted to explain.*

*I recommend that the Church send no man into these marches to put a stop to it. I recommend this with all the weight a Warden of the Ash may put on a thing, and I note that we have four times in my lifetime sent a man to put a stop to a thing and have four times been wrong, and that in the third of those cases eleven people were burned in a village that had done nothing at all.*

---

He gave the report to Tolan to read before it went, which was not a thing a Warden did, because Tolan had been the best witness he had ever had and because the man had asked.

Tolan read it in the room with the door shut and took two hours over it and did not speak for a while at the end.

"Well?"

"You've got every fact in it right," said Tolan, "and I've checked them against my own books and there's not a date in it that isn't correct to the day. It's the best account of this county that will ever be written."

"And the last four pages."

"The last four pages are wrong."

"I know," said Selwyn.

"You know."

"I've known since the garden at Vale," said the Warden of the Ash, "and I've written them anyway, and I'd like to tell you why, and then I'd like you to tell me whether I'm a coward or a liar."

He put his hand flat on the report.

"If I write what I believe — that a boy of sixteen in these marches has done all of this by knowing what a road is for and what a man is frightened of, and that he did it without a lord, without a bishop, without a single man dying who did not have to, and that the men who tried to stop him are gone and he is still here — then this report goes to eleven old men at Harrowmere and it goes on to the Church, and the Church will do the thing the Church always does. It will not believe it. It will look for the hand of a heresy, because a thing that does good without authority is the one thing the Church cannot allow to be true, and it will send a man, and the man will find four hundred people who will not say a word against the mill, and it will end with a village burned to teach the others."

"And this?"

"This will be filed," said Selwyn. "A Warden's report about an old observance in a poor county is a thing they put in a box. Nobody burns a village over a curse, Master Tolan. They burn a village over a heresy."

Tolan sat back.

"And in thirty years?"

"In thirty years," said Selwyn, "somebody will read it in a box, and they'll find that every fact in it is right, and the conclusion is one a man can see the shape of, and by then there will be a boundary in these marches and a road and four hundred names, and it will all be in a box with a wrong answer written at the bottom of it. And there will be nobody left who can say what it was."

"That's the worst thing I've ever heard a good man say."

"Yes," said Warden Selwyn. "I'm fifty-two and I've spent eleven years being wrong in public, and I've just found out that being wrong in public is a thing a man can be *paid for*, and I'm going to send this report to Harrowmere and I'm going to sleep tonight, and I'd like you to sign it as a witness so that at least one true name is on the wrong document."

---

Tolan signed it.

He signed it at the bottom in his own hand, and then he wrote, underneath, in the small careful letters he used when he was writing something he intended to be read by a stranger in fifty years:

*Attested as to the facts. The opinion is the Warden's.*

*Signed, Tolan, third captain to the Chieftain of the Veyr, in the year 1217, who has written everything down and does not know what it was either.*
