The Count of Harven heard the eastern matter in the second week of June, in a long room with a bad ceiling, and it took six days, and Seraine Varyn sat through every hour of it.

She had been in the east five months by then. She had written thirty-one pages and a letter, and had her letter read by one man and her report read by a great many, and she had spent the last of the spring in a rented house in Harrowmere waiting for a hearing and a father who did not come.

Her father did not come. He sent the Marshal instead, and this is the thing that made the summer what it was, and she understood it on the first morning in about four minutes.

---

The Royal Marshal of Varenthos was called Orlan and he was forty-six and he had been a soldier since he was fourteen and he had been made Marshal for the reason that Marshals are made: because he had never lost anything and had never wanted anything.

He was courteous to her. That was the first warning.

"Your report is the best thing of its kind I've read in twenty years," he said, on the Sunday before the hearing, over a table he had paid for. "The table of households alone. A man could take that into any county in the realm."

"Thank you."

"Your father asked me to be careful of you."

"Did he."

"He said you'd written him a letter he'd not answer in writing, and that I was to be careful of you, and that if I found you in a room with a thing that couldn't be explained I was to remember that you are nineteen and that the east is a country that makes sensible people strange." Orlan turned his cup. "I'm telling you this because I don't care for doing a thing behind a young woman's back, and because I'd like to know what was in the letter."

"Then you'll have to be a man who doesn't know."

"I thought so," said the Marshal, and he was not angry, and he went on being courteous for six days, and on the fifth day she found out what courtesy was for.

---

Rennick sent for her on the third evening, which was not permitted, and she went, which was not wise, and it was the most useful hour of her week.

They met in a room off the factor's lodging with a servant in the corner and the door propped open, because a factor who has been giving evidence for a day and a half does not receive a duke's daughter with the door shut.

"My lady. I'm told you wrote the report."

"I wrote the report."

"It's a good report." Rennick was fifty and had cried twice in the hearing and had a face that was pleasant when it was not doing anything. "There's a table in it. The table's the trouble. A man can argue with a story and a man cannot argue with a table."

"Then you should have kept a better book."

"I kept an excellent book," said Rennick. "That's the whole of what's wrong with it."

She looked at him for a while.

"What do you want?"

"To say a thing to you that I'd not say to a man," said Rennick, "and then to go back to my lodging. My lord of Thornwall is finished, and I'm a factor with nine years of a baron's accounts in my head, and when this is over there'll be a new baron at Thornwall and he'll need a factor who knows the county and he'll not be able to say so out loud. So I'll be here in nine years. That's the first thing."

"And the second?"

"The second is that you've decided there's something in the east that isn't a levy, and I've read your Marshal's remarks and the Warden's report both, and I think the pair of you are chasing a boy." Rennick turned his cup. "My lady, I've been in that county all my life. There's no curse. There's a corn factor who sold a contract to a carrying house because the price was good, and a miller who's clever with a book, and a baron who's tired. And there's a boy in a garden who's had a very good year, and do you know what a boy like that is?"

"Tell me."

"A boy like that can be reasoned with," said Rennick. "Give him a thing he wants and a name in a hall and he'll be a baron's fourth son again inside two years, and he'll marry badly and get fat and his brothers will be glad of him. A curse can't be reasoned with. A boy can. I'd have thought a duke's daughter would like that better than a mystery."

Seraine Varyn sat with her hands folded.

"Master Rennick," she said, "you have just described a plan to buy a man, to his face in front of me, in a room with the door open, and you have done it without once asking whether I would tell anybody."

"You'll not tell anybody."

"Why not?"

"Because if I'm right, it's the only sensible thing anybody's said in that county this year," said Rennick, "and because if I'm wrong about him, then he's a thing that can't be reasoned with and a curse and a spirit, and a woman who's written the report she's written would want to know which."

He stood up.

"I'll send you a note in the winter," he said. "It'll be about a bridge."

---

The hearing itself was dull, and the dullness was Casian's doing.

It came out over four days, in tables and tallies, under the heavy timber roof of the Count's high chamber, while the June heat gathered like wool in the corners of the room. It was the least dramatic inquiry Seraine had ever watched. The Count's clerk, a dry man called Hesk with ink-dark cuffs and a rasping cough, presented the levy: four hundred and twelve households, two thousand eight hundred and sixty marks collected in drafts and wool-notes, and only seven hundred and thirty-eight that could be vouched for in the timber-books.

Rennick gave evidence for a day and a half. He sat on the witness stool with a handkerchief balled in his palm, cried twice when speaking of his late master's generosity, and was impeccably prepared. He named the Baron of Thornwall eleven times and himself never, producing vouchers signed by Osric's bailiffs that made every irregular penny seem like the direct command of a desperate lord.

Osric did not attend. He was already in the south by then, living in a quiet timbered manor near Morville with an orchard and a small pension, accompanied by his wife's cousins and a physician. His proctor, an elderly advocate from the Chancery bar, read a statement of nine lines that admitted the collected levy, denied all knowledge of forged covenants, and placed the fallen baron upon the Count's Christian mercy.

The forged conveyances were read out on the fourth morning. They were dated the ninth year of the reign, transferring all manorial rights to the lands east of the cut to the Count's direct custody, and they bore the five-leaf willow seal of the Vale barony. They were, Seraine thought, extraordinarily skilful forgeries, and it took Hesk eleven minutes to demonstrate why they were false: the matrix of the seal had been copied from an impression that lacked the flaw in the sinister leaf, the legal formula belonged to the chancery usage of '12, and the parchment itself was nine years too fresh to have spent a decade in a strongbox.

And then the Baron of Vale came in.

Edric Vale answered for his barony for two hours, standing before the dais with his grey head bare and his hands resting on the iron buckle of his sword-belt. In that stifling room he was exactly what he was: a tired, honest, unremarkable border lord who had held twenty miles of difficult valley for thirty years and had nothing theatrical to say about anything that had occurred since the autumn.

That was the thing. Seraine watched it happen from the gallery and could not stop it.

They asked Edric about the cattle-raids. He said nine men had come to Bradey's holding in February and had been captured and released by his own order, and he gave his reasons: an executed tenant makes thirty blood-enemies, while an unhanged tenant returns home and tells his village that the Vale barony feeds fools. The council nodded; the reason was sensible, frugal, and traditional.

They asked about the nineteen men who had vanished off the Ashgrove road on the twenty-fourth of February. Edric said he did not know where they had gone, which was entirely true; but it was true in the flat, unembellished way of a man who refused to speculate, and in a court of inquiry a lord who says he does not know sounds like a lord who is shielding his retainers. The Count dipped his pen and made a thin tick in the margin of the roll.

They asked about Hask Veyr. Edric said the chieftain's camp at Rye had broken up when the men ran out of bread, and that the chieftain had ridden away into the uplands. They asked where the chieftain had gone. Edric said nobody in the valley knew.

And on the fifth morning the Royal Marshal of Varenthos rose from his chair beside the dais and said the thing that Seraine had spent four months trying to prevent and had never once been able to write down.

"My lord Count, I have ridden in the eastern country this spring," Marshal Orlan said. He put both palms flat on the green baize table. He was forty-six years old, square-shouldered, with the calm authority of a man who had commanded two thousand horse at the border without ever raising his voice. "I have spoken with the Warden of the Ash, who has compiled forty pages of scholarly testimony on the subject. I have spoken with the factor Rennick, and with eleven troopers who were camped at Rye in March. There is a thing in the eastern marches that is not an unpaid levy, and not a border raid, and not a forged deed. It has removed nineteen armed riders from an open road in broad daylight without a blow struck. It has dismantled a camp of sixty men in eleven days without drawing a drop of blood. And it has taken a highland chieftain out of his own tent while the lamp was still burning on his table. I have four depositions regarding the road and two regarding the tent, and having read Warden Selwyn's parchment, I do not believe the Warden understands what he has recorded."

The chamber grew utterly quiet. Even the clerk Hesk stopped his scratching quill.

"I am not asking the Count to dispatch a detachment of foot," the Marshal continued, looking across at the bench where the judges sat. "I am asking the Count to record that whatever is happening in the eastern marches is not a matter of missing marks, and that in ten years it will not be a small matter, and that the crown possesses no officer in that valley and no current means of stationing one there."

The Count of Harven, who was sixty years old and weary of border squabbles, leaned back in his tall carved chair, drew a line across the bottom of the folio, and said, "Noted."

Then he turned the page and called for the reeve of Ashford to report upon the rotted timbers of the parish bridge.

---

She got Casian Vale alone on the sixth day, in the cloister of the Count's hall, in the late afternoon.

The cloister was cool and smelled of damp river-stone and box-hedges. She had planned this conversation for four months, rehearsing every accusation and deduction, and it lasted scarcely fifteen minutes.

"Master Vale."

"Lady Varyn."

"You've grown," she said.

"Sixteen does that," he replied mildly, stopping beside the low stone parapet that looked out into the courtyard fountain. "I'm told it stops before twenty."

She looked at him closely. He was dressed in a well-fitting coat of dark grey wool, clean and unadorned, and for six days he had sat at the back of the inquiry hall beside his father and his elder brother Gareth. Gareth, who was twenty-one and carried his father's broad shoulders, had sat stiff with misery in his stiff linen collar, scowling at the clerks and looking like a young soldier who wished someone would draw steel. Casian had looked like nothing at all. He had stood when the Count entered, sat when the judges sat, and watched the dust motes drift across the shafts of sunlight like an apprentice who had been brought along to carry the baggage.

"I am going to say several things to you," Seraine said, keeping her voice low and even, "and I would like you to let me finish before you offer an answer."

"Go on," he said.

"I know what happened at Lowmere," she said. "I know about the barn and the eleven villagers who walked out through the dark without a sound. I know about the eleven ancient gates on the cut and the trained mason who mends them with four-hundred-year-old mortar, while the Warden of the Ash writes forty pages of theology about a curse. I know that the Ashgrove bridge was closed in February by a legal complaint that was entirely genuine and paid for with four marks by an unknown carter, and that the hill-drove was repaired in the same week for eight marks by a carrying firm that had only been licensed in December."

She paused, watching his grey eyes, but Casian did not blink.

"I know the Harrowmere corn contract," she continued. "I know the millrace and the ditch dug in broad daylight in front of forty witnesses off a parish notice. I know that four hundred and twelve households in that valley paid an illegal levy, and that today not one of those four hundred households can settle a boundary dispute or a ditch-right without asking Maeve Corran at the mill."

"And?" Casian asked.

"And I have walked that road myself," Seraine said. "I have counted the horses. I have sat for four hours in a back room with a cavalry captain who cannot sleep because nineteen saddles were laid straight on the grass. I have written thirty-one pages of evidence. I brought all of it to Harven, to the one court in the realm with the power to act, and it amounts to nothing."

She drew a breath, the stone parapet cold beneath her fingers.

"Nothing at all," she said. "The Count noted the Marshal's warning and moved on to a broken bridge. The Warden's parchment will be buried in an archive at Harrowmere. Osric is exiled to a southern orchard, Rennick has a clean ledger, and you are the fourth son of an unoffending border lord. There is not a magistrate in Varenthos who would accept a charge against you."

The cloister fountain splashed in the silence.

"That's not a question," Casian said.

"No," said Seraine. "The question is this. In January I came to your valley looking for the man who was moving the pieces. I found you. And I have spent four months trying to decide whether you are the best thing that has ever happened to the eastern marches, or the most dangerous thing that has ever entered them. And I have found nothing in your ledgers or your gardens that allows me to decide. So tell me what you want."

---

Casian did not answer immediately. He looked at the water falling into the stone basin, his expression quiet, completely free of the mockery or cleverness that usually inhabited men of ambition.

"I want there to be something in that county that does not need me," he said at last. "That is the whole of it. A mill with water in the leat, a road that is kept by a licensed book rather than a lord's whim, eleven places where a carter can water his team without paying toll, and a miller who can say no to a baron because her accounts are cleaner than his."

"That is not what you have made," Seraine countered.

"It is what I am trying to make," said Casian. "What I have made so far is twenty-eight people who cannot buy seed or settle a fence without asking me first. I know the number. I count them at night before I sleep, and it is the heaviest thing about this year, and I have not found the bottom of it."

"And the men who died?"

"Nine at Ashgrove in the ditch," he said, and his voice did not waver. "Five on the hill road. And a master chirurgeon named Aldous, who was fifty years old, and who was knifed in a lane forty yards from my father's gate because I was waiting for a number to be right. I did not strike any of them, Lady Varyn, and every one of them is mine."

Seraine looked at him. The frankness of the confession struck her harder than any evasion could have done. There was no posturing in it, no false humility, only the cold, arithmetic reckoning of a gardener measuring a winter blight.

"Casian," she said, using his Christian name for the first time, "my father will send me back to the east in the autumn. The crown has resolved to demand a quarterly report on the eastern bailiwicks, and I am the only investigator who knows the roads. I am going to write those reports. I will write them knowing that you are behind every ditch and wagon. And I will write them in a manner that shields four thousand country people who deserve to harvest their wheat in peace, and one boy whom I still cannot measure."

"That is a very good use of a report," Casian said.

"It is my decision," said Seraine. "I chose it myself. Nobody arranged it for me, and nobody bought me with a dinner or a promise. When I am sixty, I will still be able to say that."

---

She left the cloister and walked into the outer bailey, where the horses were being brought out. Marshal Orlan was standing near the mounting block, his grey charger held by a groom.

"Well?" Orlan asked, turning his sharp gaze upon her.

"He is sixteen years old, he speaks the exact truth within the bounds he chooses, and he gave me nothing that can be written in a warrant," Seraine said.

"I know," said the Marshal. "I was behind the third pillar."

Seraine halted. "That was a private interview."

"It was an open arcade in a Count's palace," Orlan replied calmly, taking his reins. "Lady Varyn, your father instructed me to watch over you. I have watched you all week. I will tell you what I think, and then I will mount my horse and write my dispatch to the King's Council, and you and I will never need to debate this again."

He stepped up into the stirrup and looked down at her from the saddle.

"You came to this valley expecting to find a rebel or a saint. You found neither. You found a boy with a rare talent for governance and an absolute refusal to be a lord. In forty years, there will be ten thousand people in those hills whose bread, law, and taxes are governed by customs he invented when he was sixteen in his father's rose garden. Nobody will ever elect him, nobody will ever knight him, and nobody will ever be able to unseat him. That is a completely new thing in this realm, my lady, and it will not remain confined to the eastern hills for long."

"And what should I do?" Seraine asked.

"Do your duty," the Marshal said. "You are nineteen years of age. Keep your eyes clear, write your facts down, and ensure you are in the room when the great decisions are made. That is all that is granted to people of our class, and it is a good deal more than is granted to the carters."

He touched his spurs to the horse and rode out through the archway into the city.

---

Seraine wrote her quarterly report that night in her rented chamber in Harven, working by the light of three beeswax candles until the bells of the cathedral rang for prime.

The report was four folios long. It contained every receipt, every parish date, every verified tally, and not a single speculative conclusion. It went south to the Duke's Chancery on the fourteenth of June.

At the foot of the fourth page, in the small, firm hand she reserved for decisions she had tested against her conscience, she wrote two lines, drew a single dark stroke through the first, and then wrote it again below the line, knowing that a deleted sentence in an official roll is the one that every chancellor reads twice:

*The eastern marches are quiet and will remain quiet. There is no heresy in them, and there is no warlord, and I recommend that the crown leave them alone and that no man be sent to put a stop to anything.*

*I will not write another report that does not say that.*

---

She wrote her quarterly report that night in Harven and put in it every fact she had and not one conclusion, and it was four pages and it was the most careful thing she had ever written, and it went south on the fourteenth of June.

And on the last page, in the small hand she used for the things she had decided, she wrote a line that she crossed out and then wrote again underneath, because she had learned in five months that a thing crossed out is a thing a man reads twice:

*The eastern marches are quiet and will remain quiet. There is no heresy in them, and there is no warlord, and I recommend that the crown leave them alone and that no man be sent to put a stop to anything.*

*I will not write another report that does not say that.*
