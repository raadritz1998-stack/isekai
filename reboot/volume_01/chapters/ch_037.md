The Count of Harven heard the eastern matter in the second week of June, in a long room with a bad ceiling, and it took six days, and Seraine Varyn sat through every hour of it.

She had been in the east five months by then. She had written thirty-one pages and a letter, and had her letter read by one man and her report read by a great many, and she had spent the last of the spring in a rented house in Harrowmere waiting for a hearing and a father who did not come.

Her father did not come. He sent the Marshal instead, and this is the thing that made the summer what it was, and she understood it on the first morning in about four minutes.

---

The Royal Marshal of Varenthos was called Orlan and he was forty-six and he had been a soldier since he was fourteen and he had been made Marshal for the reason that Marshals are made: because he had never lost anything and had never wanted anything.

He was courteous to her. That was the first warning.

"Your report is the best thing of its kind I've read in twenty years," he said, on the Sunday before the hearing, over a table he had paid for. "The table of households alone. A man could take that into any county in the realm."

"Thank you."

"Your father asked me to be careful of you."

"Did he."

"He said you'd written him a letter he'd not answer in writing, and that I was to be careful of you, and that if I found you in a room with a thing that couldn't be explained I was to remember that you are nineteen and that the east is a country that makes sensible people strange." Orlan turned his cup. "I'm telling you this because I don't care for doing a thing behind a young woman's back, and because I'd like to know what was in the letter."

"Then you'll have to be a man who doesn't know."

"I thought so," said the Marshal, and he was not angry, and he went on being courteous for six days, and on the fifth day she found out what courtesy was for.

---

Rennick sent for her on the third evening, which was not permitted, and she went, which was not wise, and it was the most useful hour of her week.

They met in a room off the factor's lodging with a servant in the corner and the door propped open, because a factor who has been giving evidence for a day and a half does not receive a duke's daughter with the door shut.

"My lady. I'm told you wrote the report."

"I wrote the report."

"It's a good report." Rennick was fifty and had cried twice in the hearing and had a face that was pleasant when it was not doing anything. "There's a table in it. The table's the trouble. A man can argue with a story and a man cannot argue with a table."

"Then you should have kept a better book."

"I kept an excellent book," said Rennick. "That's the whole of what's wrong with it."

She looked at him for a while.

"What do you want?"

"To say a thing to you that I'd not say to a man," said Rennick, "and then to go back to my lodging. My lord of Thornwall is finished, and I'm a factor with nine years of a baron's accounts in my head, and when this is over there'll be a new baron at Thornwall and he'll need a factor who knows the county and he'll not be able to say so out loud. So I'll be here in nine years. That's the first thing."

"And the second?"

"The second is that you've decided there's something in the east that isn't a levy, and I've read your Marshal's remarks and the Warden's report both, and I think the pair of you are chasing a boy." Rennick turned his cup. "My lady, I've been in that county all my life. There's no curse. There's a corn factor who sold a contract to a carrying house because the price was good, and a miller who's clever with a book, and a baron who's tired. And there's a boy in a garden who's had a very good year, and do you know what a boy like that is?"

"Tell me."

"A boy like that can be reasoned with," said Rennick. "Give him a thing he wants and a name in a hall and he'll be a baron's fourth son again inside two years, and he'll marry badly and get fat and his brothers will be glad of him. A curse can't be reasoned with. A boy can. I'd have thought a duke's daughter would like that better than a mystery."

Seraine Varyn sat with her hands folded.

"Master Rennick," she said, "you have just described a plan to buy a man, to his face in front of me, in a room with the door open, and you have done it without once asking whether I would tell anybody."

"You'll not tell anybody."

"Why not?"

"Because if I'm right, it's the only sensible thing anybody's said in that county this year," said Rennick, "and because if I'm wrong about him, then he's a thing that can't be reasoned with and a curse and a spirit, and a woman who's written the report she's written would want to know which."

He stood up.

"I'll send you a note in the winter," he said. "It'll be about a bridge."

---

The hearing itself was dull, and the dullness was Casian's doing.

It came out over four days, in tables, and it was the least dramatic thing Seraine had ever watched. The Count's clerk, a dry man called Hesk, presented the levy: 412 households, 2,860 marks collected, 738 that could be accounted for. Rennick gave evidence for a day and a half and cried twice and was very good, and named the Baron of Thornwall eleven times and himself never.

Osric did not attend. He was in the south by then with a house and a pension and a physician, and his lawyer read a statement of nine lines that admitted the levy, denied the forging, and asked the Count's mercy.

The forged letters were read out. They were dated the ninth year and they gave the whole of the valley east of the cut to the Count of Harven and they had the Vale seal on them and they were, Seraine thought, extremely good forgeries, and it took the Count's own clerk eleven minutes to say why they were not: the seal was right, and the hand was right, and the paper was nine years too new.

And then the Baron of Vale came in and answered for his barony for two hours and was, in that room, exactly what he was: a tired, honest, unremarkable lord who had held a small county for thirty years and had nothing to say about anything that had happened in it since the autumn.

That was the thing. Seraine watched it happen and could not stop it.

They asked Edric Vale about the raids. He said nine men had come to a farm and had been taken and released, and gave his reasons, and the reasons were good and the room accepted them.

They asked about the nineteen missing men on the Ashgrove road. He said he did not know what had happened to them, and that was true, and it was true in a way that sounded like a baron covering for the men who had done it, and the Count wrote in the margin.

They asked about the chieftain. He said the chieftain was gone. They asked where. He said nobody knew.

And on the fifth morning the Marshal of Varenthos rose and said a thing that Seraine had spent four months trying to prevent and had never once been able to write down.

"My lord Count, I've ridden in the east this spring. I've spoken with the Warden of the Ash, who has written forty pages on the subject, and with the Baron of Thornwall's factor, and with eleven men who were in the Veyr country in March." Orlan put both hands on the table. "There is a thing in the eastern marches that is not a levy and not a warlord and not a forged deed. It has removed nineteen men from an open road in daylight, it has emptied a camp of sixty men in eleven days without a wound, and it has taken a chieftain out of a tent with the lamp still burning. I have four witnesses to the road and two to the tent, and I have read the Warden's report, and I do not believe the Warden understands it either."

The room was quiet.

"I am not asking the Count to do anything about it," said the Marshal. "I am asking the Count to note that whatever is happening in the east is not a matter of a levy, and that in ten years it will not be a small thing, and that the crown has no man in that country and no means of putting one there."

The Count of Harven, who was sixty and tired of counties, wrote a line in the margin and said, "Noted," and the hearing went on to the matter of the reeve at Ashford and the bridge.

---

She got Casian Vale alone on the sixth day, in the cloister of the Count's hall, in the afternoon, and she had planned the conversation for four months and it lasted about a quarter of an hour.

"Master Vale."

"Lady Varyn."

"You've grown."

"Sixteen does that," he said. "I'm told it stops."

She looked at him. He was in a good coat and he had been at the back of the room for six days with his father and his elder brother and he had not been asked a single question, and he had stood up when the Count came in and sat down when the Count sat and had looked, throughout, like a fourth son.

"I'm going to say a great many things," she said, "and I'd like you to let me finish, and then I'd like you to answer one question."

"Go on."

"I know what happened at Lowmere. I know about the barn and the eleven. I know about the eleven gates on the cut and the mason who mends them and the fact that the Warden believes the cut is kept and nobody in that county will say by whom. I know that the Ashgrove road was closed in February by a complaint that was true and by a man who was paid, and that the drove was mended in the same month for eight marks by a carrying house that was nine weeks old." She said it all quietly and without any drama at all. "I know the corn contract. I know the millrace and the water right. I know that four hundred and twelve households in that county paid a levy and that nine months later not one of them can settle a boundary without asking at a mill."

"And?"

"And I have walked that road and counted the horses and taken four hours of testimony from a captain who has not slept since February, and I have written it down, and I have brought all of it to the one place in the world where it could be used, and it amounts to nothing." Seraine Varyn looked at him. "Nothing. The Count has noted the Marshal's remarks and moved on to a bridge. The Warden's report is in a box. Osric of Thornwall is in a small house in the south and Rennick is a factor with a clean book. You are the fourth son of a barony in good standing, and there is not a court in Varenthos that would hear me."

The cloister was very quiet.

"That's not a question," said Casian Vale.

"No." She folded her hands. "The question is this. On the day I came to that county I was looking for a man who was doing something. I have found him. And I've spent four months trying to work out whether he is the best thing that has ever happened to the east or the worst, and I have found nothing in the whole of it that lets me decide. So I'd like you to tell me what you want."

---

He did not answer at once, and she noticed that, and she noticed that when he did answer it was not clever.

"I want there to be a thing in that county that doesn't need me," he said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. A mill with water in it and a road that's mended by a book and eleven places where a man can stop, and a woman at a mill who can say no to a baron because she's got a book that says the same thing as his book."

"That's not what you've built."

"It's what I'm building. What I've built is a great many people who can't do a thing without me, and I know the number and I say it to myself most nights, and it's the worst thing about this year and I've not found the bottom of it."

"And the men who died?"

"Nine at Ashgrove in the lane, and five others, and a man called Aldous who was forty-six and was knifed in a lane beside my father's house because I was waiting for a count to be right." He said it without any change of voice and she believed him completely and it did not help. "I didn't kill any of them and they're all mine."

"Casian." She used his name for the first time. "I'm going to go on being in the east. My father will send me back in the autumn, because the crown wants a report every quarter now and there's nobody else who can write one that isn't a lie. And I'm going to write them, and I'm going to know it's about you, and I'm going to write it in a way that protects four thousand people I've met and liked and one boy I can't decide about."

"That's a good use of a report."

"That's what I've decided," said Seraine Varyn, "and I'd like you to know that I've decided it myself, and that nobody in this county has arranged it, and that when I'm sixty I'll still be able to say that."

---

She went out through the Count's yard and the Marshal was there with his horse.

"Well?"

"He's a boy of sixteen and he's honest within the exact limits he has set and I got nothing," said Seraine.

"I know. I was in the cloister."

She stopped.

"That was a private conversation."

"It was a public cloister with four arches and I was standing behind the third one, which is the one everybody uses," said Orlan, and he gathered his reins. "Lady Varyn. Your father told me to be careful of you, and I've watched you all week, and I'm going to tell you what I think, and then I'm going to go and write my own report, and the two of us will never speak of it again.

"You came here looking for a thing to be true or false. It isn't either. There's a lad in the east with a good hand for a business and a bad hand for a war, and in forty years there'll be a thousand people in those marches whose lives were decided by what he did in a wet winter when he was sixteen, and nobody will ever elect him to anything and nobody will ever be able to remove him, and that's a new thing in this realm and it will not stay in the east for long."

"And what am I to do about it?"

"Nothing," said the Marshal. "You're nineteen. Write it down and keep your own counsel and be in the room when it matters. That's what a person like you is for, and it's a great deal more than most of us get."

---

She wrote her quarterly report that night in Harven and put in it every fact she had and not one conclusion, and it was four pages and it was the most careful thing she had ever written, and it went south on the fourteenth of June.

And on the last page, in the small hand she used for the things she had decided, she wrote a line that she crossed out and then wrote again underneath, because she had learned in five months that a thing crossed out is a thing a man reads twice:

*The eastern marches are quiet and will remain quiet. There is no heresy in them, and there is no warlord, and I recommend that the crown leave them alone and that no man be sent to put a stop to anything.*

*I will not write another report that does not say that.*
