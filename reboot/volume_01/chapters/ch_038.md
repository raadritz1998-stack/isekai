The articles were eleven in number and they were read out at four places across the valley in the last week of June. The first reading was at the mill at Ashgrove on Midsummer Day, during the fair, because a county that will not gather at a church will always gather where there is beer and horse-trading.

Corin Ahl had written them. That was the reason they worked, and nobody in the crowd ever guessed it.

He had written them in the plain, blunt phrasing he used for a carrier's manifest. He had drafted four versions and burned three in his hearth, and the fourth draft had occupied six consecutive evenings at his kitchen table, with his wife Ivet reading each sentence aloud over her mending to test whether it sounded like words a carter would speak.

"There is a problem," she had said on the third evening, tapping the rough rag-paper with her bodkin.

"There are nine problems," Corin had replied, chewing on the end of a splintered quill.

"The worst one is the opening," she said. "*Be it known to all Christian men.* Nobody talks like that unless he has an archdeacon behind him or wants to steal a cow. You are writing for carters, weavers, and widows."

"How would you start it?"

"Say what the thing is," Ivet told him, "and then stop talking."

So the first article was set down:

**1. The mill at Ashgrove keeps a book. Any household within eleven miles may enter its name in the book for one silver penny a year. A name in the book is a debt to nobody.**

The second article provided that no tenant whose name stood in the book could be evicted from his holding upon the strength of any parchment he had not been permitted to hold and read.

The third provided that the road from the low fen to the upland cut was maintained by the mill's carting fund, and that any traveller robbed of goods upon that road would be indemnified from the mill's chest within a fortnight, provided the loss was vouched for by two neighbours; and it noted that this indemnity had already been paid twice during the spring and would be paid again.

The fourth provided that the millrace and the weir were held in the common name of the parish of Ashgrove, and not in the private name of any lord, gentleman, or factor.

The fifth established that any border dispute brought to the mill would be heard on the first Tuesday of each month, and that the miller would record in ink what both disputants asserted before either man was permitted to propose a remedy.

The sixth provided that the surgery at Vale was open to every household recorded in the book, that treatments would be charged according to the published table of herbs, and that the surgery's accounts would be read out from the church steps at Michaelmas and Easter.

The seventh provided that a boundary stone, once set by mutual agreement, could not be moved upon the authority of the mill's officers alone.

The eighth provided that no family's name could be struck from the book for poverty or arrears of dues, and that any arrears would be discharged by labour upon the ditches before any erasure was made.

The ninth set forth exactly what the mill collected for its pains: one penny a year from each registered household, eleven marks a year from the licensed carrying contracts of Corran and Son, and the ordinary miller's toll of grain.

The tenth article set forth what the mill would never do. This was Corin's own article, and the one Casian had debated with him for two hours by candlelight. It ran: **The mill will never carry a dispute to a magistrate, will never petition a lord for an order of distress, and will never require any Christian man or woman to swear an oath.**

And the eleventh article provided that these covenants could be altered, expanded, or struck out at the Midsummer fair by a common vote of the registered households, and that every proposed amendment would be recorded in the book alongside the names of the movers, whether it carried or failed.

---

Casian read the articles himself, standing upon a six-gallon beer barrel outside the mill-gate, and he read them deliberately without skill.

He had urged Corin to read them. Corin had refused with his usual flat stubbornness.

"They won't listen to a carter reading covenants," Corin had told him the day before. "They know what a carter earns. They will listen to a baron's son, and they will watch your eyes to see whether you look like you're playing a trick on them."

So at the tenth hour of the morning, while the roasting pig hissed over the charcoal pit and four hundred and eleven people crowded between the wool-stalls and the cattle-pens, Casian stood on the beer barrel in his plain grey coat.

His family stood in the crowd. Gareth, twenty-one years old and broad as an oak beam, stood by the horse-lines with his arms crossed over his chest; his hand rested near the pommel of his belt-knife, his soldier's eyes scanning the throng to make sure no drunken drover thought to throw an egg at a Vale boy. Elna, twelve years old, stood on an upturned wash-tub near the granary wall, eating a piece of spiced gingerbread with sugar on her fingers, her bright eyes fixed on her fourth brother.

Near the surgery cart, Nessa stood quietly in her clean linen apron, holding her satchel of herb-vouchers to hear Article Six. Across the leat, leaning against the damp masonry of the mill-tail, Vesna Orrow watched in silence, her dark upland wool cloak pinned with bone, holding herself apart as hill-people always did.

Casian's voice cracked on the third article, and Watt the innkeeper handed up a mug of small beer, which raised a shout of laughter from the weavers. On the fourth article, when Casian read the words *in the common name of the parish*, an elderly dyer at the back shouted, "Which parish?"

Casian lowered the sheet. "This one," he called out across the heads of the crowd, "and if you want to argue about the ditch-bounds, I have a stool here and I will stay until the moon comes up."

The crowd chuckled, settled, and listened. It took him fifteen minutes to reach the end of the parchment.

Then he laid the paper flat across his forearm and looked out at the faces of the people he had spent seven months guarding without their leave.

---

"Two things more," Casian said, "and then I will step down off this barrel.

"First: there are men in this valley who say that the Vale has built this ditch and this road to draw every man's trade into our own pocket. So hear this clearly: the mill belongs to Maeve Corran. The leat belongs to the parish. The ledger belongs to the desk in the mill-house, and any man who has paid his penny may demand to read every line in it. Maeve showed it to four farmers this week, and when a clerk from Harrowmere tried to look at it without paying his penny, she shut the wicket in his face. I have spent my own money this winter, and I have spent my father's money, and neither I nor my house has made a single silver groat from it, nor will we ever take one.

"Second: if anyone asks you at harvest-time what the fourth son of Baron Edric is for, I want you to be able to tell him the truth. This is what I am for: I am for a county where an honest family cannot be turned off their wheat-strip by a forged parchment they cannot read. I am for a road that stays passable in the black mud of February. And I am for a village where a mother with a coughing child does not have to weigh the price of a chirurgeon against the price of next winter's flour. That is all. There is nothing behind it, and there is no secret banner in my closet.

"And now I will tell you the part that is not comfortable. There is a woman from the hills who told me in March that when a man does things in silence, the world will invent a monster to explain him. She was right. So I will speak the truth before anyone else invents a lie.

"I am sixteen years old. I do not own this barony. My father holds it, and he is a wiser lord than I will ever be, and he has sat quietly on his horse at the edge of this meadow all morning letting his boy make a spectacle of himself on a barrel—which is a forbearance I would not show in his place.

"But we have four hundred and eleven households written in that book. We have eleven repaired crossings on the cut. We have a mill with water running through the gates. If you bring a disputed hedge to that table on the first Tuesday of July, Maeve and I will hear both sides, we will write down the boundary, and we will probably be right. Nobody in this meadow ever voted for me to do that.

"So take it if it serves you, and leave it if it does not. If a season comes when you decide you would rather have a lawyer from Harven and a bailiff with a whip, say so at this fair, and I will lay the ledger on this barrel, take my hat, and walk home to my garden. That is the eleventh article, and it is the only one that matters."

---

A silence fell over the fairground—the deep, sudden stillness of four hundred country people who had expected a sermon or a boast and had received an offer instead.

Then an old wood-turner near the front cleared his throat and called out, "Read that eighth article again, lad! The one about the arrears!"

A ripple of laughter went through the meadow. The tension broke like dry twig-wood.

Maeve Corran unlatched the wicket of the beer-tent, and within an hour her carters had tapped three barrels of October ale and sold a hundred and forty loaves of white barley bread.

Calla of Fenwick pushed her way through the throng, her woollen shawl tucked tight, and stood before Casian as he climbed down from the barrel.

"You said eleven miles," Calla said, her grey eyes narrowed.

"I did."

"The manor house at Fenwick is nine miles and two furlongs from that water-wheel."

"Then you are inside the boundary," Casian said.

"I am inside the boundary," said Calla, shaking her head. "I have managed seven hundred acres of fen for six years without asking a man's help, and I have just listened to myself being made a registered household in a miller's book for a copper penny. And the worst of it is that I am glad of it. That is what I will be turning over in my bed tonight, Master Vale."

---

The first Tuesday of July was the second day of the month, and nine disputes were brought to the timber table in the mill's outer yard.

Seraine Varyn rode nineteen miles from Harrowmere to sit on an oak bench at the back of the barn and watch the proceedings. She was not the only watcher: a notary's clerk from Harven sat on the mounting block with ink on his cuffs, and a quiet brother from Warden Selwyn's college observed everything from beneath his cowl without writing a word.

The hearings lasted from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon.

The first case was between a widow named Ayleth and a grazier named Hob. Ayleth testified that Hob had moved a thorn-hurdle four paces into her clover-patch. Hob swore by the saints that the hurdle had occupied that exact line since the year of the frost.

Maeve Corran dipped her quill, recorded what both parties asserted, and read the sentences back to them.

"Is that your testimony, Hob?" Maeve asked.

"Every word of it," the grazier grunted.

"Then tell me, Hob," Maeve said, leaning forward, "where were you standing on Thursday evening when you set the north stake in the ditch?"

"At the north corner, holding the mallet," Hob blurted, and then his jaw snapped shut as a collective gasp of laughter ran through the watching farmers.

It was over in ten minutes. Hob was assessed two marks for the trespass, the hurdle was moved back four paces before sunset, and the matter was concluded without an oath, without an attorney, and without a summoner's fee.

The fourth case involved a drainage dispute between two hamlets that had festered for nine years. Maeve referred it to a panel of three elderly freeholders with an instruction to walk the ditch on Friday.

The seventh case was brought by a woman in a dark travel-cloak who refused to speak before the room. Maeve cleared the barn, heard the woman alone for twenty minutes behind closed doors, and emerged with the matter resolved; the woman departed with her hood drawn, and Maeve entered a single line in the ciphered register at the back of the book.

The last case was a nine-year-old boy accused of stealing half a basket of green pippins from an orchard at the lane-end. The assembly watched with solemn amusement as Maeve found the theft proved, levied a fine of one copper penny upon the boy's father, and required the lad to surrender his whittling knife to the miller's boy until the apples were paid for.

---

After the yard had cleared, Seraine stood by the millrace with an earthenware cup of cider. Casian came out through the low timber arch and leaned against the stone coping beside her.

"You rode nineteen miles in the dust," Casian said.

"I rode nineteen miles to see whether this was a court of justice or a young man playing at lordship," Seraine replied.

"And what did you find?"

"I don't know yet," she said, looking into her cup. "Hob the grazier was caught by his own tongue in front of forty of his neighbours. The judgment was fair, but he will harbor a grudge against this mill for a decade. Yet in a single morning, nine disputes were settled that would otherwise have dragged through the bishop's consistory at Harven, costing each party five marks and two years of hatred. And the cloaked woman who would not speak in public—in an ordinary court she would have been turned away at the door."

"That is what it is for," Casian said quietly. "Nine small quarrels on a summer Tuesday, and a woman who can tell the truth in a room with the latch down."

"And when there are ninety quarrels?" Seraine asked, turning her sharp gaze upon him. "When there are eleven mills in eleven parishes, and a Tuesday tribunal in every bailiwick?"

Casian looked across the sunlit water of the millrace.

"Then it will belong to the parishes, not to me," he said. "I have written that covenant into the preface. I want to be believed, Lady Varyn, and I know why you should not believe me."

---

Edric Vale signed the eleven articles on the twenty-eighth of June, in his own solar, with a notary from Harrowmere present to affix his seal. It was Corin's insistence that the signing be public and witnessed, which Casian had resisted until Corin said: *A covenant signed in a closet is an ambush. A covenant signed before witnesses is a law.*

The baron read all eleven articles through twice, his rough forefinger tracing the ink across the vellum.

"Article ten," Edric said, pausing on the parchment. "*The mill will never send for a lord.*"

"That is Corin's phrasing," Casian said.

"It is the only line that the King's Chancellor will read with alarm in twenty years," Edric said. He looked up at his fourth son. "You understand what you have built here."

"I have put into writing what the people were already doing."

"You have built an independent jurisdiction," the baron said, his voice quiet and grave. "There is a mill at Ashgrove with a register and eleven covenants and a Tuesday sitting; and there is a manor house at Vale with a wall, an armsman's watch, and a baron's seal. In twenty years, the people of this valley will bring their ditch-rights to the mill because it costs a single penny and settles a feud by dinner-time; and they will come to the baron only when they require a timber-warrant or a highway cleared of bandits. The barony will be smaller, Casian. It will be cleaner, but it will be a diminished office. I want you to understand that I see that, and that I am setting my name to it anyway."

"Why?" Casian asked.

"Because in the year '09 I sat in the hall at Harrowmere while a bishop's proctor ruined an honest tenant over four yards of headland," Edric said, dipping his quill. "It took nine months, it cost forty pounds of silver, and when it was finished, both men were broken and the land went to thistles. And because your mother told me in the autumn that a father must recognize which of his sons understands the soil he stands on. I have made my choice, and I want to sign this before the county forces my hand."

He pressed his matrix into the warm red wax beside the willow emblem.

"One question," Edric added, leaning back in his chair. "Article one: a penny a year from each household. How many names will stand in that book by winter?"

"Four hundred and eleven today," Casian said. "Nine hundred by the Martinmas fair, if Corin's reckoning holds."

"And what will nine hundred names cost you?"

"Nine hundred copper pennies," said Casian, "and a very great number of Tuesdays."

---

The silent presence of Argius waited on the terrace wall when Casian walked the garden that night.

The old stone was cool beneath Casian's fingers, the night air filled with the scent of flowering privet and bruised mint. Casian did not wait for the question; he spoke the number into the silence.

"Thirty-four."

*And by the autumn?* the voice asked within his thought.

"Forty. Perhaps forty-one."

*Tell me the difference between forty and forty-one.*

"Forty-one is the number that matters," Casian said, looking out toward the dark line of the hills. "It is not a tally of men who owe me money. It is a tally of men who can no longer conclude an agreement without asking my leave. There is a terrible difference, and nobody in this valley sees it except me."

*Do you see it on the days when they cheer you?* Argius asked.

"I see it most clearly on those days," said Casian. "I stood on a beer barrel this morning and told four hundred people what I was for, and they cheered until their throats were dry, and a proud woman from Fenwick told me she was content to be a line in my book. It was intoxicating, Argius. And I came home, sat by the water-gate, and understood the danger. The true peril is not taking power by force. The true peril is being loved while you take it."

The garden lay motionless under the stars.

*Yes,* Argius said after a long interval. *That is the truth of it, Casian. You have discovered it at sixteen, and in fifty years it will still be the only truth worth remembering. Have you written it in the book?*

"No," said Casian. "The book is for accounts and ditch-bounds."

*Then write it upon your heart,* Argius whispered, *for there will come an evening in another realm, under a colder sky, when you will have need of that memory, and there will be no garden wall to remind you.*
