The articles were eleven in number and they were read out at four places in the last week of June, and the first reading was at the mill at Ashgrove on the twenty-fourth, at the fair, because a county that will not come to a church will always come to a fair.

Corin had written them. That was the thing that made them work and nobody afterward ever knew it.

He had written them the way he wrote a bill of lading, and he had done it in four drafts and thrown three away, and the fourth had taken him six evenings at his own table with Ivet reading each sentence aloud to see whether it sounded like a thing a man would say.

"There's a problem," she had said on the third evening.

"There's nine problems."

"The biggest one's the first sentence. *Let it be known.*" She put the paper down. "Nobody says that. You're writing to carters and widows."

"What would you have?"

"Say what it is and then shut up."

So the first article said:

**1. The mill at Ashgrove keeps a book. Any household within eleven miles may have its name in the book for a penny a year. A name in the book is a debt to nobody.**

The second said that a man whose name was in the book could not be put off land by a paper he had not seen.

The third said that the road from the fen to the cut was kept by the mill and that any man robbed on it would be made good out of the mill's own money within a fortnight, and that this had been paid twice and would be paid again.

The fourth said that the millrace was held in the name of the village and not in the name of any house.

The fifth said that a dispute brought to the mill would be heard on the first Tuesday of the month and that the miller would write down what both men said before either of them said a word about what should be done.

The sixth said that the surgery at Vale was open to any household in the book and that its accounts would be read out twice a year.

The seventh said that a boundary stone, once set, would not be moved by the mill's word alone.

The eighth said that no man's name would go out of the book for a debt, and that a debt would be settled before a name ever went.

The ninth said what the mill took: a penny a year from a household, eleven marks a year from a carting contract, and the mill's own trade.

The tenth said what the mill would not do, and this was Corin's article and the one Casian had argued against for two hours. It said: **the mill will not take a matter to a lawyer, and will not send for a lord, and will not ask any man to swear an oath.**

And the eleventh said that the articles could be changed at the fair in June by any household in the book, and that a change would be written down with the names of the men who had asked for it, whether it was carried or not.

---

Casian read them out himself, which was the whole point, and he read them badly.

He had wanted Corin to do it. Corin had refused in the flat way he refused things.

"They'll not hear a carter read a law," he said. "They'll hear you and they'll watch whether you're ashamed of it."

So on the twenty-fourth of June, at the fair, on a barrel, in front of four hundred and eleven people, the fourth son of the Baron of Vale read eleven articles out loud in a voice that went twice and had to be given water, and got a laugh on the fourth one because he read "in the name of the village" and a man at the back shouted, "Which village?" and he said, "This one, and if you'd like to argue about it I'll be here till dark."

He got through it in a quarter of an hour.

Then he said the thing he had promised Vesna Orrow in a mill in March.

---

"Two things and then I'll get off this barrel.

"First. There's people in this county who think I've been doing this in the dark and that there's something in it for a house at Vale. So: the mill's not mine. The race isn't mine. The book's not mine — it's the miller's, and she'll show it to any man who asks, and she's shown it to four this month and thrown one out. I've spent money this year and I've spent my father's money and I'll spend more and I've not made a mark out of any of it and I never will.

"Second. If you're asked in the autumn what the fourth son of Vale is for, I'd like you to be able to say a thing that's true. So this is what I'm for. I'm for a county where a man can't be put off his ground by a piece of paper he can't read, and where a road stays open in February, and where a woman with a sick child doesn't have to decide between a physician and a winter. That's all of it. There's no more.

"And I'll tell you the part that isn't comfortable, because there's a woman in the hills who told me in March that a thing which says nothing gets a story told about it, and she was right, so I'll say the story before anybody else tells it.

"I'm sixteen. I don't hold this county. My father holds it and he's a better lord than I'll ever be and he's sat at the back of this fair all morning letting me stand on a barrel, which is a thing I'd not do in his place.

"But I've got four hundred names in a book and eleven places on a road and a mill and a race, and I've had them since the spring, and if you come to me with a boundary in October I'll hear it and I'll write it down and I'll probably be right, and that's the whole of what's happened this year and it's not a thing anybody voted for.

"So: it's yours if you want it and it's mine if you don't. If a day comes when you'd rather have a baron and a lawyer, say so at the fair and I'll put the book on the barrel and walk home. That's the eleventh article and it's the only one that matters."

---

There was a silence of about four seconds, which is a long time at a fair, and then a man near the front said, "Read the fourth one again, I didn't hear it," and everybody laughed, and it was over.

Maeve Corran sold three barrels of beer and a hundred and forty loaves.

Calla of Fenwick stood at the back with her arms folded through the whole of it and afterward said, "You said *eleven miles*."

"I did."

"My barony's nine from that mill."

"Then you're inside it."

"I'm inside it," said Calla, "and I've been running a county for six years and I've just heard myself made a household in a book for a penny, and I find I don't mind, and that's the thing I'll be thinking about tonight."

---

---

The first Tuesday after the fair was the second of July and there were nine cases on it, and Seraine Varyn rode nineteen miles to sit at the back of it and see whether any of it was true.

She was not the only one. There was a lawyer's clerk from Harrowmere at the back with a notebook, and a man from the Warden's college who wrote nothing at all and looked at everything, and eleven people who had come with a boundary.

The cases took from the eighth hour to the fourth hour of the afternoon with a break at noon.

A widow called Ayleth said that a man had moved a hurdle four paces into her ground. The man said the hurdle had always been there. Maeve Corran wrote down what each of them said before either of them said what should be done, and read it back, and asked each of them whether it was right, and both said yes, and then she said, "Now, master, where were you standing when you moved it?"

And the man said, "At the north end," and the room made a sound, because he had said he had not moved it.

It took a quarter of an hour and it cost him two marks and the hurdle went back four paces and the room was satisfied, and the whole of it had been done without an oath, without a lawyer, and without a lord.

The fourth case was two villages about a ditch and it was nine years old and it was sent, that day, to the three oldest householders in the county with a note from the mill saying what had been tried.

The seventh case was a woman who had come nineteen miles on her own and would not say what she wanted in front of the room, and Maeve cleared it and heard her alone and afterward would not say a word about it to anybody, and the woman went out with her hood up and something had been settled.

The ninth case was a boy of nine accused of taking apples, and it was heard last because everybody wanted to see it, and the mill found it proved and fined him a penny and made him give the miller's boy his knife, and the whole room was delighted and the boy was furious and it was, Seraine thought, the most useful hour of the day.

---

Afterward she stood in the yard with a cup of something and Casian Vale came out and stood beside her.

"You came nineteen miles."

"I came to see whether it was a court or a man settling things."

"And?"

"I don't know yet," said Seraine. "The man with the hurdle said a thing he hadn't meant to say because a miller asked him a question in front of his neighbours, and it was fair, and he will hate that mill for eleven years." She turned the cup. "And in this morning nine cases have been settled that would have gone to a lawyer at Harrowmere and cost four marks each and taken a year. And the woman with the hood — she'd have gone home with nothing, and she hasn't."

"That's the whole of it," said Casian. "That's what I've built. Nine cases on a Tuesday and a woman who can say a thing in a room with the door shut."

"And when there are ninety cases, and eleven mills, and a Tuesday in every county?"

The boy was quiet for a moment.

"Then it's not my book," he said, "and I've written that down, and I'd like to be believed and I know that I shouldn't be."

---

Edric Vale signed the articles on the twenty-eighth with a clerk present and a copy going to Harrowmere, which was Corin's idea and Casian's argument against and Corin won by saying, "Then it's not a secret and a man who says it's a secret is a man who's got something to hide."

The baron read them first, all eleven, twice, and then sat with his hands on the paper for a while.

"Article ten," he said. "The mill will not send for a lord."

"That's Corin's."

"It's the only one that will be read in Harven in twenty years with any interest." Edric looked up. "You know what you've done."

"I've written down what happened."

"You've written down a court." The baron turned the page. "There's a mill at Ashgrove with a book and eleven articles and a Tuesday, and there's a baron at Vale with a wall and a levy and a steward, and in eleven years the men in these marches will bring their boundary to the mill because it's quicker and it doesn't cost them a mark, and the baron will be a man they go to when they want a road mended and a chapel roofed and a boy beaten for stealing apples. And that will be all right, because it will be a smaller office and it will be honest. But it is not the office I inherited, and I'd like you to understand that I know it, and that I'm signing anyway."

"Why?"

"Because in the year '09 I sat in a room at Harrowmere with a bishop's man and a farmer who had lost a ditch, and it took nine months and cost the farmer everything he had, and the bishop's man was right and the farmer was right, and there was no place in the world for either of them to go with it." Edric Vale dipped the pen. "And because your mother told me in the autumn that I'd have to decide which of my sons was going to be a lord, and I've decided, and I'd like to be able to say I did it before the county made me."

He signed.

"One thing," he said, without looking up. "Article one. A penny a year from a household. How many names by the winter?"

"Four hundred and eleven today," said Casian. "Nine hundred by the winter, if Corin's right."

"And what do nine hundred names cost you?"

"Nine hundred pennies," said Casian, "and a great deal of Tuesdays."

---

Argius was on the wall that night, and he was there in the way that he was there, and Casian said the number aloud before he was asked, which was the first time.

"Thirty-four."

*And by the winter?*

"Forty. Maybe forty-two."

*Say the difference.*

"Forty-one is the number," said Casian Vale, "and it isn't a list of people who owe me. It's a list of people who can't settle a thing without me, and there's a difference and nobody in this county can see it, including them."

*Including you?*

"Including me on the evenings when the fair goes well and a man shouts that the fourth article's the best thing he's heard in nine years." Casian looked out at the drove. "I stood on a barrel today and said what I was for and four hundred people cheered and a woman from Fenwick told me she didn't mind being in my book, and it was the best hour of my life, and I came home and I sat in the yard and I thought: that's the whole of the danger, isn't it. Not the taking. The being liked while you take."

Argius was quiet for a while.

*Yes,* he said. *That is the whole of it, and you have said it at sixteen, and in forty years it will still be the only thing worth saying. Write it in the book.*

"I'm not putting that in the book. The book's for money."

*Then write it somewhere,* said Argius, *because there will be a night in another county in another year when you will need to have written it down, and it will not be a night like this one.*
