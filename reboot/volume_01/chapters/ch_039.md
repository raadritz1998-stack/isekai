Maeve Corran brought him the list on the eleventh of September, in the tally-room behind the water-wheel at Ashgrove. She pulled the heavy oak door shut and dropped the iron latch into its keeper, an action she had never once taken in the entire year they had worked together.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down," said Maeve, setting a heavy stone ink-pot upon the desk, "because I have been compiling this since March, and I have kept it tucked in the parchment flap of the barley book for four months, and I want to be able to say afterward that I made you sit down before you read it."

Casian sat down on the three-legged stool by the window.

She laid four sheets of ribbed paper on the boards between them. They were ruled in her own firm hand, using a carpenter's straight-edge and a lead plummet. Down the left margin ran forty-one numbered entries, each followed by a line of small, dense script.

"What is it?" he asked.

"It is a register of everyone in the eastern marches who can no longer settle a dispute, buy seed, or mend a boundary without asking you first."

---

He read it.

It took a long time, because her script was the square, unhurried hand of a miller who measured grain by the peck, and because the names followed no order of rank, village, or wealth. Only when he reached the middle of the third folio did he realize that she had set them down in the chronological order of their dependence — the order in which they had become true.

*1. Corin Ahl — carries the carrying-firm's tally and decides what a road is for.*

*2. Ivet Ahl — assigns the winter byres at Hanging Rake; no family enters without her token.*

*3. Kael — reads a highway as a room; has twice settled a right of way upon his own word.*

*4. Watt — keeps the cart-toll register at the inn; every carrier's credit hangs on what Watt remembers.*

*5. Nessa — the surgery; seven households will not permit the physician from Harrowmere to touch an arm.*

*6. Maeve Corran — the book. (Written down because it is true, not because I enjoy it.)*

*7. Hollis Wray — the ford at the salt-house. Eleven wagoners stopped there this summer to ask him what the toll-agreements were.*

*8. Ayleth of Hanging Rake — nine households; will not arbitrate a fence-line without sending to the mill.*

*9. Edric Vale, Baron of Vale — asks his fourth son before he signs an eviction or a highway-grant, and does so openly, and has told me so to my face.*

*10. Maren Vale — country women carry to her kitchen the grievances they will not bring to an open court.*

*11. Elna Vale — twelve years old; settles the disputes of every child in the village and does so in the manner of the Tuesday court.*

*12. Brennan Vale — the fair and the horse-lines; will not let a carting contract pass without checking the desk.*

*13. Calla of Fenwick — holds two parcels of the eastern strip and has begun directing her own tenants to the Ashgrove Tuesday instead of her manorial hall.*

*14. Vesna Orrow — crossed through with three dark strokes of ink.*

Casian paused, his finger resting upon the black strokes that cancelled the upland woman's name.

"Why is Vesna struck out?"

"Because she is the only person on this parchment who has grown lighter," Maeve said. "She guided four families over the moor in March and lost an old man to the frost, and she has carried that death upon her own shoulders ever since. In June she stood between two grazing households at Hanging Rake, walked the ditch herself, settled the boundary, recorded it in her own memorandum, and told nobody at this mill for a fortnight. She does not need you, Casian. That is what the line through her name means."

---

He continued down the list.

There were eleven smallholders from the low fens; a woman named Marjory who kept forty hives on the Common; and a corn factor at Harrowmere called Darke, who had sold his spring contract to Corran and Son back in February and had been ruined and then re-established by the carrying-trade, so that he now refused to quote a price for barley without inquiring what the Ashgrove mill was paying.

There was the parish priest at Ashgrove, who had read a drainage notice from the church steps in May and had thereby become a man whose church notices carried legal weight.

There was the youth Wex, who had fled Thornwall Keep in April and was now copying ledgers in the back office of the mill for eleven marks a year, keeping his silence and speaking to no one except Maeve.

There was Garm, Hask Veyr's veteran captain, who had sent his resignation from Harven in April and had brought his four daughters east in June, asking Corin for carter's work; because an old soldier who has walked away from a lifetime of service needs to be given bread by men who do not ask him for stories about dead chieftains.

And at the very foot of the fourth page, set apart by an inked rule and standing in a line of its own:

*41. Casian Vale — decides.*

---

He sat looking at the last line for several minutes.

Below their feet, the massive water-wheel groaned against the current of the leat, sending a slow, rhythmic shudder through the floor-timbers. Outside the unglazed window, a carter shouted at his mule, an iron hoop rang against a cooper's anvil, and the smell of fresh flour dust mingled with the damp weed of the river.

"Say the number," Maeve said quietly.

"Forty-one."

"You have been saying thirty-four since the Midsummer fair."

"I was counting the people I saw on the road," Casian said, his voice flat. "You have counted the people who count."

"Do not treat that as a compliment," she warned.

"It is not a compliment," he said. "It is the darkest reckoning I have faced since Aldous died in the lane." He turned the sheets over, his gaze drifting across the blank backs of the leaves. "Maeve, how long have you been writing this?"

"Since the fourth of March," she said, leaning her apron against the desk. "I began it the morning after the chieftain's camp at Rye broke up. I sat at this window and thought: if our fourth son were thrown from his pony tomorrow and broke his neck, how many people in this valley would find their lives stopped? I went through the bailiwicks in my head, and I found forty who could not proceed without him. I hated the thought. So I began setting them down, adding a name whenever an agreement was made that led back to your garden, and crossing nobody off until Vesna."

"And why bring it to me today?"

"Because in August there were nine disputes brought to the Tuesday sitting, and on the first Tuesday of this month there were nineteen," Maeve said. "And because at the fair you stood on a beer barrel and told four hundred folk that the eleventh article was the only one that mattered—that you would lay the book down and walk home whenever they asked it. I want to know whether you were telling the truth, or whether you were simply being charming."

---

Casian did not answer immediately. When he spoke, it was in the way he had learned in the east: by doing something dull and institutional.

"The book must not remain in one person's hands," he said. "It goes to three keepers. You, and Wex, and a third who does not dwell at this mill—Calla's bailiff at Fenwick, or the priest, or Corin. It does not matter which, so long as any valid entry requires two signatures across the margin."

"That will make the accounting twice as slow," Maeve observed.

"It will be a slow accounting that outlives me," Casian said. "Second: the Tuesday tribunal goes to two arbiters. You and Vesna on alternate months. If a dispute involves more than one mark of silver, both of you must hear it. If you disagree, the question goes to three elderly freeholders of the parish, and I will not sit on the bench."

"And where will you be?"

"In my father's orchard, where I belong," he said. "Third: Corin is drafting a separate set of articles for the fen-dwellers, and Calla's ditch at Fenwick is being cut next month under her own name, with her own parish fund. Fourth: Nessa has already taken a second apprentice into the surgery. She chose the girl herself, arranged the wage from her own box, and told me about it after the indenture was sealed. And fifth—"

"Fifth," Maeve prompted.

"Fifth: this list must be transcribed into the back of the barley register with today's date. I want it read aloud at the Midsummer fair every June, immediately following the eleven articles. I want the reader to state plainly what it is: a tally of every soul in the eastern valley whose daily livelihood depends upon this mill. Let it be read whether the number is forty-one, or eleven, or ninety. And if the number reaches ninety, I want the crowd to hear it, because a community that grows accustomed to ninety masters has forgotten what it means to be free."

---

Maeve sat back on her bench, studying him with her grey, unsparing eyes.

"You have been turning this over in your head for weeks."

"I have turned it over every night since Midsummer," Casian said. "I stood on that barrel and four hundred people shouted their approval, and when I walked home in the dark I felt sick with the ease of it. I had six months of work that went precisely as I intended, and the danger of that success frightened me more than Hask's hundred spears ever did."

"That is an honest confession," she said.

"It is a confession that is easy to speak and hard to live by," said Casian. "Hand me the pen."

She pushed the barley ledger across the oak boards, then stopped and turned it around so the spine lay to his left. "Write it on the leaf before the names. The back is reserved for the list itself."

---

Casian dipped the goose quill and wrote in the square, legible book-hand he had practiced with Corin, setting down each word without ornament:

*The eleventh day of September, 1217.*

*Maeve Corran has drawn up a roll of the persons within these marches who cannot conclude their affairs without recourse to the Ashgrove mill. The roll contains 41 names. One of those names is mine; one is my father's.*

*I have passed a fortunate year. My calculations have proved sound, our borders are quiet, and many households have bread who would otherwise have faced ruin. There is nothing in those achievements that I regret.*

*Yet I have created a county that cannot endure without my presence, which is a very different thing from a county that has learned to govern itself. That failure is mine, and it is the only thing of which I am ashamed.*

*Therefore: three custodians for the book; two arbiters for the Tuesday court; an independent leat at Fenwick; a second practitioner at the surgery; and this roll read aloud at the Midsummer assembly each year, whatever the count may be.*

*If I walk these fields in ten years and the number remains forty-one, then I have merely replaced an old tyranny with an agreeable one, and I invite any honest man to tell me so to my face.*

*Casian Vale.*

---

Maeve read the leaf twice through, blowing the wet ink dry.

"That last sentence," she said. "A list that stays at forty-one is not a crime, Casian. People are weak, seasons are hard, and there will always be neighbours who cannot agree upon a ditch without an arbiter."

"Then let them depend upon a covenant and an open court," Casian replied, "not upon a boy in a garden."

"They will always look for a man," Maeve said with the blunt wisdom of forty years. "If it is not you, it will be a baron with three bailiffs and a troop of horse. You have read the old chronicles. You know what happens to a border shire when the peacemaker dies and leaves no successor."

"Then we had better ensure there are three peacemakers who distrust each other," Casian said, standing up from the stool.

---

On the thirteenth of September, he laid the four sheets before Corin and Calla in the same tally-room.

Corin stood throughout the reading, his woollen cap held in his calloused hands, listening without a single interruption, contrary to his habit. Calla sat with her arms folded inside her mantle, her dark eyebrows drawn tight.

When Casian finished reading the forty-one lines, Corin pointed with his thumb at the paper.

"Read the entry for the miller again," Corin said.

"*Maeve Corran — the book. Written down because it is true, not because I enjoy it.*"

"That is the only line on the paper that tells the unvarnished truth," Corin said, turning his cap. "The other forty lines are about your cleverness. That line is about the law. Master Casian, I have carried your manifests since December. I have entered hundreds of names in that register, and I have never once had to decide an issue that could not be proved by a voucher. That is the division between us: you decide the policy, I record the result, and then we all pretend the register made the choice."

Calla set her cider-mug down on the timber table with a sharp tap.

"Read the third entry," she commanded.

"*Kael — reads a highway as a room; has twice settled a right of way upon his own word.*"

"There is your remedy," Calla said, looking straight into Casian's face. "You have a rider who has settled two boundaries by his own good sense, and you have entered him in a list as a burden to be pitied. Put Kael on the Tuesday bench! Give him the northern bailiwick for a month, let him make an error before his neighbours, and go sit at the back of the barn with your mouth shut. You have spent six months trembling over forty-one names, Casian, when in truth you have eleven men and women who are fully capable of carrying the load, and thirty more who would gladly help if you stopped treating them like children."

---

That evening, Casian carried the parchment to the surgery cottage at Ashgrove. Nessa read it by the light of her oil-lamp while the scent of boiling elderberry syrup filled the small room.

When she reached the fifth line, she tapped the vellum with a lancet.

"This is inaccurate," she said.

"Seven households refused the Harrowmere physician," Casian said.

"They refused him because he demands four silver shillings to splint a thumb, and because he is sixty years old and hasn't washed his lancet since the coronation," Nessa said briskly. "If Maeve had written *seven families prefer clean linen and fair prices*, it would have been the truth." She turned the folio. "And you may draw a line through my name."

"Why?"

"Because Sarah, the weaver's daughter from Rye, has been my sworn assistant since the fourth of September," Nessa told him. "She sets a wrist more smoothly than I do, and I have taught her every salve in Aldous's chest. She takes the patients on Thursdays, I take them on Tuesdays, and the day-book is open to the parish wardens. I chose her myself, I pay her four marks a year out of the medicine-box, and I informed you of the arrangement after it was finished."

"I am glad of it," Casian said.

"Then do not write my name in your catalogue of sins," Nessa said firmly, sliding the sheet toward him. "Write it on the front leaf, beside the herb-tallies: *The surgery has two leeches.* That is an asset, Casian, not an obligation."

Casian looked at her clear, determined face, and wrote the entry where she directed.

---

He walked the long road back to Vale manor in the gathering dusk, a habit he had neglected since midsummer. The evening was mild and dry, the white dust of the highway soft under his boots. Harvesters were returning from the oat-fields with their scythes over their shoulders; three of them touched their caps to him, and an elderly ditcher asked whether the sheep-wash at Fenwick would be cleared before Michaelmas.

"Tuesday at the mill," Casian answered with a smile. "Bring the fen-warden with you."

When he reached the lane below the orchard, he paused by the dry-stone wall and looked back across the darkening valley.

*Forty-one,* the presence spoke in the silence of his thoughts.

"Forty-one," Casian replied aloud.

*Tell me what the list means.*

"It means I have built an empire of debts and disguised it as benevolence," Casian said. "I spent the summer believing I was merely helping my neighbours. That was the flattering version, Argius, and I have discarded it."

*And what will you do with the harsh one?*

"Keep it in the front of my mind," Casian said. "Carry it into every court, read it at every fair, and strive with all my strength to make that list shrink to ten."

He pushed through the timber gate and entered the manor kitchen.

The room was warm, filled with the comforting smell of roasted mutton and woodsmoke. Maren was setting a steaming bowl of broth and crusty barley bread upon the scrubbed table. Edric sat near the hearth, carefully oiling a bridle-buckle with neat's-foot grease.

On the wooden settle by the chimney, Elna was swinging her legs, her ribbons untied, chattering happily to her father about her day in the village.

"And then, Father," Elna was saying, "Moll said that Wat had stolen her wool-card, but Wat said he found it in the ditch! So I made them both sit on the bench, and I wrote down what they both said with a piece of chalk, and then I asked Wat who had greased the leather! And he turned quite red and gave it back!"

Edric chuckled into his grey beard, glancing up as Casian closed the door.

"You're late, lad," the baron said, his voice warm and easy. "Your mother has had that broth simmering for an hour."

"The carting accounts took longer than I expected," Casian said, hanging his coat on the peg.

Maren pushed the bowl toward him, her hand resting briefly upon his shoulder. "Eat your supper, Casian," she said softly. "You have spent the whole summer carrying the weight of forty men, and you look like you haven't tasted bread since Monday."

Casian sat down between his father and his sister, took up his wooden spoon, and ate.
