Maeve Corran brought him the list on the eleventh of September, in the tally-room at the mill, with the door shut, which she had never once done in a year.

"Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Sit down," said Maeve, "because I've been making this since March and I've had it in the back of the barley book for four months and I'd like to be able to say afterward that I made you sit down."

Casian sat down.

She put four sheets of paper on the table. They were ruled in her own hand, and the ruling was done with a straight-edge and a pencil, and there were forty-one lines on them.

"What is it?"

"It's everyone in the eastern marches who cannot settle a thing without you."

---

He read it.

It took a while, because it was in her writing and her writing was not a clerk's, and because the names were not in order of anything he could work out until he got to the bottom and understood that they were in the order she had written them, which was the order in which they had become true.

*Corin Ahl — carries the tally and decides what a road is for.*

*Ivet Ahl — decides who has the byre at Hanging Rake.*

*Kael — reads a road as a room; has twice settled a right of way on his own word.*

*Watt — keeps the cart tally; a man's trade depends on what Watt remembers.*

*Nessa Vale — the surgery; four households will not go to the physician at Harrowmere.*

*Maeve Corran — the book. (Written down because it is true, not because I like it.)*

*Hollis Wray — the ford. Eleven parties stopped there this summer and asked him what the arrangements were.*

*Ayleth of Hanging Rake — nine households; will not hear a case without asking the mill.*

*Edric Vale, Baron of Vale — asks his fourth son before he signs a thing, and does it openly, and has told me so.*

*Maren Vale — women come to her with the things they will not bring to a mill.*

*Elna Vale — twelve years old; settles the disputes of every child in the village and does it in the manner of the Tuesday.*

*Brennan Vale — the fair and the road; will not let a carting contract go without asking.*

*Calla of Fenwick — holds two parcels of the strip and has begun sending men to the Tuesday instead of to her own hall.*

*Vesna Orrow — crossed through.*

He stopped at that one.

"Why is that crossed through?"

"Because she's the only one on the list who's got lighter," said Maeve. "She moved four families in March and lost one and she's been up and down that hill ever since, and in June she took a boundary at Hanging Rake between two households and settled it herself and wrote it in the book and told nobody for a fortnight. She doesn't need you. That's what the line through it means."

---

He read the rest.

There were eleven smallholders on it, and a woman who kept bees, and a corn factor at Harrowmere called Darke who had sold his contract to Corran and Son in February and had been ruined and remade by it and now would not sell grain to a man without asking what the mill's price was; and there was a smith at Ashford and the priest at Ashgrove — who had read out a notice from a church door and had thereby, in Corin's phrase, become a man whose notice got read.

There was the boy Wex, who had left Thornwall in May and was now copying ledgers at the mill for eleven marks a year and had told nobody but Maeve what he had done.

There was Garm, the chieftain's old captain, who had written four lines from Harven in April and had come east in June with his daughters and had asked, of all people, Corin, whether there was work — because a man who has resigned from a thing he did not resign from needs to be given a thing to do by somebody who has not heard the story.

And at the bottom, in a line of its own, in the same hand as the rest and not crossed out:

*Casian Vale — decides.*

---

He sat with it for a long time.

The mill worked under them. Somebody was rolling a barrel across the yard and somebody was arguing about the price of a sieve, and a cart went out through the gate and the gate was left open the way it had been since March.

"Say the number," said Maeve.

"Forty-one."

"You've been saying thirty-four since June."

"I've been counting the people I know about," said Casian Vale. "You've counted the people who count."

"That's not a compliment and I'd like it understood."

"It's not a compliment. It's the worst morning I've had since Aldous." He turned the sheets over and looked at the back of them, which was blank. "Maeve. How long have you had this?"

"Since March the fourth. I started it the morning after the chieftain was taken, because I sat in this room and thought: if a man has to be able to do a thing, and I went through the county in my head, there's forty of them who'd have to be told, and I didn't like it, and I wrote it down, and I've been adding to it every week since and crossing nobody out."

"And why now?"

"Because in August there were nine cases at the Tuesday and in September there's been nineteen, and because at the fair you told four hundred people that the eleventh article was the one that mattered and you'd put the book on a barrel and walk home." Maeve put both hands flat on the table. "I'd like to know whether you meant it."

---

He did not answer that for a while, and then he answered it in the way he had learned to answer things in the east, which was by doing something dull.

"The book goes to three people," he said. "Not one. You, and Wex, and a third who isn't at this mill — Calla's steward at Fenwick, or the priest, or Corin, I don't care, but it goes to three and all three can sign a page."

"That'll be a slow book."

"It'll be a slow book that works when I'm dead."

"Second," he said, "the Tuesday goes to two. You and Vesna on alternate months, and if a case is worth a mark it goes to both and if they disagree it goes to the county's three oldest householders and I'll not be one of them."

"And what will you be doing?"

"Writing down what I think and getting overruled." He put the sheets down. "Third. Corin's writing a second set of articles for Fenwick, and the millrace at Fenwick is being dug in October, and it'll be Calla's race and Calla's book and I'll not be in it. Fourth. Nessa's surgery gets a second person trained in the winter and it's already decided and she chose the person and told me rather than asking. Fifth—"

"Fifth."

"Fifth, I want this list in the back of the barley book with the date on it, and I want it read out at the June fair every year along with the articles, and I want the man reading it to say what it is: the number of people in this county who cannot do a thing without the mill. And I want it read out whether it's forty-one or eleven or ninety, and if it's ninety I'd like whoever's reading it to say so, because a man who's got used to ninety is a man who's stopped minding."

---

Maeve Corran sat back.

"You've been thinking about this since June."

"I've been thinking about it since the twenty-fourth of March," said Casian, "when I stood on a barrel and four hundred people cheered, and I went home and couldn't sleep, and I worked out the whole of it that night and then did nothing about it for three months because I was enjoying myself."

"That's honest."

"It's a true thing that's easier to say than to do, and I'd like it written down somewhere that I said it before anybody made me."

"Then write it," said Maeve, and pushed the barley book across the table at him, and then took it back and turned it round, because a man writing in a book with the spine toward him writes crooked. "Not there. The back's for the list. There's a page before it."

---

He wrote on the page before the list, in his own hand, in the flat unhurried letters that Corin had taught him to make the winter before, and he wrote for some while and did not let her read it until it was done.

*The eleventh of September, 1217.*

*Maeve Corran has made a list of the people in this county who cannot settle a thing without the mill, and it is 41 names, and one of them is mine, and one of them is my father's.*

*I have had a good year. I have been clever and it has worked and I have not been frightened for one hour of it. The men who came at us are gone and the county is quiet and a great many people are better off than they were in the spring, and there is not one of those things I would take back.*

*And I have made a county that cannot lose me, which is not the same as one that does not need me, and the difference is the whole of what I am ashamed of.*

*So: three keepers of the book, two hearers of a case, a race at Fenwick that is not mine, a second at the surgery, and the list read out at the fair with the number on it whatever the number is.*

*If I am still here in eleven years and the list is still forty-one, then I have failed and I would like somebody to say so to my face.*

*Casian Vale.*

---

Maeve read it twice and then sat with it.

"That last part," she said. "The list staying at forty-one isn't failing. There'll be people who need a thing done and there's no sense in pretending otherwise."

"Then they'll need a mill and a Tuesday and a woman who can say no."

"They'll need a person," said Maeve Corran, "and it'll be a person, and if it's not you it'll be somebody worse, and you know that as well as I do, because you've read the same books I have and you've seen what a county does when the man who settles things dies and nobody's thought about the day after."

Casian looked at her.

"Then I'd like it to be three people who don't like each other," he said.

---

---

---

He read the list out to Corin and Calla in the tally-room on the thirteenth, because it was Corin's book and Calla's parcels and it was not a thing he was going to have in a drawer.

Corin heard it standing, with his hat in his hands, and did not interrupt once, which was not usual.

Calla heard it sitting with her arms folded.

When he got to the end of the forty-one, Corin said, "Read the one about the miller again."

"Maeve Corran — the book. Written down because it is true, not because I like it."

"That's the only honest line on it," said Corin Ahl. "The rest of them are about you. That one's about a book."

"And the eleventh one?"

"*Casian Vale — decides.*" Corin turned his hat round. "Master, I've carried that tally since March and I've written nine hundred names in a book and I've not once had to decide a thing I couldn't write down. That's the difference between us and it isn't to my credit. You decide and I write what you decided and then we all agree it was the book that did it."

Calla of Fenwick put her cup down.

"Read the third line again."

"*Kael — reads a road as a room; has twice settled a right of way on his own word.*"

"There's your answer," said Calla. "There's a man on that list who's done it twice on his own word and you've written him down as a thing to be managed. Put him in the Tuesday. Put him on a month and let him be wrong in public and go and sit at the back and don't say anything." She looked at Casian. "You've spent six months being afraid of a list of forty-one and it's not a list of forty-one. It's eleven people who could do the work and thirty who'd be glad to be asked."

---

He took the list to Nessa that evening and she read it standing up, and when she got to her own name she said, "That's wrong."

"It's not wrong. Four households won't go to the physician at Harrowmere."

"They won't go to the physician at Harrowmere because he's expensive and he's sixty and he's a fool about a broken arm," said Nessa, "and if Maeve had put *four households prefer a woman who washes her hands* it would be nearer the truth." She read on. "And put a line through mine."

"Why?"

"Because there's a second person in that surgery since the fourth of September and she's better than me at a chest and I've told her so, and she takes the Thursday and I take the Monday, and if a household won't have her they can have me and it's the same book." Nessa sat down. "I'd like the record to show that I chose her myself and told you rather than asking you."

"Noted."

"Not noted, Casian. Written down." She pushed the paper across. "On the front, with the date, in the book. *The surgery has two people in it.* Not in the back with a list of things you're ashamed of. On the front with the money."

He looked at her for a moment, and then did as he was told.

---

He walked home along the road in the evening, which was something he did not often do any more, and the road was full of people going the other way, and three of them said good evening to him and one of them asked him a question about a boundary at the top of the drove and he said, "Tuesday at the mill, and bring the man you're quarrelling with."

And then he stopped in the road, about a mile from the house, and said the number aloud, and Argius was there.

*Forty-one.*

"Forty-one."

*Say what it is.*

"It's a list of people who count," said Casian Vale, "and I've spent the summer telling myself it was a list of people who owe me, and it isn't, and that was the comfortable version and I've put it away."

*And what will you do with the uncomfortable one?*

"Carry it in the back of a book and have it read out at a fair every June," said Casian, "and be a man with forty-one names who is trying very hard to be a man with eleven."

The evening was warm and the road was white and the light was going.

*Good,* said Argius. *Now go home and eat. You have not eaten properly since the fair and your mother has noticed and she has not said anything, which is worse.*
