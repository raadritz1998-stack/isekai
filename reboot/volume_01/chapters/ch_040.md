The walnut went in on the eighteenth of October, in the corner of the walled garden where the wall had been mended in March, and it took four of them to do it.

The tree had come from a nursery at Fenwick in a barrel, and it was nine feet and had cost eleven marks, and the nurseryman had said it would not take, because a walnut moved at nine feet almost never does, and Casian had paid him the eleven marks and said he would take his chances.

Gareth dug the hole and complained about it. Brennan carried the water. Elna held the tree upright while the first of the soil went in and then refused to let go for a quarter of an hour, on the grounds that a tree which has just been planted needs somebody to be there.

"Your arms will drop off."

"They haven't."

"Elna."

"*You* did this to me," said Elna Vale. "I was in a millrace in February and now everything's a thing that has to be stood next to."

Maren came out at noon with bread and beer, and stood with her hands on her hips and looked at the corner of the garden, and the wall, and the four of them with mud on their boots.

"It'll be nothing but leaves in ten years," she said.

"It's a walnut, Mam. It's nuts."

"In ten years there'll be a tree, and a great deal of shade, and boys in it, and you'll have to say something to me about it." Maren went back to the house and then stopped at the door. "Casian. Your father wants you in the hall at two, and it's about the road at Ashford, and it'll take an hour, and then I want you in the kitchen at three and it will take the rest of the afternoon, and I'd like you to be there and not to be thinking about anything."

"What's at three?"

"Your father's birthday was in the summer and we could not do anything about it at the time, on account of we were all in a mill being fed, and I have decided that we're doing it today and that it will be a family dinner with no excuses and nobody talking about counties."

---

He was in the hall at two and it took an hour and it was dull, and this was the thing he had wanted most in the year and could not have said out loud: for the last four months the work had been dull.

The road at Ashford needed mending. The county had been assessed for it in the spring and the assessment had been wrong, and it had been wrong in a way that had a man's name on it — a surveyor at Harrowmere who had put four hundred and ten loads where there should have been two hundred and sixty — and the Baron of Vale had found it because his steward had found it, and the steward had found it because the mill's book had a carrier's tally for that road going back nineteen years.

So there was a comparison. Nine hours of a man's reading against the note in a miller's book, and the note in a miller's book was right, and the surveyor was wrong, and the surveyor was a man who had done it for thirty years and was not dishonest and had simply walked a road in the rain in March.

"It's not a complaint," said Edric. "I'll not have a man of thirty years' standing complained of over a mis-count. But the county's to pay forty marks for a wrong number and I'd like the number corrected and the man told privately."

"Then write to him privately," said Casian, "and have the letter carried by Watt's boy and not by a clerk, and have it say what you'd say to him in his own hall."

"That's what I thought. Sit down and write it and I'll sign it."

So he sat in his father's hall for an hour in the afternoon of an October day and wrote a careful, ordinary letter about a wrong number on a road, and it was the most peaceful hour he had spent since the spring.

---

At three there was a dinner, and it was the loudest that house had been since the funeral in November.

Edric was forty-nine and had said he did not want anything done and then had, at half past three, begun telling the story of the drainage dispute at Harrowmere in the year '09, with improvements. Oswin, who was seventy-one and had heard it eleven times, closed his eyes at one point and was still awake for the last part.

Gareth argued about the wall being mended in the wrong order at the east end. Brennan had brought a barrel of something from the fair and would not say what it was, and it turned out to be extremely good and then extremely bad in the morning. Nessa came late with her book under her arm and was made to put it under the table, and did, and told everybody afterward that she had done it under protest.

Emric was nearly two and had been told he was too young for a birthday dinner and had come anyway, and spent the meal between his mother and his elder brother, and at the end of it fell asleep against Gareth, who did not move for a quarter of an hour and told anybody who looked at him that it was because the boy would wake up otherwise.

Elna was allowed a cup of the barrel and was very funny for eleven minutes and then silent, and then wanted to know what everyone was going to do next year and would not accept "the same" from anybody.

---

---

At about seven, when the table had gone quiet and Emric was asleep on Gareth, Elna asked the question she had been saving.

"What happened to the nine?"

"The nine?"

"The nine at Bradey's. The ones who came in February." She was winding wool again and doing it badly and the wool was a pretext and everybody at that table knew it. "They went home and nobody hanged them and that was the right thing and I've said so. But what happened to them after?"

The table was quiet.

"Four of them are working," said Casian. "Two on the fen road and two on Corin's carts. One went to Harrowmere and I don't know. One took his family over the cut in May and I've not heard since."

"And the others?"

"Two are dead," said Casian. "One in a fight in a beer-house at Harrowmere in June, and one of a fever in August, and I heard about both from Watt, who keeps a tally of everything."

Elna wound wool.

"So they came, and they were fed, and they went home, and then the world happened to them anyway."

"Yes."

"But not to the ones we kept."

"No," said Casian Vale. "And I've thought about that and I don't have a good answer, and the honest version is that I gave nine men a story and a road home and two of them were dead by August anyway, and the four who stayed are the four who were given something to do that needed doing."

"That's not fair."

"No."

"Then what's the point of it?"

And Casian looked at his sister, who was twelve and had been behind a stone in a millrace in February, and said, "The point is that the thing that killed them was a fight in a beer-house and a fever in August, and those are the ordinary things, and no county has ever beaten the ordinary things. What I can do is make sure that a man isn't killed by a baron's levy or a chieftain's road or a paper he can't read. The rest of it isn't mine."

Elna thought about that for a while.

"That's a good answer," she said, "and I don't like it."

"Write it down," said Casian, "and read it to me in six years."

Gareth, who had not said anything for ten minutes, said, "She'll be eighteen in six years and she'll be running this county and you'll be the one winding the wool."

Elna threw a ball of it at him and he caught it one-handed because he was holding a sleeping child with the other, and Edric Vale laughed until he had to wipe his eyes, and Maren told them all to be quiet, and the evening went on being ordinary and it was, Casian thought, the best evening of the year.

---

He got away at about eight, and went out to the garden, and the evening was cold and clear, and the walnut stood in its corner with mud around it and a hurdle against it to keep the wind off.

Argius was there.

He was there in the way he was there, which was not a glow and not a shape and not the old world either; Argius looked, in the darkness of a walled garden in a small county, exactly like a man standing at the end of a wall.

*Say the number.*

"Forty-one."

*And the tree.*

"It'll die."

*It might not.*

"It's a walnut and it's nine feet and the man said it wouldn't take," said Casian, "and I paid eleven marks for it because the man said it wouldn't take, and I'll not pretend that was about the tree."

*What was it about?*

"A thing that takes forty years to be worth anything," said Casian Vale, "in a county where everything I've done this year has taken about a fortnight."

Argius was quiet for a while. Down past the wall there was a light in the mill window and a cart going out through the gate and a man calling goodnight to somebody at the ford.

*I have been with you since a bench in March,* said Argius, *and I have been difficult and I have been silent and I have told you forty times that you are dangerous, and I would like to say one more thing before the year turns, and then I will go back to being quiet.*

"Say it."

*There are four things in this county that were not here last year and that will still be here when you are dead. A millrace. A road with a book behind it. A surgery with two people in it. And forty-one names that are a list of the people who count.*

*Everything else you have done this year has been a thing you did. Those four are a thing this county does now, without asking you, and I want you to notice that you got there by being personable at people and by putting your hand on a thing you did not understand and counting on being right, and that it will not work twice.*

"I know."

*You do not know. You are sixteen and you have not been wrong yet about a thing that mattered.* Argius looked out at the wall. *I said in the spring that you would be here for all of it. I have never once been wrong about that part.*

Casian stood at the corner of a walled garden.

"What happens now?"

*The year turns,* said Argius. *And then the world comes in through the gate.*

---

---

He was out in the garden at first light and there were three buds on the scion.

The tree was nine feet and had been in the ground thirteen days and the buds were the size of a fingernail and green, and there was not a thing wrong with them except that it was the eighteenth day of October and a walnut does not do that.

Casian Vale stood in a walled garden in a small county and looked at a tree for a long while, and what he felt was not pleasure and it was not fear; it was the feeling he had had on a bench in March of the year before, when he had understood that a thing had begun and that he was going to be inside it for the whole of it.

Elna came out with her hair unbrushed.

"Mam says there's buds."

"There's buds."

"Can I see?"

"Come and look."

She looked for a while, and then said, "Is that bad?"

"No."

"Is it good?"

"I don't know," said Casian. "It's the wrong season and the tree's healthy and it'll come to nothing and there'll be a hard frost in a fortnight and we'll see."

Elna considered that.

"You're doing the thing you do."

"What thing?"

"You've said a lot of words that were all true and now you're going to go inside and not say the rest." She folded her arms. "Mam said I was to ask you what happens next year and you were to tell me something."

Casian looked at his sister.

"Next year the crown sends a man to look at us, and the Fair comes round again and there'll be ninety names in the book and not forty-one, and the Warden dies or he doesn't, and Nessa trains a third person, and Corin digs a race at Fenwick that's not mine." He put his hands in his pockets. "And you'll be thirteen, and you'll settle a thing between two households in this village and somebody will say, 'that's the mill's way of it,' and mean you."

"That's not an answer, that's a list."

"It's the same thing," said Casian. "Ask me again in June."

---

At mid-morning a rider came up the Ashgrove road, which happened about once a fortnight now, and this one was a clerk of the Marshal's and had come from Harven in nine days and had two letters.

The first was for the baron and was a courteous note about the road at Ashford and the surveyor's count, which pleased Edric enormously because it meant the crown had read his letter.

The second was for Casian and was from Seraine Varyn, and it was four lines long, and it said:

*Master Vale. The Marshal has written his account of the east and I have read it and it is fair and it is not the whole of it. In the spring I am to be in these marches for a season with a commission and a clerk of my own, and I will be asking questions in your county whether you like it or not.*

*I am writing so that you can be ready and not surprised, and so that you know I have told you first.*

*— S. V.*

He read it twice and then stood in the yard with it in his hand and did a thing he had not done in a year, which was to be pleased about something that was going to be difficult.

"Well?" said Elna, from the door.

"The world's noticed the garden," said Casian.

"Is that good?"

"Yes," he said. "That's the part I've been waiting for."

---

The gate.

He noticed it on the way back, in the dark, because the wall gate had been left open since March and nobody had shut it, and there was a bad line in his head that he had written on a barrel at the fair in June: *it's yours if you want it, and it's mine if you don't.*

He stood in the yard and looked at the open gate for a moment, and then went in past it.

In the kitchen his mother was sitting with her sleeves tied back and a pot of something on the table, and she looked up when he came in and said, "Sit down and eat that. You've had a cup of Brennan's and nothing else since two and you'll be sorry at four in the morning."

He sat down and ate it.

"Elna wants to know what's happening next year," said Maren Vale.

"So did I. I couldn't answer."

"You'll tell her in the morning, then, and you'll tell her something." His mother picked up her mending. "And Casian. That tree in the corner."

"Yes."

"It'll take. I've looked at it. There's three buds on the scion and it's October and there shouldn't be."

Casian put his spoon down.

"That's not possible."

"There's three buds on it," said Maren Vale, "and I've been gardening since before you could talk, and I know how many buds a thing ought to have in October, and I want you to go out in the morning and look at it and then come in and not make a fuss and not tell your father that there's anything unusual about a walnut. He's had a long year."

She bit the thread.

"And then," she said, "have a look at the gate. Somebody's shut it."
