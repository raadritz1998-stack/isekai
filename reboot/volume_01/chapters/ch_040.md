The walnut tree went into the earth on the eighteenth of October, in the sheltered corner of the walled garden where the stone had been rebuilt after the spring wet. It required four of them to do the work.

The sapling had come from an upland nursery above Fenwick, packed into an oiled herring-barrel with damp moss around the root-ball. It stood nine feet tall and had cost eleven marks of silver. The nurseryman had spat into the ditch and told Casian plainly that the tree would wither before the solstice, because a walnut moved at nine feet almost never survives the winter shock. Casian had handed over the eleven marks in good silver pennies and replied that he would take his chances with the frost.

Gareth dug the pit, grumbling with every spadeful of heavy clay, though his broad shoulders moved with the steady rhythm of a ditcher. Brennan carried the water up from the rain-butt in two wooden buckets, splashing his leather gaiters. Elna, twelve years old and wearing her old gardening smock, held the rough trunk upright while the first layers of loam were shovelled over the root-fibres, and then stubbornly refused to take her hands away for a quarter of an hour, asserting that a newly planted tree requires someone to stand beside it until the earth settles.

"Your arms will drop off before noon," Gareth told her, leaning on his spade.

"They have not dropped off yet," she retorted without looking at him.

"Elna," Casian said, raking the fine soil between the root-tines with his bare fingers.

"You brought this upon me yourself, Casian," Elna said, her chin lifted. "I was crouching behind a weir in a millrace in February, and now every living thing in this county is a matter I have to stand beside."

Maren came out from the kitchen at midday, carrying a loaf of dark barley bread, a wedge of hard ewe's-milk cheese, and a stone jug of small beer. She stood with her fists on her aproned hips, looking at the fresh earth, the grey masonry of the wall, and the four of them with black mud smeared across their knees.

"It will yield nothing but shade and caterpillar-wrack for ten years," she said.

"It is a grafted walnut, Mother," Casian answered, wiping his hands on a wisp of straw. "It will bear nuts."

"In ten years there will be a great leafy crown, and boys clambering over the copings to steal the green hulls, and you will be sitting on that bench looking solemn while I complain about the leaves in the gutter," Maren said. She turned back toward the low kitchen door, then paused on the threshold. "Casian. Your father requires you in the hall at two. It concerns the road-tally at Ashford. It will take an hour. And at three I expect you in the kitchen, and it will occupy the remainder of the day, and I want you present in body and mind, without turning ledgers over in your head."

"What occurs at three?" Casian asked.

"Your father reached forty-nine in July," Maren said, "and we were unable to mark the day because we were all sitting in an unthatched mill being fed by a baker's widow while six bailiffs watched the lane. I have resolved that we will observe it today. It will be a family dinner without excuses, and without anyone debating bailiwicks, charters, or ditch-tolls."

---

He went into the great hall at two. The business took an hour, and it was entirely dull; and that dullness was the quiet blessing he had desired above all things through the violent spring.

The parish highway at Ashford required stone and ditch-clearing before the Martinmas rains. The bailiwick had been assessed forty marks for the timber and cartage by a surveyor from Harrowmere who had recorded four hundred and ten loads of gravel where two hundred and sixty were sufficient. Baron Edric had detected the discrepancy not by martial suspicion, but because his bailiff had compared the survey against the carting registers of Corran and Son, which had recorded the gravel-runs along that parish border for nineteen consecutive years.

It was an ordinary comparison: eight folios of a surveyor's estimate set beside the grease-stained notebook of a licensed carter. The carrier's notebook was accurate to the furlong; the surveyor had simply paced the road in a March sleet-storm and guessed at the washouts.

"It is not a grievance," Edric said, turning his horn spectacles on the table. "Master Cole has surveyed for the Crown for thirty years, and I will not permit an honest surveyor to be disgraced over a wet afternoon's miscalculation. But the tenants cannot bear forty marks for a ghost-ditch. I want the number reduced quietly, and the surveyor informed without a public summons."

"Write to him privately," Casian suggested, sitting on the bench opposite his father. "Send the message with Watt's wagoner rather than a sheriff's clerk. Let the letter say what one honest gentleman would say to another in his own solar."

"My own thought," Edric agreed. "Take the quill and draft it. I will set my seal to it."

So Casian sat in the quiet hall of his ancestors on a bright autumn afternoon, the low sunlight slanting through the leaded panes, writing an unadorned, courteous letter about two hundred and sixty cartloads of gravel. It was the most untroubled hour he had known since the snow melted.

---

The dinner began at three, and it became the loudest gathering that the manor house had witnessed since the old lord's funeral.

Edric sat in the high chair, insisting repeatedly that forty-nine was an age of no consequence whatever, and then proceeded at half-past three to recount the full history of the Harrowmere drainage dispute of the year '09, embellishing each legal turn with fresh irony. Uncle Oswin, seventy-one years old and frail in his furred mantle, closed his eyes through the third recital of the bishop's injunction, but opened them promptly in time to correct the date of the harvest-frost.

Gareth argued vigorously that the east wall of the orchard had been pointed in the wrong sequence by the stonemasons. Brennan produced a small wooden cask from the back of his pony-cart, declining to name its origin; the contents proved remarkably sweet and fiery, and promised to produce severe headaches on the morrow. Nessa arrived late from the village, carrying her account-satchel under her arm; she was ordered by Maren to stow it under the bench, complied with an air of theatrical grievance, and then declared that she was eating under formal protest.

Emric, an infant of nearly two years, had been judged too small for the table, but had escaped the nurse and established himself securely between his mother and his eldest brother. He spent the meal drumming an alder spoon upon the oak boards until he collapsed in exhaustion against Gareth's broad sleeve. Gareth remained frozen for twenty minutes, scowling darkly at anyone who dared glance in his direction and whispering that he dared not stir lest the infant begin howling again.

Elna was permitted a small horn cup from Brennan's mysterious cask. She became exceedingly talkative for twelve minutes, fell into a profound silence for ten, and then demanded to know what every member of the family intended to achieve during the coming year, refusing to accept "the same" as an answer from anyone.

---

At seven in the evening, when the trenchers were cleared and the fire in the great hearth had died down to glowing logs, Elna sat on the hearth-bench, winding a skein of rough grey wool with clumsy fingers.

"What became of the nine?" she asked suddenly into the quiet room.

"The nine?" Edric echoed, looking up from his cider.

"The nine raiders at Bradey's holding," she said, her young face serious in the firelight. "The ones who were taken in February, whom Casian gave loaves of barley bread and their boots, and sent back across the border. It was the right action, and everyone praised it. But what happened to them afterward?"

The room fell still. The clatter of Maren's scouring-cloth in the kitchen ceased.

"Four of them are at work," Casian said, his voice quiet. "Two are clearing ditch-lines for Calla at Fenwick. Two are driving wagons for Corin Ahl. One returned to his brother's forge at Harrowmere. One took his wife and child over the western hills in May, and I have had no word of him."

"And the other three?" Elna pressed.

"Two are dead," Casian answered. "One was killed in a tavern brawl at Harrowmere in June. One died of the lung-rot in August. I learned of both from Watt at the inn, who keeps note of all who pass through the drove."

Elna paused, the wool slack between her thumbs.

"So they came here, and we fed them, and we spared their lives, and the world broke them anyway."

"Yes," Casian said.

"Except for the ones who remained with us."

"Except for those," said Casian. "And I have considered that many times. The plain truth is that I gave nine men a loaf of bread and an open road, and two of them were in their graves before harvest. The ones who lived are the ones who were given an honest task to do with their hands."

"That seems hard," she said softly.

"It is hard," Casian agreed.

"Then what was the purpose of sparing them?"

Casian looked at his sister, remembering the terrified child who had crouched beside the millrace in the freezing dark.

"The purpose was to ensure that they died of ordinary human misfortunes," he told her. "A tavern quarrel and an August fever are the common hazards of mortal flesh, and no lord can hold back sickness or folly. What I can do—what our house can do—is ensure that an honest peasant is not slaughtered for a baron's ambition, nor starved by an unlawful levy, nor dispossessed by a forged parchment. The rest belongs to God and their own feet."

Elna turned the wool around her fingers, her brow furrowed.

"That is a very good answer," she muttered, "and I do not like it at all."

"Keep it in your heart," Casian said gently, "and tell me what you think of it when you are eighteen."

Gareth shifted the sleeping child upon his shoulder. "She will be eighteen in six years," the soldier remarked dryly, "and she will be managing this entire valley, while you are sat by the hearth winding her yarn."

Elna hurled the ball of wool straight at his forehead; Gareth caught it cleanly with his left hand without jostling the infant, while Edric chuckled until he wiped his eyes. Maren came in from the scullery, scolding them all for their foolishness, and the room settled back into the warm, unhurried ease of an ordinary home.

---

At eight o'clock, Casian slipped quietly through the side door and walked into the kitchen garden.

The night was crisp and clear, the October stars sharp as salt across the black sky. In the far corner, the nine-foot walnut tree stood slender against the grey stone of the wall, its roots banked with fresh turf and sheltered from the north wind by a hurdle of hazel-wattle.

He walked past the dormant herb-beds to the low timber gate that opened toward the river-path.

The gate was shut.

The heavy oak bar, which had rested on iron hooks beside the jamb since the thawing rains of March, had been laid firmly across the leaves. The iron drop-latch was seated deep in its keeper.

Casian placed his hand upon the rough wood. For seven months, through cattle-raids, audits, desertions, and commissions, that gate had stood open to the valley—a silent pledge that the fourth son of the Vale held no secrets and manned no ramparts. Now, someone had walked out into the dark and barred it against the autumn night.

He turned back toward the manor. In the scullery, a single horn lantern burned upon the scrubbed table. Maren sat alone on the bench, a needle in her hand and an earthenware bowl of broth kept warm above a pot of embers.

"Sit and eat that," she said without raising her eyes from her hem. "You had two sips of Brennan's brew and half a crust of bread, and your stomach will wake you at the fourth hour."

Casian took the wooden spoon and sat opposite her.

"Elna asked me what happens next year," he said. "I gave her a list of dates."

"You will give her an answer in the spring, when the grass grows," Maren said. She snipped a thread with her teeth and looked across the table at him. "Casian. The tree in the angle of the wall."

"Yes, Mother?"

"It has taken," she said.

He paused, the spoon suspended over the bowl. "The nurseryman said it would not survive the transplanting."

"The nurseryman is a fool who sells saplings by the cord," Maren said calmly. "I went out before dusk to bank the turf. There are three buds on the upper scion. They are green, firm, and the size of my thumbnail. It is the end of October, and a walnut tree does not put forth buds in the frost."

Casian looked down into his broth. "That shouldn't happen."

"I have tended gardens in this valley since thirty years before you were born," his mother said, her voice dropping to a low, steady register. "I know precisely when an ash buds, when an apple ripens, and when a walnut sleeps. There are three green buds on that wood, and I want you to go out tomorrow morning and inspect them, and then I want you to come back into this house and say nothing about it to your father. He has carried the burden of Thornwall and the Crown for nine months, and he deserves to believe that a tree is merely a tree."

She folded her linen and rose from the bench.

"And Casian," she added from the doorway, "I closed the river-gate myself. The summer is finished, lad. Winter is coming, and it is time this house had a latch on its door."

---

Casian finished the broth in silence, extinguished the kitchen dip, and walked back out into the cold darkness of the garden.

The frost was beginning to whiten the grass along the gravel path. He walked to the corner wall and reached up into the darkness, his fingertips brushing the rough, dormant bark of the walnut.

There, just beneath the highest graft, three small swellings rose against the wood: cool, plump, and undeniably alive.

*Three buds,* the thought formed beside him.

Casian did not turn. The presence was there as it had always been—not a spirit of flame, nor an ancient shadow, but the quiet companion of his exile, standing near the coping with the familiar indifference of an old friend.

"Three buds," Casian said aloud. "In the frost."

*A sign of vitality,* Argius murmured in the silence of his mind. *Or a sign of an unnatural spring.*

"I did not ask for it," Casian said. "I planted a tree to replace the timber we cut for the leat."

*You cannot touch the earth in this valley without waking what sleeps beneath it,* Argius said. *I have watched you for seven months, Casian. You have been extraordinarily clever. You have built four things that will endure long after your name is forgotten: a millrace that belongs to a village; a carrying-road governed by a public ledger; a surgery with two trained women; and a roll of forty-one souls who hold the peace of this border.*

Casian looked at the closed timber gate. "And the cost?"

*The cost is that you are no longer an unnoticed fourth son,* said Argius. *You have made yourself indispensable to an entire shire. You believed you could erect a boundary around your garden and remain harmless within it. But a garden that bears fruit in winter cannot hide behind a stone wall.*

"What happens now?" Casian asked.

*The year turns,* Argius whispered, the presence fading like mist into the starlight. *The cold deepens. And in the spring, the realm will come seeking the gardener.*

---

At mid-morning on the nineteenth of October, a courier in royal livery rode up the lane from Ashgrove, his horse's flanks steaming in the crisp autumn air.

He carried two packets from Harven. The first was an official acknowledgment from the Royal Chancery addressed to Baron Edric, confirming the reduction of the Ashford highway assessment from forty marks to twenty-six, and commending the barony's precision. Edric carried the folio into the hall with a broad smile, calling for Oswin to read the Chancellor's complimentary subscript.

The second packet was a small, oblong parchment tied with green silk and sealed with a plain signet of three ermine bars. It was directed to *Master Casian Vale, at the Manor.*

Casian broke the seal beside the rain-butt in the yard. Inside lay four lines of fine, upright script:

*Master Vale.*

*The Royal Marshal has delivered his account of the eastern marches to the Duke's council. It is an honorable report, and it omits everything that matters. In the fourth month of the new year, I am commissioned to return to your valley with two clerks and an independent warrant from the Court of Rolls.*

*I send this warning so that your ledgers may be in order, and so that you will know I told you before the heralds arrive.*

*— Seraine Varyn.*

Casian folded the parchment, smoothed the crease between his fingers, and tucked it into the inner breast of his woollen coat.

Elna came across the yard, carrying a wicker basket of winter pears from the store-house.

"Was that a dispatch from Harven?" she asked, eyeing the green silk thread on the stones.

"It was," Casian said.

"Is the Crown coming to trouble us?"

Casian looked past her, toward the walled garden where the young walnut tree stood against the grey stones, its three buds hidden from the wind, and toward the gate that now stood firmly latched against the road.

A faint, quiet smile touched the corners of his mouth—the genuine, unhurried pleasure of a man who saw the chess-board widening, and welcomed the challenge.

"Not to trouble us, Elna," Casian said softly, taking a pear from the basket. "They are coming to see what we have planted."
